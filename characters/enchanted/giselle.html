Giselle

An Andalasia maiden of the purest Disney specification — animal-whisperer, soprano, dreamer of true love's kiss — exiled to a world whose physics refute her at every step, and who responds not by hardening but by editing: the fairy tale, revised by experience, in her own hand.

01 / CHARACTER FILE

Overview

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Giselle is built from the canon's own parts — Snow White's animals, Cinderella's industry, Aurora's song, Ariel's curiosity — and then dropped, in a wedding dress, into Times Square. The film's gamble is that she will not curdle: that the princess specification, exposed to a world of divorce law and weather, will adapt rather than shatter. She does — gloriously and specifically. She learns anger and uses it; learns that a date precedes a wedding; learns that true love's kiss is a test her fairy-tale fiancé fails and a cynical lawyer passes. At the climax she picks up a sword and climbs after a dragon herself. The princess survives New York by becoming its citizen without surrendering her citizenship in Andalasia: the film's heroine is the edit, not the exile.

ROLE

The exile

Protagonist; Andalasia maiden, Edward's bride-to-be, Narissa's target, Robert's guest and eventual wife.

DEFINING TRAIT

Adaptation without surrender

Giselle revises every fairy-tale rule she was issued — engagement length, anger, who rescues whom — while keeping the rule underneath them all: love is real and worth organizing a life around.

FATE

A new happily-ever-after

Poisoned at the ball, revived by Robert's kiss — true love's, where Edward's failed — she defeats the dragon, stays in New York, marries Robert and builds a life (and a fashion house) in the real world.

Place in the story

Giselle is the film's experiment and its verdict: the Disney princess treated not as a relic or a joke but as a hypothesis — and the hypothesis holds. Adams' performance, switching between animation-referenced sweetness and live-action discovery, is the film's engine and its most honoured element.

03 / BIOGRAPHY

Biography: Their Story

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL FULL CHARACTER STORY

In Andalasia, Giselle lives in the forest among talking animals, dreaming in song of true love's kiss. Prince Edward hears her singing, rescues her from a troll freed by Nathaniel to kill her, and the two are engaged within the day — married tomorrow, per specification.

Queen Narissa, whose reign ends if Edward marries, intercepts Giselle on her wedding morning disguised as an old hag and pushes her into a well. Giselle surfaces — transformed into a live-action woman — in Times Square, in her wedding dress, frightened and overwhelmed by a world with no talking animals and no happily-ever-after guarantee.

Divorce lawyer Robert Philip and his six-year-old daughter Morgan find her; at Morgan's insistence, Robert reluctantly shelters her. Giselle applies Andalasia's methods to Manhattan — animals summoned to clean the apartment (the city's fauna answers: rats, pigeons, roaches), songs in Central Park — and begins, scandalously, to ask questions: about Robert's five-year courtship of Nancy, about why a date should not precede a wedding, about her own anger, which arrives as a new and fascinating emotion.

Edward follows her through the well; Nathaniel follows with Narissa's three poisoned apples, failing twice to feed her one. When Edward finds her, Giselle — to his bewilderment — proposes a date first, and a final night at the King and Queen's Ball before returning to Andalasia.

At the ball she dances with Robert, and the fairy tale's machinery completes its audit. Narissa attends in person, disguised, and offers Giselle an apple that will erase her New York memories. Giselle bites — and falls into the death-sleep. Edward's kiss fails. Robert's succeeds: true love's kiss, certified by elimination. Narissa becomes a dragon; Giselle, revived, takes up a sword and follows her up the Woolworth Building, and the queen falls to her death. Giselle stays — marries Robert, mothers Morgan, and opens Andalasia Fashions, exporting the fairy tale as a business rather than a biography.

The biography above is an editorial paraphrase of the documented plot accounts (Wikipedia and the cited sources), not a scene-by-scene transcript.

04 / PERSONALITY & PSYCHOLOGY

Personality & Psychology

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

This section is editorial interpretation, grounded in the character's on-screen actions.

Sincerity as method

Giselle's optimism is not ignorance; it is a discipline she maintains under evidence. Interpretation: the film's respect for her is its thesis — she is the only character whose worldview survives contact with both worlds, because hers is the only one that updates.

The education of anger

Her discovery of anger — and of doubt, delay and desire — is played as growth, not corruption. The princess does not fall from innocence; she graduates from it.

Industry

She sews, cleans, organizes and eventually founds a company: the Cinderella inheritance, converted from servitude into enterprise.

08 / SYMBOLISM & THEMES

Symbolism & Themes

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

This section is editorial interpretation.

The wedding dress

She spends her exile in it — increasingly battered, never removed until she chooses — the specification worn as both armour and evidence.

The well

The canon's own device (wishing wells, rabbit holes) used as an exile's chute: the fairy tale's plumbing, weaponized by its queen.

The apple

Snow White's prop, offered with a modern pitch — forgetfulness as mercy — and bitten by a woman who chooses, for one moment, not to remember. The film's darkest transaction is a memory for a wedding.

09 / ANALYSIS & INVESTIGATION

Analysis & Investigation

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

This section is editorial analysis. Documented facts are cited to the sources in Section 13; readings of the character's function are the archive's own.

Homage as critique

The record describes the film as both homage to and self-parody of Disney's animated features. Analysis: Giselle is where the two modes fuse — every parody beat (the cleaning animals, the instant engagement) is also a sincere test of whether the underlying virtue (kindness, industry, hope) functions outside the cartoon. The film's answer, delivered by Adams' performance, is that the virtues were never the cartoonish part.

The kiss as audit

True love's kiss is the canon's oldest certification, and the film runs it as an experiment with a control: the designated prince kisses first, and nothing happens. The device does not mock the ritual; it re-certifies it — the kiss works, on the man who earned it in the real world's currency: time, doubt, and a dance.

11 / BEHIND THE CHARACTER

Behind the Character

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Creation

Screenwriter Bill Kelly and director Kevin Lima created Giselle as an archetypal Disney princess-to-be; animator James Baxter supervised her animated sequences from Adams' live-action footage to keep the two Giselles one person.

Performance

Adams' turn was the film's most honoured element, earning major award nominations and establishing the film's reputation; the character record notes the portrayal as both parody and homage carried by a single performance.

12 / LEGACY & SIGNIFICANCE

Legacy & Significance

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Giselle became the Disney canon's self-portrait: the princess who audited the princesses. She returned in Disenchanted (2022) — and the original film's reputation rests on her: the proof that the fairy tale's heroine could survive the real world by the radical method of learning from it.
