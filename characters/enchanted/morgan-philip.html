01 / CHARACTER FILE

Overview

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Every revolution in the film passes through a six-year-old's vote. Morgan is the one who insists the woman in the wedding dress comes home; Morgan is the one who believes, instantly and correctly, that Giselle is a princess; Morgan's delight is the referendum that ratifies each stage of her father's thaw. She is not a plot device in a party dress — she is the film's argument about what protective realism costs: Robert withheld the fairy tales to armour his daughter against disappointment, and the armour's price was a childhood running on empty where wonder should be. Giselle's arrival is Morgan's vindication, and the ending — a stepmother acquired, a fairy tale installed as family — is the swing voter's platform, enacted in full.

ROLE

The advocate

Robert's daughter; Giselle's sponsor, believer and eventual stepdaughter.

DEFINING TRAIT

Instant, accurate belief

Morgan identifies Giselle correctly on sight — the only character whose first assessment needs no revision. The child is the film's most reliable narrator of people.

FATE

The platform, enacted

She ends the film with a stepmother, a re-enchanted father, and a childhood re-stocked with the stories it was owed.

Place in the story

Morgan is the film's electorate of one: no major turn — the shelter, the stay, the marriage — happens without her vote, and the plot's respect for her judgment is its thesis about children and stories in miniature.

03 / BIOGRAPHY

Biography: Their Story

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL FULL CHARACTER STORY

Morgan is introduced inside her father's protective system: a motherless six-year-old raised on realism — no fairy tales, because the world is what it is and stories would only set her up for the fall.

In Times Square she meets the refutation in a wedding dress. Her identification is instant — a princess — and her advocacy is absolute: Giselle comes home with them, over her father's professional objections. The swing voter has voted.

Through the week she is Giselle's sponsor inside the family: delighted by the cleaning animals and the songs, she provides the household's referendum at every stage, and her starvation for story — visible in every scene — is the quiet audit of her father's parenting doctrine.

At the ball she watches the machinery complete itself — the dance, the apple, the kiss — and the film closes on her platform enacted: Giselle installed as stepmother, the fairy tale naturalized as family, and Morgan's childhood re-stocked at last.

Morgan's belief is not naivety; it is pattern recognition unclouded by the adult scripts. Interpretation: the film positions her as its epistemological standard — she is wrong about nothing, all film, at six.

09 / ANALYSIS & INVESTIGATION

Analysis & Investigation

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

This section is editorial analysis. Documented facts are cited to the sources in Section 13; readings of the character's function are the archive's own.

The child as standard

Enchanted's boldest structural choice is making its six-year-old its most reliable judge of character. Analysis: every adult in the film misreads someone on first meeting — Robert misreads Giselle, Edward misreads New York, Nathaniel misreads his queen — except Morgan, whose instant verdicts are all correct. The film's epistemology is a child's: look, believe your eyes, vote accordingly.
