01 / CHARACTER FILE

Overview

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Leonidas is introduced the way Sparta introduces everyone: as a child inspected for defects, raised by the agoge, sent into the wild to kill a wolf and come back or not. The film's plot is his single decision — to take 300 men to Thermopylae in defiance of a purchased oracle — and everything in it is that decision's working-out: the emissary in the pit, the "stroll" north that circumvents the law, the shield wall at the Hot Gates, the refusal of Ephialtes, the dismissal of Dilios, and the final feigned kneeling that lets him throw one spear at a god's face. He dies under a sky of arrows having achieved exactly what he went for: not victory, but proof.

ROLE

The king at the gate

Protagonist; commander of the 300 and author of the stand.

DEFINING TRAIT

Law used against itself

Leonidas obeys every Spartan form while defeating every Spartan obstruction — the stroll, the bodyguard fiction, the consultation performed and then priced correctly as bought.

FATE

Killed at Thermopylae

He dies in the final arrow storm after his spear grazes Xerxes's cheek — the film's thesis, delivered by his hand.

Place in the story

Leonidas is the film's fixed point: the narration worships him, the queen out-argues councils for him, the god-king offers him the world, and the traitor's whole tragedy is not being allowed to die beside him.

03 / BIOGRAPHY

Biography: Their Story

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL FULL CHARACTER STORY

Dilios's narration raises him: inspected at birth and passed, taken at seven into the agoge, taught to show no pain and no mercy, sent at fifteen into the wilderness where he kills a giant wolf by letting it charge onto his spear — and returns to be crowned. As king he receives Xerxes's emissary, who demands earth and water in token of submission; Leonidas, noting the wall of skulls Sparta keeps for previous envoys, kicks the man down the pit.

The Ephors, bribed with Persian gold, produce an oracle forbidding war during the Carneia. Leonidas obeys the letter and defeats the fact: he marches north with 300 picked men as a personal bodyguard — a stroll, not an army. Gorgo's farewell counsel, as the narration gives it, is the Spartan wife's formula: return with your shield, or on it.

At Thermopylae the Spartans and their Greek allies break the Persian assaults in sequence — the infantry driven off the cliffs, the arrow storm endured ("we will fight in the shade"), the Immortals repelled, the beasts and giants killed. Xerxes offers Leonidas wealth and command of Greece as his warlord; Leonidas refuses. Ephialtes, refused a place in the phalanx because he cannot raise his shield to the line, sells Xerxes the goat path around the pass.

With the position turned, Leonidas dismisses the allies and sends the wounded Dilios home — over Dilios's anguish — because the story is now the weapon Sparta needs. On the last morning he feigns submission, kneels before Xerxes, and at Stelios's leap rises to hurl his spear: it grazes the god-king's cheek and draws blood. The 300 die under the arrows. A year later Dilios finishes the tale before the army at Plataea.

The biography above is an editorial paraphrase of the documented plot accounts (Wikipedia and the cited sources), not a scene-by-scene transcript.

04 / PERSONALITY & PSYCHOLOGY

Personality & Psychology

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

This section is editorial interpretation, grounded in the character's on-screen actions.

Formidable, formally

Leonidas's rebellion is conducted entirely inside Spartan law's forms — he is the system's product turning the system's tools on its priests.

A commander's economy

He spends men like a man who has priced them: the 300 are not wasted but invested, and Dilios — the most valuable survivor — is withdrawn from the position at exactly the right moment.

Contempt for gods, Persian and purchased

He prices the Ephors' oracle as bought and Xerxes's divinity as marketing; the spear at the cheek is an audit, not an assassination attempt.

Wolf-killer at fifteen; the phalanx's anchor and the final stand's spear.

COMMAND

The Hot Gates held

Terrain chosen, rotations run, allies integrated, assaults of four kinds broken in sequence.

RHETORIC

The pit and the parley

His diplomacy is conducted at spear-point and his refusals are the film's most quoted lines.

LIMIT

Bound by the forms he uses

He cannot simply override the oracle or the festival; his whole campaign is a loophole, and loopholes have widths — 300 men wide.

08 / SYMBOLISM & THEMES

Symbolism & Themes

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

This section is editorial interpretation.

The spear at the cheek

The film's thesis in one wound: the god-king bleeds. Leonidas does not throw to kill but to demonstrate, and the demonstration is what Plataea is fought on.

The shield formula

With it, or on it: Gorgo's farewell prices the whole expedition in advance, and the film honours the invoice exactly.

09 / ANALYSIS & INVESTIGATION

Analysis & Investigation

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

This section is editorial analysis. Documented facts are cited to the sources in Section 13; readings of the character's function are the archive's own.

The narrator's king

Everything recorded here arrives through Dilios, who announces his own embellishment as a soldier's duty; the archive therefore files Leonidas as the film presents him — a story told to make 40,000 men charge — which is the character's actual historical function in the film's design.

The Ephialtes decision

Leonidas's refusal is mechanically correct — a shield that cannot reach the line breaks the phalanx — and strategically fatal. The film does not soften either fact, and his page is where the archive files the tragedy of a system whose quality control is also its doom.

11 / BEHIND THE CHARACTER

Behind the Character

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Butler's breakthrough

300 made Gerard Butler a star; the performance — and the physical regime behind it, much publicised at the time — became the template for the graphic-novel epic's leading man.

The narration's frame

The film is adapted from Frank Miller and Lynn Varley's 1998 limited series, and preserves the comic's device of the tale told by a survivor — with Dilios's unreliability written into the film's own account of itself.

12 / LEGACY & SIGNIFICANCE

Legacy & Significance

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Leonidas as Butler plays him became the popular culture's default Spartan — the pit-kick and the spear-throw entering the language — and the film's defence has always been the one Dilios offers: it is a story told by a soldier, and it works on soldiers.
