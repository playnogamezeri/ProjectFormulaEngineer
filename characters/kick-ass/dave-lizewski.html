01 / CHARACTER FILE

Overview

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Dave is the film's controlled experiment: take the fan's question — why doesn't anyone ever try this in real life? — and run it on an unremarkable teenager with nothing but comic-book conviction. The experiment's results are the film: stabbed and run over on his first outing, rebuilt with metal plates and damaged nerves that let him endure what would stop anyone else, famous from a parking-lot video, framed for another vigilante's war, tortured on a live stream, and finally airborne on a jetpack to fire the bazooka that ends a crime empire. His narration frames it all in the fan's own grammar — this is a boy reviewing his life as if it were a comic — and his arc is the genre's founding myth restaged at street level: an ordinary person, a costume, and the discovery that the only superpower the real world issues is refusal to stay down.

ROLE

The protagonist; the experiment

The nobody who tries it: his ordinariness is the film's thesis and his endurance its only miracle.

DEFINING TRAIT

Refusal as a superpower

He cannot fight, plan or read a room — the Galaxy's film page's verdict — and wins his place in the story purely by returning, every time, to a fight that has already beaten him.

FATE

Retired, and answered

He gives up the costume with the war won, takes up an ordinary life with Katie, and watches his question answered at city scale by the copycat wave his example started.

Place in the story

Dave is the Galaxy's street-level founding hero: the archive treats his page as the record of the superhero idea tested on an unpowered body — what it costs, what it accidentally builds, and why the city answers it anyway.

03 / BIOGRAPHY

Biography: Their Story

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL FULL CHARACTER STORY

Dave's documented course begins in invisibility: an awkward Staten Island teenager, comic-shop regular, invisible to his crush Katie Deauxma, who asks his friends the question the film is built on — with all the crime in the world, why does nobody ever just try to be a superhero? He answers it himself: a wetsuit bought online, batons, a name, and a first patrol that ends with him stabbed by muggers and hit by a car fleeing the scene. He strips off the costume before the paramedics arrive and swears them to secrecy; the hospital rebuilds him with metal plates, and the nerve damage leaves him with a freakish tolerance for pain. At school, the naked-rescue story mutates into a rumour that he is gay — which, in the film's cruellest joke, finally makes Katie notice him: she adopts him as her safe gay friend, a confidence Dave shamefully maintains to stay close to her.

His second career goes better: he intervenes in a gang beating, takes a savage beating in return, refuses to fall, and the bystanders' video makes Kick-Ass famous overnight. Katie's plea sends him against the drug dealer Rasul, who has been harassing her — a rescue that becomes his first genuine near-death until Hit-Girl comes through the door and kills the room. Now Dave is inside a war he did not start: Big Daddy and Hit-Girl have been dismantling Frank D'Amico's empire, and D'Amico bills the carnage to the famous name — Kick-Ass. A new hero, Red Mist, befriends him; the friendship is Chris D'Amico's trap, and though Dave quits — confessing everything to Katie, who forgives him and becomes his girlfriend — one last favour for Red Mist tricks him into leading D'Amico's men to the Macreadys' safe house. Captured, he is beaten on a live internet broadcast beside a burning Big Daddy, and saved by the eleven-year-old he helped doom. In the final assault on the penthouse he fights Red Mist to a standstill and, when D'Amico is about to execute Hit-Girl, arrives on a jetpack and puts the crime lord out of the window with a bazooka. He retires: the costume off, Katie his, Mindy enrolled at his school — and a city filling with imitators behind him.

The biography above is an editorial paraphrase of the documented plot accounts (Wikipedia and the cited sources), not a scene-by-scene transcript.

04 / PERSONALITY & PSYCHOLOGY

Personality & Psychology

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

This section is editorial interpretation, grounded in the character's on-screen actions.

The fan as founder

Interpretation: Dave's heroism is a reader's — he approaches reality with a genre's expectations and is educated by impact. What survives the education is the part the genre got right: somebody should stand up. His narration never loses the fan's voice, which is the film's argument that the voice was never the naive part.

Shame, honestly carried

The gay-rumour deception is his one sustained dishonesty, maintained for access to Katie — and the film makes him confess it at real cost before it lets him be a hero again. His arc prices integrity above safety twice: the confession, and the final return to a fight he had honourably quit.

Endurance as identity

He is beaten in nearly every appearance and defined by the getting up: the parking-lot video that makes him famous is not a victory but a refusal, filmed. The archive reads his fame as the city recognising the only credential it trusts.

05 / ABILITIES & SKILLS

Abilities, Skills & Weaknesses

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
PAIN TOLERANCE

The accidental power

Metal plates and damaged nerves: he absorbs beatings that would hospitalise or kill an ordinary teenager and remains functional through them — documented in the parking-lot fight and the broadcast torture alike.

THE NAME

Fame as a weapon and a target

Kick-Ass becomes a symbol the city rallies to and the mob bills its losses to: his celebrity is simultaneously his only real influence and the reason a war he did not start is fought over his address book.

IMPROVISATION

The jetpack

His one piece of genuine equipment arrives at the climax: a jetpack with mounted guns, bought with the Macreadys' stolen mob money — the fan's toy budget finally spent at the decisive second.

WEAKNESS

No training, no plan

Every professional in the film outclasses him — Hit-Girl rescues him twice; Big Daddy uses him as a flag; Red Mist plays him like a level. His survival is a team statistic, not a personal one.

08 / SYMBOLISM & THEMES

Symbolism & Themes

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

This section is editorial interpretation.

The wetsuit

A superhero costume bought online and modified at a bedroom desk: the symbol of the genre democratised — no cave, no fortune, no radioactive anything; a delivery, a mirror, and nerve.

The parking-lot video

His founding document is not a victory but a refusal, filmed by strangers: the archive reads it as the film's definition of the heroic in the phone-camera age — not winning, but being seen not to fall.

The metal plates

His body is literally rebuilt by his first failure into the only version of him that can do the job: the origin story's oldest shape — the wound as the gift — restaged with surgical hardware.

09 / ANALYSIS & INVESTIGATION

Analysis & Investigation

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

This section is editorial analysis. Documented facts are cited to the sources in Section 13; readings of the character's function are the archive's own.

The experiment and its results

The archive's reading: the film runs Dave's question honestly, which is why it remains the genre's sharpest self-examination. The results are genuinely mixed — a man saved in a parking lot, a girl's war given cover and then nearly ended by him, a crime lord bazookaed out a window, a torture broadcast, a city of imitators whose outcomes the film declines to guarantee. Dave's narration sells it as a triumph; the footage keeps filing minority reports, and the tension between the two is the film.

Fame as the real superpower

Nothing Dave does with his fists changes anything structural; everything that changes — the mob's strategy, the city's imagination, Mindy's schooling — changes because he is famous. The analysis: the film is a media story wearing a superhero story's wetsuit, and Dave is its case study in celebrity as the one force an ordinary person can actually acquire by accident.

11 / BEHIND THE CHARACTER

Behind the Character

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Johnson's Dave

Aaron Johnson plays Dave and narrates the film; he was credited as Aaron Johnson at release and is now known as Aaron Taylor-Johnson, the billing the Galaxy's film page uses. The character originates in Mark Millar and John Romita Jr.'s comic, published concurrently with the film's production — a documented peculiarity of the property: Dave existed in print and on screen almost simultaneously, each version racing the other.

Vaughn and Goldman

The film is directed by Matthew Vaughn from a screenplay by Jane Goldman and Vaughn, per the film's record; the Dave material is its spine — the narration, the experiment and the retirement are all his.
