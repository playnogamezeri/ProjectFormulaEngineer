01 / CHARACTER FILE

Overview

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Raka is the orangutan mentor of Kingdom of the Planet of the Apes (2024), played by Peter Macon — a hermit scholar living among the ruins of the old human world, and, by his own account, the last living member of the Order of Caesar: the ape fellowship that preserved the original Caesar's teachings of compassion, unity and coexistence across three centuries. Where Proximus Caesar preaches a Caesar of conquest, Raka keeps the other gospel — "apes together strong" as a doctrine of mutual care rather than massed power. Raka matters out of all proportion to his screen time because he is the film's transmission line: everything true that Noa learns about history, he learns from Raka, and the necklace Raka gives him becomes the physical form of the inheritance the whole story contests. Critics singled the character out as the film's best — its warmest presence and its most painful loss.

ROLE

Mentor & keeper of the true teaching

The Order of Caesar's last member: Noa's history teacher, Mae's first ape benefactor, and the standard against which Proximus's gospel is measured.

DEFINING TRAIT

Compassion practiced, not preached

Raka's doctrine is visible in his hands before his words: food and a blanket for a feral human, and finally his own safety, spent on her life at the river.

FATE

Swept away — unconfirmed

He saves Mae from drowning at the bridge ambush and is carried off by the rapids. The film never confirms his death, and he is not seen again.

Place in the story

Raka is the reboot continuity's living link to Caesar — not by blood or by the previous trilogy's cast, but by institution: an Order that carried the teachings the way monasteries carried texts, shrinking across the centuries until it fits inside one orangutan in a ruin. Screenwriter Josh Friedman positioned the character in the line of Maurice, the Caesar trilogy's gentle orangutan conscience; Raka is that conscience three hundred years on, nearly extinct, still teaching.

Raka's identity is institutional before it is personal: he introduces himself through his Order, his books and his prophet, and the film gives him almost no biography outside the fellowship he survives. That is the character's point. Raka is what a teaching looks like when everyone who held it is gone — one ape, a ruin full of books, and a necklace whose symbol he can pass on but whose origin even he has lost.

03 / BIOGRAPHY

Biography: Their Story

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL FULL CHARACTER STORY

Of Raka's early life the film says nothing, and this file invents nothing: he is found, not introduced — an elderly orangutan living as a hermit among the ruins of the old human world, in a place of books and memory, keeping the rites of the Order of Caesar. He was not always alone. The Order dwindled across the generations as Proximus's power rose and hunted its preachers; Raka's own companion — the other member of his fellowship — was killed by the masked raiders, leaving him, as he believes, the last of the Order on earth.

Noa finds him while tracking his abducted clan, and the meeting rearranges Noa's world. Raka tells him of Caesar — of whom the Eagle Clan knows nothing — and of the teachings: that apes are stronger together, that Caesar valued humans as individuals, that the old world of the ruins was built by creatures who wrote their speech in books, one of which Raka shows him, explaining how humans once communicated through symbols on paper. He gives Noa the Order's necklace, a pendant in the shape of a window-like star — though even Raka admits the origin of the symbol has been lost to history. (The audience, not the apes, can recognize it: the window of Caesar's childhood home.)

When the human scavenger begins following them, Raka practices the teaching before he explains it: he offers the frightened creature food and a blanket, and names her Nova, explaining that his kind call them all Nova — he doesn't know why. He urges Noa toward the same compassion, over Noa's suspicion. At the river crossing on the road to Proximus's settlement, Sylva's raiders ambush the party on the bridge. Mae goes into the rapids; Raka goes in after her, and holds her to safety — and the current takes him instead. He is swept away downstream, out of the story, with no body, no farewell and no confirmation: the film simply continues without him, the way the world continues without the Order.

His presence persists in objects and in conduct: the necklace on Noa's chest through the vault and the climax, and Noa's own choices — the trust extended, the revenge declined — made by a student applying lessons whose teacher never saw them applied.

The biography above is an editorial paraphrase of the documented plot accounts (Wikipedia and the cited sources), not a scene-by-scene transcript.

04 / PERSONALITY & PSYCHOLOGY

Personality & Psychology

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

This section is editorial interpretation, grounded in the character's on-screen actions.

The cheerful extinction

Raka's most striking psychological quality is his lightness in the face of his own order's annihilation. He is the last of a fellowship, hunted by a rising tyranny, living in a ruin — and he is funny: wry, warm, self-deprecating, delighted by a young ape's ignorance and a human's blanket-wrapped suspicion. The interpretation the archive offers is that the humor is doctrinal, not incidental: an Order whose teaching is compassion cannot be carried by a bitter man, and Raka's gentleness is the teaching's proof of concept, maintained deliberately at the end of the world.

Faith without evidence

Raka believes in coexistence with a species that, in his entire lifetime, has given him no evidence for it: the humans he has met are feral, and the speaking one he shelters is secretly an agent. His faith is therefore exactly that — faith, inherited and chosen daily. The film positions this as his nobility and his vulnerability in the same trait: the blanket and the river are the same decision, made twice.

The teacher's economy

Raka gives everything away: food, shelter, history, the necklace, finally his safety. Nothing he owns is withheld, including the Order's last relic, pressed on a stranger of another clan within days of meeting him. Read psychologically, it is the behavior of a man who knows the alternative — an Order that dies with its treasures unshared. His generosity is a distribution strategy for a legacy that cannot survive storage.

05 / ABILITIES & SKILLS

Abilities, Skills & Weaknesses

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
SKILL

Keeper of history

The film's only scholar: Raka preserves books, can explain what writing is and what the old world's ruins were, and carries three centuries of transmitted teaching accurately enough to contradict a king's gospel to his face-value.

SKILL

Teaching

Converts an ignorant, grieving young ape into a student within days — by demonstration first (the blanket) and doctrine second. His pedagogy is the film's model of how the true Caesar survived: person to person.

SKILL

Survival & strength

Lives alone in raider country into old age, travels the road to Proximus's kingdom on foot, and in the river shows an orangutan's full strength — enough to hold a drowning human against the current, at the price of his own footing.

WEAKNESS

No protection but obscurity

The Order has no army, no walls and, by the film's present, no second member. Raka's survival strategy is insignificance — which fails the moment he travels with the two people Proximus most wants.

WEAKNESS

Trust without verification

His compassion is unconditional by design, and therefore exploitable: he shelters an agent, vouches for her to Noa, and dies — if he dies — for a woman whose mission he never learns. The film honors the trait and prices it in the same scene.

07 / CHARACTER ARC

Character Arc

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
Beginning state — the last keeper

Alone in a ruin of books, companion dead, Order extinct but for himself, keeping a teaching the world has stopped wanting. His arc begins at the end of his institution's.

The student arrives

Noa's appearance converts custodianship into transmission: for the first time in the film's present, the teaching has somewhere to go. Raka's decision to teach — and to travel — is his arc's one active choice, and he makes it immediately.

The doctrine practiced

The blanket, the name Nova, the defense of Mae against Noa's suspicion: the middle of Raka's arc is a demonstration, lesson by lesson, that his gospel is a behavior and not a text.

The river

His arc closes — or breaks — at the bridge: the doctrine's final demonstration, performed at the price of the demonstrator. The film withholds whether it is an ending or an interruption.

End state — transmitted

Absent in body, present in everything Noa does afterward: the necklace at the climax, the restraint in victory. By the measure Raka himself would use, his arc completes in his student, whatever the river did with him.

08 / SYMBOLISM & THEMES

Symbolism & Themes

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

This section is editorial interpretation.

The necklace — the window

The Order's pendant is the film's densest symbol: a shape its keepers no longer understand, which the audience recognizes as the window of Caesar's childhood home — the frame through which the first Caesar looked out at the human world that raised him. Raka carries the symbol's form without its origin, which is exactly how the film says traditions survive: the shape outlasts the story, and a later generation — Noa at the telescope, looking outward through a frame of his own — may refill it.

The books — the other vault

Raka's ruin of books is the film's quiet counterweight to Proximus's vault: two archives of the old world, one kept by a hermit who shares it freely and one besieged by a king who would hoard it. Knowledge in Raka's keeping produces a teacher; in the vault it produces an arms race. The film's argument about inheritance is staged as a choice between libraries.

The river — faith's price

That the gentlest character is removed by water while saving the most duplicitous one is the film's refusal to let doctrine be cheap. Raka's river is not a punishment or a reward; it is the cost of the teaching, presented without commentary — and the unresolved fate keeps the cost from hardening into a martyr's monument, which the character himself would have been the first to refuse.

09 / ANALYSIS & INVESTIGATION

Analysis & Investigation

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

This section is editorial analysis. Documented facts are cited to the sources in Section 13; readings of the character's function are the archive's own.

Narrative function: the exposition with a heartbeat

Structurally, Raka is the film's history lesson — three centuries of backstory delivered by one character in one location. The investigation the archive records is how completely the film disguises the mechanism: the exposition arrives as companionship, humor and a blanket, and the character was received by critics not as a device but as the film's soul — widely called its best character and its show-stealing performance. The lesson for the archive's later files is the standard Raka sets: information carried by affection is the franchise's preferred technology.

The Maurice succession

Screenwriter Josh Friedman explicitly built Raka into the line of Maurice, the orangutan who was Caesar's conscience in the previous trilogy — the peacemaker at the prophet's side. The succession matters analytically because it shows the franchise's memory working at two levels: within the story, the Order preserves Caesar; within the craft, the filmmakers preserve Maurice's function in a new body. Raka is both a character and a citation.

The unresolved fate — design or hedge?

The film never confirms Raka's death: no body, no witness, a current that could deposit as easily as drown. Critics noted the disappearance's emotional weight and its openness in the same breath. The archive's reading is that the openness is functional, not evasive: the story needs Raka's teaching to operate without his supervision (hence removal) and needs Noa's world to retain one unspent hope (hence ambiguity). Whether a sequel cashes the ambiguity is a question this file leaves, as the film does, to the river.

Unresolved questions the film leaves about him

Three: whether he lives; whether the Order has any other remnant he did not know of; and whether his faith in Mae — the blanket, the rescue — would survive learning what she was. The film's silence on the third is its most respectful cut: Raka is the only character never required to price his compassion with full information.

10 / KEY MOMENTS

Key Moments

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
The ruin of books

Noa finds him among the old world's remains — an orangutan who can explain what a book is, in a world that has forgotten. The franchise's memory, embodied in one hermit.

The necklace given

He presses the Order's pendant on a stranger of another clan — the legacy's whole survival strategy, performed in a single gesture of trust.

"We call them all Nova"

Food, a blanket and a borrowed name for a feral human: the doctrine's demonstration, and the film's most-quoted line of exposition disguised as kindness.

The defense of Mae

Against Noa's suspicion he argues the teaching's hardest clause — that humans are individuals, not a category — with his own conduct as the only evidence.

The river

He holds Mae out of the rapids and is taken by them. The film's gentlest character exits the way his Order did: in the act of the teaching, unwitnessed by any record.

The necklace at the climax

His posthumous scene: the pendant on Noa's chest as the clan sings and the eagles strike — the transmission, visibly completed.

11 / BEHIND THE CHARACTER

Behind the Character

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Casting

Peter Macon — a veteran character actor known to genre audiences from The Orville and Shameless — was announced among the film's early cast in September 2022, with his role initially undisclosed. The character was written by screenwriter Josh Friedman as the new film's Maurice figure: the peacemaker-scholar through whom Caesar's true teachings reach the new generation.

Performance

Macon's performance capture work drew the film's warmest critical notices: reviewers called Raka the standout of the ensemble, the film's most likable character, and a performance of mildness and inner fire that steals its scenes. The character's sudden removal was widely singled out as the film's most affecting beat — and the brevity of the performance relative to its impact became a critical talking point in itself.

Design of the character

Raka is designed as an anachronism on purpose: a monastic figure — hermitage, books, relic pendant, doctrine — assembled from the franchise's own iconography, down to a symbol drawn from Caesar's childhood home. The archive notes the documented design logic (the Maurice succession, the Order's monastic structure) and records that detailed accounts of the character's facial design process were not part of the verified record consulted for this file.

12 / LEGACY & SIGNIFICANCE

Legacy & Significance

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Within the franchise, Raka is the proof that Caesar's legacy survived its prophet by three centuries — barely, institutionally, and in a form the prophet would recognize. He is also the reboot continuity's second great orangutan: Maurice's heir in function, carrying the earlier trilogy's conscience into a world that has nearly finished forgetting it. His unresolved fate is among the sequel's most obvious inheritances, and his necklace — already on the new protagonist's chest — is positioned as the era's defining relic.

Within this Galaxy, Raka's file documents the Character Archive's standard for the mentor figure: a character whose importance is measured not in screen time but in transmission — what the story would lose if he were removed. By that measure the archive files him, with the critics, as this film's indispensable ape.
