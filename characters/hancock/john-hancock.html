John Hancock

01 / CHARACTER FILE

Overview

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Hancock is the superhero film turned inside out: a being of flight, invulnerability and seismic strength who has spent eighty years as a vagrant drunk, because omnipotence without a past, a people or a purpose curdles. He intervenes in Los Angeles's crimes the way weather intervenes — effectively and catastrophically, leaving millions in damage and a city that hates him for saving it. The train he derails to save Ray Embrey is the hinge: Ray, a public-relations man with a gratitude debt and a logo (the AllHeart), rebuilds him by the book — apology, prison, patience — until the city that cursed him begs for him. Then the book runs out: Mary Embrey, Ray's wife, is what Hancock is, the last of a paired kind, and her account of their three thousand years — and of why being near her makes them both mortal — turns his rehabilitation into a choice between the only love his forgotten life contains and the life itself.

ROLE

The problem and the hero

Protagonist; the last male of his kind, the city's disaster and its saviour in the same body.

DEFINING TRAIT

Power without a past

Smith plays him as a man whose invulnerability is the least interesting thing about him — the performance is all sullenness, thirst and amnesia, a god with a hangover and no one to call.

FATE

Alive, at distance

He survives by leaving — leaping away from the dying Mary until distance restarts her heart — and a month later paints the AllHeart logo across the Moon, exiled from his own kind by the physics of loving them.

Place in the story

Hancock's page is the record of the superhero as a public-relations and metaphysical problem at once: what a city owes its disaster, and what an immortal owes the one person whose company is fatal to them both. The Galaxy keeps it as the last-of-his-kind file — a god's biography with the first eighty years missing.

03 / BIOGRAPHY

Biography: Their Story

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL FULL CHARACTER STORY

Hancock's biography, as he can tell it, begins in a Miami hospital eighty years before the film: he woke with a head injury, total amnesia, and a body that would not age, break or explain itself; no one came for him; a nurse asked for his "John Hancock" on the forms and the phrase became his name. In the decades since he has drifted to Los Angeles and into the bottle — the film introduces him asleep on a bench, drinking, roused to stop a freeway chase with the subtlety of a landslide: the criminals stopped, the freeway destroyed, the city's bill and its hatred itemised on the news. His interventions are a public utility run as a public nuisance, and the city's verdict on him is unanimous and, on the evidence, fair.

The derailment changes the management. Saving Ray Embrey — a PR executive whose car is stuck on the tracks — costs the city a train, and Ray, alone in Los Angeles, responds with gratitude and a plan: the AllHeart campaign, a full rehabilitation. Apologise. Surrender to the outstanding warrants and serve the prison time like a citizen. Wait, while crime rises and the city's opinion turns, until the call comes. It comes at a bank robbery run by Red Parker, whose hostages wear dead man's switches; Hancock, released into the crisis, ends it by severing Parker's hand — and walks out a hero, in a proper costume, to applause. The rehabilitation is total. It is also the moment the film's real story walks in, wearing Ray's wife's face.

Mary Embrey has been cold to him from the first dinner, and drunk one night he learns why: he kisses her, and she throws him through the wall of the house. She is what he is. Her account — given in stages, with one deflection (that they are siblings) later corrected by her own admission that he was her husband in another life — is the film's cosmology: their kind lived three thousand years, were called gods and angels, and were built in pairs; a pair that stays together becomes mortal together, aging, weakening, killable. Eighty years ago in Miami they were attacked; his head injury took his memory; and Mary, knowing proximity would finish what the attack started, left him to forget her — the abandonment that made his eighty years of thirst. The knowledge nearly kills them both on schedule: weakened by closeness, Mary is shot in a robbery and Hancock bleeds beside her in hospital, where Parker, escaped, comes to finish the job. Ray kills Parker with a fire axe; and Hancock, dying on the hospital floor, makes the only move his nature allows — he hurls himself away, bounding across the city, putting distance between them until, miles out, Mary's heart restarts with his removal. A month later he is in New York, whole again, and has painted Ray's AllHeart logo across the face of the Moon — the largest thank-you in the history of public relations, and a signature visible from the exile he now keeps.

The biography above is an editorial paraphrase of the documented plot accounts (Wikipedia and the cited sources), not a scene-by-scene transcript.

04 / PERSONALITY & PSYCHOLOGY

Personality & Psychology

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

This section is editorial interpretation, grounded in the character's on-screen actions.

The sullenness of the unclaimed

Interpretation: Hancock's drinking and contempt are the film's diagnosis before it is stated — an immortal whom no one came for, whose every rescue is anonymous and whose every bill is public. His rudeness is a man invoicing a world that never thanked him, until one man does.

The student of the book

His rehabilitation works because, underneath the contempt, he wants it to: the prison surrender, the patience, the costume — he plays the citizen with a convert's rigour, which is the performance's quiet joke and its pathos.

The final leap

His last act in the film is departure as a medical procedure — love expressed as distance, at ballistic speed. The film's whole metaphysics lands in that choice: he saves her by leaving her, again, but this time knowing why, and waving from the Moon.

05 / ABILITIES & SKILLS

Abilities, Skills & Weaknesses

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
THE FULL PACKAGE

Flight, strength, invulnerability

He flies, stops trains with his body, and is impervious to weapons and weather alike — the complete classical kit, exercised with a drunk's finesse.

IMMORTALITY

Three thousand years, per Mary's account

Ageless and unaging across millennia — conditional, fatally, on distance from his pair.

PUBLIC RELATIONS, LEARNED

The rehabilitation

Apology, patience and timing, acquired from Ray: the film's second superpower, and the one that changes his life first.

WEAKNESS

Proximity, and the bottle

Near Mary, he bleeds; away from purpose, he drinks. His two vulnerabilities are the same one at different scales: he cannot be close to anything — a person, a city, a life — without it costing him the invulnerability that isolates him.

08 / SYMBOLISM & THEMES

Symbolism & Themes

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

This section is editorial interpretation.

The Moon logo

Interpretation: the AllHeart painted on the Moon is the film's thesis in one image — the exile's signature written where his pair can see it and he cannot reach her: public relations as the love language of a man who must love at a distance or not at all.

The name

Interpretation: "John Hancock" is a signature with no signer — a name that means "the mark a person makes" attached to a man with no past to sign for. The film's last joke on it is the Moon: the signature, finally, at the scale of the signer.

09 / ANALYSIS & INVESTIGATION

Analysis & Investigation

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

This section is editorial analysis. Documented facts are cited to the sources in Section 13; readings of the character's function are the archive's own.

Function: the superhero, audited

The archive records Hancock as the genre's audit from inside: it prices the rescues (a train, a freeway, a city's patience), itemises the hero's damage, and then rebuilds him not with a bigger power but with manners — apology, patience, gratitude received. The metaphysical plot completes the audit: even his immortality turns out to be a relationship with terms and conditions. He is the rare screen god whose story is entirely about what his power costs other people and what other people's proximity costs him.

11 / BEHIND THE CHARACTER

Behind the Character

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Will Smith

Smith was attached through the project's long development — the screenplay, by Vince Gilligan and Vy Vincent Ngo, circulated for a decade under earlier titles before Peter Berg directed it — and the production built the film around the inversion of Smith's persona. The train derailment and the hospital leap were staged as the effects centrepieces of a film that spends its spectacle budget early and its heart late, by design.

12 / LEGACY & SIGNIFICANCE

Legacy & Significance

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Hancock is the 2000s' defining deconstruction of the superhero — the drunk god, the PR rehabilitation and the Moon-sized signature. In the Galaxy's records his page stands as the last-of-his-kind file: eighty missing years, one believer, one pair, and a heart painted on the Moon by the man exiled from his own kind.
