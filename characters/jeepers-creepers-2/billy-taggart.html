01 / CHARACTER FILE

Overview

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Billy Taggart is on screen for minutes and is the reason for everything. Sent into the cornfield to shoo crows off the scarecrows, he notices the talons on one scarecrow's feet — and the scarecrow takes him: dragged through the corn before his father's and brother's eyes, carried into the sky on the feeding cycle's 22nd day. His taking builds the harpoon, staffs the hunt, and drives the film. And he returns — in Minxie Hayes's visions, where the Creeper's dead, Billy and Darry Jenner together, attempt to warn the besieged bus of what is hunting it. The archive keeps Billy's page under its rule for the absent: he is the plot's emotional core, he appears in person at its origin, and his vision appearances are recorded as visions — the film's own mechanism, stated as such, never upgraded to fact about any afterlife.

03 / BIOGRAPHY

Biography: Their Story

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL FULL CHARACTER STORY

Billy is the Taggarts' youngest, working the farm beside his father and brother on the feeding cycle's 22nd day. Sent to shoo the crows from the scarecrows, he approaches one and stops: its feet end in talons. He runs, yelling for help — and the scarecrow leaps, pounces, and drags him through the cornfield as Jack Sr. and Jack Jr. sprint after them, arriving second as the creature sprouts wings and takes the boy into the sky. The film shows no more of Billy alive, and never shows his body: his fate beyond the taking is the record's silence, and this page keeps it.

His second presence in the film is as a messenger. In Minxie Hayes's visions — recorded here as the film presents them, as visions — Billy appears with Darry Jenner, the Creeper's victim from the first film, and the two attempt to warn her about the creature hunting the bus. The warning system of the siege is thus staffed by its casualties: the taken son of the county becomes part of the intelligence that saves the bus's marked students, while on the ground his father's harpoon — built because of him — does the rest.

04 / PERSONALITY & PSYCHOLOGY

Personality & Psychology

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

This section is editorial interpretation, grounded in the character's on-screen actions.

The perceiver

Interpretation: Billy's one gift is noticing — the talons under the disguise, the wrongness in the familiar. The film's prologue is a parable of attention: the child sees truly and it does not save him, which is the horror the rest of the film is organised against.

The ordinary boy

He is given no precocity, no speeches — a chore, a run, a yell for help. The archive records the ordinariness as the performance's and the writing's joint discipline.

08 / SYMBOLISM & THEMES

Symbolism & Themes

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

This section is editorial interpretation.

The scarecrow

Interpretation: the guardian effigy that is itself the predator is the film's whole rural horror in one figure — the thing posted to protect the crop, eating the farmer's son. Billy's page carries the image because he is the one who paid for its meaning.

09 / ANALYSIS & INVESTIGATION

Analysis & Investigation

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

This section is editorial analysis. Documented facts are cited to the sources in Section 13; readings of the character's function are the archive's own.

Function: the absent centre, again

The analysis the archive records follows this run's precedent: Billy is kept because he is the plot's emotional core and appears in person at its origin; his vision appearances are recorded as the film's mechanism, not as metaphysics. Structurally, he is the hinge between the film's two plots — his taking starts the hunt, his warning arms the siege — a character who connects both fronts without surviving the first reel.

12 / LEGACY & SIGNIFICANCE

Legacy & Significance

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Billy Taggart stands in the Galaxy record as the prologue victim done at full weight — a whole film (a harpoon, a hunt, a 23-year vigil, a warning system) unfolded from one boy's chore — and his scarecrow remains the franchise's most reproduced image of rural dread.
