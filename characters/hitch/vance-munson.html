01 / CHARACTER FILE

Overview

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Vance Munson is the film's smallest role and its load-bearing villain: the man the method exists to exclude. He arrives at Hitch's office as a customer of exactly the wrong kind — he wants Casey Sedgewick for an evening, and he wants the doctor's technique as a tool for the taking — and Hitch refuses him flat, in the scene that defines the practice's ethic: the service is for men who want the woman, not the night. Vance proceeds uncoached, burns Casey with borrowed polish, and then, when Sara Melas comes investigating the legend, commits the act the whole third act hangs on: he lets her believe he was Hitch's client — borrowing the refused doctor's prestige to varnish his own conduct, and handing the columnist the one false fact her exposé needs. The film's verdict on him is delivered by Hitch at the speed-dating confrontation, in terms the archive preserves as the practice's charter: men like Vance are the reason the service exists — because the sincere men need levelling against the naturals who treat women as evenings.

ROLE

The refused client

The method's negative definition; the exposé's false source; the plot's detonator at one remove.

DEFINING TRAIT

Polish as predation

Vance's smoothness is entirely instrumental — a set of surfaces maintained for extraction — and the film is precise that it is not the method's product but its opposite: technique without the ethic that licenses it.

FATE

Exposed by the defence

His lie is corrected on the record in the speed-dating confrontation; the film leaves him where his kind is left in its accounting — outside the wedding, uninvited by the narrative.

Place in the story

Vance is the Hitch batch's control case for the method's morality: everything Hitch is accused of being, Vance actually is. His page carries the refusal — the practice's founding ethic, stated in one office scene — and the lie that tests it in print.

03 / BIOGRAPHY

Biography: Their Story

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL FULL CHARACTER STORY

Vance's film is three scenes and a consequence, and the file follows the record in that proportion. The refusal comes first in importance whatever its order on screen: Vance presents himself at the date doctor's office as a client — he has a target, Casey Sedgewick, and a requirement, one evening — and Hitch, whose vetting is the practice's whole moral architecture, reads the requirement and declines the engagement. The scene is brief and total: the legend has a door, and this is what it is closed against. Vance proceeds without coaching, deploys his native polish on Casey, and burns her in the manner of his kind — the injury that reaches Sara Melas as a friend's story and starts the investigation.

His second act is the lie. Sara, hunting the date doctor, reaches Vance as a source — a man who name-dropped the legend — and Vance, offered the chance to varnish a squalid episode with a professional's prestige, lets her believe the coaching happened: that his conduct with Casey was the date doctor's work. The archive records the lie's exact mechanics because the plot does: it is not a fabrication but an implication, allowed to stand — the cheapest kind of falsehood, and the kind a columnist's deadline is built to miss. Printed, it converts the exposé from a true story (the practice exists) into a false verdict (the practice produces Vances). The correction, when Hitch delivers it at the speed-dating confrontation, names Vance as the charter's negative example: the service exists because of men like him. The film spends no further time on him, and neither does this file: his function is complete — the refusal, the burn, the lie — and the wedding he is not at is his final entry.

09 / ANALYSIS & INVESTIGATION

Analysis & Investigation

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

This section is editorial analysis. Documented facts are cited to the sources in Section 13; readings of the character's function are the archive's own.

Function: the negative definition

Vance exists so the film can state its ethic by exclusion rather than by speech: the method is what refuses him. The analysis the archive records is that his lie is equally functional — the plot requires the exposé to be wrong in exactly one fact, and Vance is that fact's only plausible source: a man whose whole character is the borrowing of unearned credibility.
