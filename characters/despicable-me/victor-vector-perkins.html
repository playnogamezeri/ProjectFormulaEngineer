01 / CHARACTER FILE

Overview

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Vector is the film's argument about what villainy becomes when it is inherited rather than earned. He opens the story having stolen the Great Pyramid of Giza — the crime of the century, committed as a debut — and spends the film as Gru's nemesis: ambushing him for the shrink ray (revenge for a frozen head), fortifying himself behind sharks and missiles, and finally kidnapping the three girls to trade for the Moon. His gadgetry is genuine — the piranha gun, the squid launcher, a fortress that eats aircraft — but the film's reveal prices it all: Vector is Victor Perkins, the son of the Bank of Evil's president, and his empire is a family business with better branding. His ending is the film's neatest piece of physics: holding the girls' ransom, he refuses to complete the exchange, and the temporarily-shrunk Moon expands back to full size inside his aircraft, leaving modern villainy's crown prince stranded on the surface of the thing he tried to own — dancing, in the credits' coda, in the dark, alone with his brand.

ROLE

The antagonist

The rival villain: the profession’s new management, and the old villain’s mirror.

DEFINING TRAIT

Branding over substance

The name, the tracksuit, the lair tour: he is a supervillain as a marketing exercise — which works, until physics attends.

FATE

Stranded on the Moon

The re-expanding Moon destroys his aircraft and leaves him on its surface: the ransom’s collector, collected.

Place in the story

Vector is Gru's before-picture and after-picture in one: the villain Gru's mother might have applauded, and the son Mr. Perkins funded instead of loving in any way the film can detect. Within the set, his page pairs with Gru's as the profession's two generations, and with Perkins's absence — the father who appears only as a bank and a reveal.

03 / BIOGRAPHY

Biography: Their Story

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL FULL CHARACTER STORY

Vector's film is a hostile takeover conducted as a victory lap. He enters as the pyramid's thief — the inflatable replacement still bouncing on the Giza plateau — and as Gru's ambusher: catching the older villain mid-triumph with the stolen shrink ray, he takes it, in declared revenge for Gru's having frozen his head at their first meeting, and retires behind the most fortified domestic address in animation. The fortress sequences are his character in architecture: sharks in the moat and the lobby, missile systems, a lair designed as a showroom, and a villain who gives tours of his own menace — the piranha gun demonstrated with pride ('it shoots piranhas'), the squid launcher, the cookie robots of Gru's Trojan horse received as a delivery. His one penetration is the girls' cookie route, which Gru rides into the fortress to steal the ray back — a humiliation Vector answers, once his father's intelligence identifies Gru's new domestic arrangements, with the film's darkest move: kidnapping Margo, Edith and Agnes against the shrunken Moon.

The exchange is his audit. Gru delivers the Moon without negotiation — a priority Vector visibly cannot compute — and Vector, holding both prizes, simply declines to release the girls, the villain's prerogative exercised as a whim. Physics overrules the whim: Dr. Nefario's law of the shrink ray — temporary, and faster for bigger objects — re-expands the Moon inside Vector's aircraft in mid-flight, wrecking it and marooning its pilot on the lunar surface as the Moon resumes its size and orbit. The film's coda leaves him there, dancing in the dark in his tracksuit: the profession's crown prince, in sole possession of the thing every villain in the film was trying to steal, at the price of everything else.

04 / PERSONALITY & PSYCHOLOGY

Personality & Psychology

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

This section is editorial interpretation, grounded in the character's on-screen actions.

The brand manager

Interpretation: Vector's villainy is a presentation — the name with its slogan, the orange tracksuit, the fortress as showroom. The archive reads him as the film's satire of the start-up villain: disruption as a wardrobe, funded by the oldest money in the film, his father's bank.

Entitlement as a superpower

He has never been told 'eh' in his life, and the film prices the difference exactly: Gru steals to rebut a childhood; Vector steals because the family's portfolio lacked a pyramid. His confidence is real, and it is entirely uncollateralised by experience.

The whim

His refusal to complete the exchange — holding the Moon and the girls, because he can — is the film's definition of his evil, set against Gru's professionalism: Gru's villainy had rules, schedules and a code of trade; Vector's has only appetite, which is why the film trusts physics, not Gru, to punish it.

08 / SYMBOLISM & THEMES

Symbolism & Themes

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

This section is editorial interpretation.

Direction and magnitude

His chosen name is the film's thesis about him: a vector is defined by direction and magnitude, and he has both — entirely supplied by others. The archive reads the slogan as the confession: a quantity with a direction is still only as real as what it points at, and Vector points at whatever his father's money is facing.

The Moon, occupied

Every villain in the film wants the Moon as a trophy; Vector alone gets to keep it, as a prison. The archive files the ending as the film's neatest moral geometry: the prize, possessed entire, is indistinguishable from exile — the difference being everything he traded away to hold it.

09 / ANALYSIS & INVESTIGATION

Analysis & Investigation

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

This section is editorial analysis. Documented facts are cited to the sources in Section 13; readings of the character's function are the archive's own.

The heir as antagonist

The archive reads Vector as the film's structural masterstroke: by making the villain a funded son, the film turns its caper into a comparative study of fathers — Perkins's chequebook against Gru's surrendered Moon — and lets the audience grade parenthood by its products. Vector is not evil the way Gru is despicable; he is unraised, at scale, with a fortress.

Comedy as the sentence

The film punishes its villain with its own genre: not death but marooning, not tragedy but a dance in the dark in a tracksuit. The archive notes the sentence's precision — for a villain whose whole being is presentation, the film's worst punishment is an audience of none, forever, on the Moon he branded.
