01 / CHARACTER FILE

Overview

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Gru is the franchise's founding paradox: a supervillain whose evil is entirely professional. He freezes the coffee queue, pops children's balloons, and maintains a lair of genuine menace beneath a suburban house — and the film's first act establishes, with a shrink ray and a stolen pyramid, that he is not even the worst man in his own profession: the younger Vector has out-stolen him, and the Bank of Evil will not fund a comeback without collateral. Gru's answer is the heist of the century — shrink the Moon and steal it, the astronaut dream his mother Marlena met with a lifetime of 'eh' — and its instrument is three orphan girls from Miss Hattie's Home for Girls, adopted as a cookie-delivery Trojan horse to penetrate Vector's fortress. The film is the record of the instrument outgrowing the plan: the girls reprogram the lair, the schedule and the villain, until the Moon heist and the ballet recital fall on the same day and Gru must choose, in public, which of his two lives is the performance. He steals the Moon. Then he trades it — offers it up for the kidnapped girls without a flicker — and the film's verdict lands: the despicable man was a costume worn by a father who had not been cast yet.

ROLE

The protagonist

The supervillain as leading man: the heist’s author and its saboteur, in one bald head.

DEFINING TRAIT

Evil, professionally; everything else, accidentally

His villainy is a career; his kindnesses are unplanned and therefore, the film argues, the truth.

FATE

A father, with the Moon restored

The Moon returned to orbit by physics, the girls adopted in earnest: the villain, retired by his own heist’s collateral.

Place in the story

Gru is the film entire: every other page in this set is a pressure on his double life — the rival who out-villains him, the scientist who schedules him, the three girls who re-purpose him. Within the Galaxy, his page is the franchise's founding file: the villain before the family, in the act of becoming it.

03 / BIOGRAPHY

Biography: Their Story

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL FULL CHARACTER STORY

Gru's film opens with his profession's humiliation: the Great Pyramid of Giza has been stolen — replaced with an inflatable — by a younger villain, and Gru's own résumé (the freeze ray, the fart gun, the lair) is suddenly the field's second-best. His comeback is pitched to the Bank of Evil's president, Mr. Perkins: shrink the Moon and steal it, the childhood astronaut dream his mother Marlena answered with 'eh' in every flashback the film provides. Perkins's terms are the plot's first hinge: funding follows the shrink ray, which Gru duly steals from an East Asian research base — and loses, in mid-air triumph, to Vector, the pyramid thief, who ambushes him and takes it as revenge for a frozen head. Vector's fortress is impenetrable to villains and open, daily, to three orphan girls selling cookies. Gru's adoption of Margo, Edith and Agnes from Miss Hattie's Home for Girls — under a dentist's persona, with the Box of Shame in living memory — is therefore filed, in his own ledger, as logistics.

The logistics colonise the ledger. The girls move into the lair's orbit — the Minions re-tasked, the house re-zoned, a roller-coaster at Super Silly Fun Land ridden because children need an adult aboard — and Gru's schedule acquires a second column, headed by a ballet recital. Perkins, unimpressed by the delay, cuts the funding; the girls' piggy bank and the Minions' pooled resources finance the rocket anyway, in the film's quietest mutiny. Nefario, reading the distraction correctly and the man incorrectly, arranges the girls' return to the orphanage — Gru lets it happen, flies, and steals the Moon on recital day, shrinking it to a souvenir. He comes home to an empty house and a kidnapping: Perkins has told Vector — revealed as Perkins's own son — that Gru has the ray, and Vector has taken the girls against the Moon's return. Gru surrenders the Moon without negotiation. Vector refuses the exchange anyway, and physics finishes the argument: the shrink effect is temporary, the bigger the object the faster it reverts, and the Moon expands back into orbit through Vector's aircraft, stranding him on its surface. The girls are rescued in mid-air, one falling hand at a time — Margo's trust, rehearsed all film, spent at altitude — and the film closes at home, the recital restaged in the lair, the adoption made real: the supervillain, his three daughters, and a bedtime story he has written himself.

The biography above is an editorial paraphrase of the documented plot accounts (Wikipedia and the cited sources), not a scene-by-scene transcript.

04 / PERSONALITY & PSYCHOLOGY

Personality & Psychology

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

This section is editorial interpretation, grounded in the character's on-screen actions.

The professional

Interpretation: Gru's villainy is a trade he practises rather than a nature he has — the film's evidence is his incompetence at every unscripted cruelty and his fluency at every unscripted kindness. The archive reads him as the franchise's thesis in one man: 'despicable' is his job title, and the film is his resignation, processed as a heist.

The ‘eh’ economy

His mother's lifelong verdict — 'eh' — is the heist's true motive, and the film is exact about it: the Moon is not wealth or power but a rebuttal, addressed to a woman in the audience of his childhood. The girls give him the first applause of his life, and it is priced, correctly, above the Moon.

The schedule’s convert

He is converted not by argument but by calendar: the recital and the rocket share a day, and the film watches him choose wrong, learn the price in one empty house, and choose again at the price of the Moon — the archive files the two choices as his real arc, the first being the tuition for the second.

05 / ABILITIES & SKILLS

Abilities, Skills & Weaknesses

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
VILLAINY

The complete practice

Freeze rays, cookie robots, a lair, an army of Minions and a successful Moon theft: the profession’s full toolkit, exercised at the highest level.

IMPROVISATION

The mid-air adjustments

Stolen rays, cut funding, kidnapped daughters: his plans fail continuously and his recoveries are the film.

FATHERHOOD

The unplanned gift

Bedtime stories, amusement parks, a recital restaged in a lair: the talent he never trained is the one the film grades him on.

WEAKNESS

The applause he never had

Marlena’s ‘eh’ built the Moon heist; the girls’ belief rebuilds the man. Both are the same hunger, differently fed.

08 / SYMBOLISM & THEMES

Symbolism & Themes

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

This section is editorial interpretation.

The Moon

The film's prize is a childhood rebuttal — the biggest object a boy told 'eh' could steal — and its disposal is the thesis: Gru gives it up in one sentence for three children, and physics gives it back to the sky anyway. The archive reads the Moon as the last thing Gru steals and the first thing he returns.

The recital

A children's ballet recital, restaged in a supervillain's lair, is the film's closing composition: the two lives on one stage, the audience in goggles. The archive files it as the franchise's founding image — the lair, repurposed as a home, with the villain in the front row, applauding correctly at last.

09 / ANALYSIS & INVESTIGATION

Analysis & Investigation

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

This section is editorial analysis. Documented facts are cited to the sources in Section 13; readings of the character's function are the archive's own.

The heist as a custody story

The archive reads the film's structure as a custody hearing conducted in the grammar of a caper: every heist beat is doubled by a domestic one (the fortress infiltration is a cookie route; the rocket launch is scheduled against a recital), and the climax's real question is not whether Gru can steal the Moon but which appointment he will keep. Illumination's first feature is thereby a family film wearing a villain film's coat — the reverse of the usual arrangement — and its durability comes from the coat fitting.

Carell’s crack

The performance's design — menace with the tenderness leaking through — is the film's whole argument in a voice: the archive notes that Gru is never more villainous than when he is performing villainy (the queue, the balloon) and never more himself than when he forgets to (the roller coaster, the puppet book). The adoption plot simply gives the leak a schedule.

11 / BEHIND THE CHARACTER

Behind the Character

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Illumination’s first feature

Despicable Me was the debut feature of Illumination Entertainment, directed by Chris Renaud and Pierre Coffin — Coffin also voicing the Minions, the film’s breakout creation.

The design

Gru’s silhouette — the vulture hunch, the scarf, the bald head — was designed as a villain a child could read at fifty paces, which the film then spends two hours refuting from inside the silhouette.

12 / LEGACY & SIGNIFICANCE

Legacy & Significance

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Gru became modern animation's defining reformed villain — the anchor of a franchise of sequels and Minion spin-offs — and his first film the template: a heist that is secretly a custody story. Within the Galaxy, his page is the founder's file: the Moon, the recital, and the resignation.
