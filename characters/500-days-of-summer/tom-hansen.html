01 / CHARACTER FILE

Overview

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Tom is the film's narrator, subject and unreliable editor: the 500 days are his memories, presented out of order, of a relationship he experienced as a destiny and the film presents as a case study. He meets Summer Finn on January 8, the boss's new assistant, and falls in love with the idea he has brought to the meeting — that somewhere there is one person, that meeting her will feel like recognition, and that a shared taste in music is a signature on a contract. Summer states her terms plainly and repeatedly: she does not believe in love, does not want a boyfriend, is not looking for anything serious. Tom agrees to her terms and spends the relationship trying to renegotiate them by devotion. The breakup at day 290, the engagement ring at the party, the transfer to the consolations department, the depression measured in junk food — and then the film's actual plot, which is not the romance but the editing: Tom learning, via his preteen sister's counsel and a bench conversation at day 488, to look at the same footage again and see what was always in it.

ROLE

The protagonist; the editor

The story is his memory of the relationship, and his arc is learning to read his own footage honestly — the film's romance is the raw material; the editing is the plot.

DEFINING TRAIT

The script in his head

He does not fall in love with Summer so much as cast her — the Galaxy's film page's diagnosis — and every heartbreak in the film is a collision between the casting and the woman.

FATE

The clock, reset

He quits the card company, returns to architecture, makes his peace at the bench — and on day 500 meets a woman named Autumn, as the on-screen clock winds back to its start.

Place in the story

Tom is the Galaxy's definitive unreliable romantic: the archive treats his page as the record of a memory being corrected — what he believed, what the footage showed, and what it cost him to finally watch it in order.

03 / BIOGRAPHY

Biography: Their Story

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL FULL CHARACTER STORY

Tom's documented course begins before Summer: a trained architect who has shelved the training to write greeting cards in Los Angeles, manufacturing sentiments he privately believes in their strongest form — that true love exists, is fated, and can be recognised. On January 8 he meets Summer Finn, his boss's new assistant from Michigan, and the recognition he has been rehearsing fires at once, assisted by a shared taste in music. His friend McKenzie, drunk at a karaoke night, announces Tom's feelings to the room and to Summer — forcing the relationship into existence — and Summer, on her stated terms, agrees to something casual: she does not believe in love, does not want a boyfriend, is not looking for anything serious. Tom accepts the terms the way he accepts everything in the film: as the first act of a story whose ending he has already read.

The middle is happiness, honestly recorded and honestly edited: IKEA trips played as domestic bliss, his favourite bench above the city's buildings, Summer confiding things she has told no one — day 109's intimacy filed by Tom as escalation toward the destiny. His friends and his preteen half-sister Rachel push him to ask where they stand; Summer brushes it off — if they are both happy, why does it matter? On day 290 they see The Graduate; Summer weeps at the ending Tom had always read as a romantic fairy tale, and over pancakes at a diner she casually ends it: it hasn't been working. What follows is the film's second movement — Summer quits the company, Tom is moved to consolations, a train ride and a party rebuild his hope to its peak before the engagement ring on her hand destroys it, and the depression runs its documented course of alcohol and junk food. The recovery, when it comes, is the film's thesis in action: he rededicates himself to the architecture Summer had always pushed him toward, builds a portfolio, interviews. On day 488, at his bench, Summer is waiting — married, certain, kind — and tells him he was right that true love exists; he was only wrong that it was with her. He wishes her happiness and means it. Twelve days later, at an interview, he meets Autumn, and the clock rewinds.

The biography above is an editorial paraphrase of the documented plot accounts (Wikipedia and the cited sources), not a scene-by-scene transcript.

04 / PERSONALITY & PSYCHOLOGY

Personality & Psychology

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

This section is editorial interpretation, grounded in the character's on-screen actions.

The believer

Interpretation: Tom's romanticism is a metaphysics, not a mood — fate, the One, recognition — and the film treats it with respect even while dismantling its application: his error is never believing in love; it is believing the belief exempts him from listening.

The editor

His memory is his real antagonist. He replays the relationship the way the film presents it — out of order, weighted toward the golden days — and his sister's counsel names the practice exactly: check whether you are remembering the relationship or editing it. His arc is a man learning to watch the unedited footage.

Decency under the delusion

His failures are of perception, not of conduct: he is kind to the blind date Alison, honest with her mid-date about not being over someone, generous at the bench at the end. The film's sympathy rests here — the script in his head never makes him cruel, only blind.

05 / ABILITIES & SKILLS

Abilities, Skills & Weaknesses

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
THE EYE

An architect's seeing

His one unambiguous gift is professional: the bench, the buildings, the parking lots spoiling the view — he reads the city the way he wishes he could read people, accurately and with love. His recovery begins when the gift is re-employed.

SINCERITY

Feeling at full strength

Everything he feels is real — the elation staged as a full dance number the morning after, the depression measured in days unshaven. The film never discounts his emotions; it audits his interpretations.

THE REVISION

Learning to re-read

His rarest act is the bench conversation: he arrives with an indictment and leaves with a blessing, having finally heard the sentence Summer says — you were right about love; you were wrong about me — as information rather than as a plot twist.

WEAKNESS

Listening as a formality

Summer tells him her terms at the start, in the middle and at the end, in plain language, and he hears each statement as dialogue in his script rather than as her position. His deafness is total, courteous and catastrophic.

08 / SYMBOLISM & THEMES

Symbolism & Themes

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

This section is editorial interpretation.

The bench

His favourite spot — beloved buildings, spoiled by parking lots: the symbol of his whole vision, a beautiful view with the flaws edited out of the description. Summer meets him there at the end because it is the one place his seeing was always honest.

The greeting cards

He manufactures feelings for a living in other people's words: the symbol of a culture that scripts romance industrially — and his meltdown denunciation of the industry, in the boardroom, is his first unscripted professional act.

The clock

The on-screen day counter, jumping out of order, is memory's own interface — and its final rewind at Autumn's name is the film's whole philosophy in one effect: the counter resets; what the man has learned is up to him.

09 / ANALYSIS & INVESTIGATION

Analysis & Investigation

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

This section is editorial analysis. Documented facts are cited to the sources in Section 13; readings of the character's function are the archive's own.

Whose film is it?

The archive's central file on Tom: the film is explicitly his version — the narrator frames it, the chronology is his memory's, and Summer exists in it only as he perceived her. The analysis: the film's fairness is structural, not verbal — it gives the audience Rachel's counsel and the bench conversation as instruments to correct the narrator, and trusts them to. Tom is neither acquitted nor convicted; he is audited, by his own footage.

The Graduate problem

His misreading of The Graduate — a film about a hollow affair's desperate gesture, read by Tom as a romantic fairy tale — is the film's thesis in miniature, and Summer's tears at the same ending are hers. The analysis: two people watch the same movie and see their own positions reflected; the relationship fails in that cinema before it fails in the diner, and the film dates the beginning of the end to a screening.

11 / BEHIND THE CHARACTER

Behind the Character

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Gordon-Levitt's Tom

Joseph Gordon-Levitt plays Tom; the film is directed by Marc Webb in his feature directorial debut, from a screenplay by Scott Neustadter and Michael H. Weber, per the film's record. The screenplay is documented as drawn from Neustadter's real romance — and the record preserves the detail the archive considers the property's conscience: when Neustadter showed the script to the real woman who inspired Summer, she said she related more to the Tom character.

The tenth-anniversary correction

Entertainment Weekly's tenth-anniversary interviews with Gordon-Levitt and Deschanel addressed the audience's frequent misconception of the characters — including the reading of Summer as a villain — a documented part of the film's reception history that this page's analysis follows.
