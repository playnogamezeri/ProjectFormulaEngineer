Jules Winnfield

Marsellus Wallace's contract killer who recites a paraphrased Ezekiel passage before executions — until the morning the bullets miss, and he spends the rest of the film doing the hardest thing in it: reinterpreting his own sermon and changing his life.

01 / CHARACTER FILE

Overview

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Jules Winnfield and his partner Vincent Vega are hitmen sent to retrieve a briefcase from Brett, a business partner who tried to double-cross their boss, Marsellus Wallace. Jules's ritual is a paraphrased passage from the Book of Ezekiel, delivered before the killing. This time, a fourth man bursts from the bathroom and empties a gun at them at close range — and misses with every shot. Vincent calls it luck. Jules calls it divine intervention, and the disagreement structures the rest of his story: through the accidental killing of their informant Marvin, the cleanup at Jimmie's house under Winston Wolfe, and a diner robbery he refuses to answer with murder, Jules works out what retirement — 'to walk the earth' — actually requires of him.

ROLE

The convert

Co-lead of the film's third storyline in Tarantino's conception; the only principal whose arc is a deliberate, argued change of life.

DEFINING TRAIT

Interpretation as action

Jules does not feel his way out of the life; he argues his way out, re-reading his own Ezekiel ritual until it indicts him.

FATE

Alive; retired by his own declaration

He leaves the diner having spared the robbers, keeps the briefcase for Marsellus, and walks out of the profession — the film's presentation ends there, and chronologically his story ends there too.

Place in the story

Jules is the film's moral instrument: the same morning, the same miracle, produces his conversion and Vincent's shrug, and the film measures both men by it. The Galaxy card's reading stands — conversion as an intellectual act, performed by a man of rhetorical genius on the only text he knows by heart.

Jackson's performance won the role on a second audition: Wikipedia's cast record documents that his first reading was overshadowed by Paul Calderón, that Harvey Weinstein persuaded him to audition again, and that his performance of the final diner scene won Tarantino over. Jules was scripted with a giant afro; a mistakenly purchased Jheri-curl wig was kept at Jackson's persuasion. He was nominated for the Academy Award for Best Supporting Actor. Tarantino had written the role for Laurence Fishburne, who turned it down.

03 / BIOGRAPHY

Biography: Their Story

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL FULL CHARACTER STORY

Story chronology (the film presents these events out of order; the diner sequences frame the film, while the apartment and cleanup occur earlier the same morning): Jules and Vincent drive to Brett's apartment to retrieve Marsellus Wallace's briefcase. Jules tastes Brett's burger, recites his paraphrased Ezekiel passage, and the two kill Brett for double-crossing Marsellus. A fourth man bursts from the bathroom firing — and misses both men entirely. They kill him, and leave with the briefcase and Marvin, Marsellus's informant inside Brett's crew.

Driving away, Vincent — gun carelessly trained on Marvin — accidentally shoots him in the head when the car hits a bump, covering the interior and both men in blood. They hide the car at the home of Jules's friend Jimmie, who demands the corpse and car be gone before his wife Bonnie returns from work. Marsellus sends the cleaner Winston Wolfe, who directs them to hide the body in the trunk, clean the car, surrender their bloody clothes, and take the car to a junkyard. The hitmen emerge in borrowed casual clothes.

Afterwards, over breakfast at a diner, Jules tells Vincent he plans to retire: their survival at the apartment was, he is convinced, divine intervention — a miracle he is obliged to answer. Vincent disagrees. While Vincent is in the bathroom, two robbers — Pumpkin and Honey Bunny — hold up the diner and demand the briefcase. Jules overpowers Pumpkin and holds him at gunpoint; Honey Bunny points her gun at Jules, and Vincent returns pointing his at her. Jules defuses the standoff by a new reading of his own passage: he has been, he says, the tyranny of evil men, and he is trying to be the shepherd. He lets the robbers keep the money from his own wallet, keeps the briefcase, and lets them leave. Jules and Vincent then deliver the briefcase to Marsellus at his bar — an event the film shows earlier in its presentation order — and Jules's declared intention, restated in the diner, is to retire and walk the earth.

The biography above is an editorial paraphrase of the documented plot accounts (Wikipedia and the cited sources), not a scene-by-scene transcript.

04 / PERSONALITY & PSYCHOLOGY

Personality & Psychology

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

This section is editorial interpretation, grounded in the character's on-screen actions.

The rhetorician

Interpretation: Jules thinks in performed text — scripture paraphrase, negotiation, sermon. His conversion is not a breakdown but a re-reading: the same words, audited. The archive reads him as the rare screen convert whose change is presented as an argument he wins against himself.

Composure as doctrine

Interpretation: in the diner he is outnumbered in every practical sense — a gun on him, his partner's gun creating a crossfire — and he wins by lowering the temperature of the room, including his own. The man who once performed wrath now performs mercy, and the performance is the substance.

Loyalty, re-scoped

Interpretation: he does not betray Marsellus — the briefcase is delivered, the robbers get only his own money. His retirement is negotiated with his obligations, not fled from them, which the film presents as the difference between Jules's exit and every other character's escape attempt.

05 / ABILITIES & SKILLS

Abilities, Skills & Weaknesses

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
SKILL

Oratory under arms

He talks an armed robber down from a robbery in progress while holding him at gunpoint — the diner scene is a negotiation conducted across three weapons.

SKILL

Professional violence

Half of the team that executes Brett's crew and survives the bathroom ambush; his competence is never in question, which is what makes his renunciation of it legible as a choice.

SKILL

Crisis discipline

Through the Marvin disaster he executes Wolfe's protocol without panic — the cleanup is his last professional job, performed flawlessly on his way out of the profession.

LIMIT

The old text

His Ezekiel ritual, examined honestly, convicts him: by his own final reading he has been the tyranny of evil men. The limit is the point — his instrument of authority becomes his instrument of indictment.

07 / CHARACTER ARC

Character Arc

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
The ritual

Apartment: the Ezekiel performance and Brett's execution — Jules at the height of his professional liturgy.

The miracle

A gun emptied at close range, two men untouched. Jules begins, immediately, to interpret.

The mess

Marvin's accidental death and Wolfe's cleanup: the life at its most squalid and most efficient, in the same hour.

The argument

Breakfast: Jules states his retirement and his reasoning; Vincent refuses both. The film lets the debate stand unresolved between them.

The shepherd

The diner robbery answered without killing: the passage re-read, the robbers spared and paid from his own pocket, the briefcase kept. Conversion demonstrated, not declared.

08 / SYMBOLISM & THEMES

Symbolism & Themes

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

This section is editorial interpretation.

The Ezekiel passage

Interpretation: the film's central text is a paraphrase — Jules's version of scripture, as the sources note, is a fictional/paraphrased passage rather than the biblical text. Its meaning changes as its speaker does: threat, then habit, then self-indictment, then a shepherd's charter. The archive flags this as interpretation of a documented device.

The briefcase

Interpretation: Jules is the only character who handles the briefcase as a duty rather than a desire — he surrenders his own money in its place. What is inside it is never shown in the film, and this archive does not speculate.

09 / ANALYSIS & INVESTIGATION

Analysis & Investigation

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

This section is editorial analysis. Documented facts are cited to the sources in Section 13; readings of the character's function are the archive's own.

Two readings of one morning

Analysis: the film's structure is an experiment — one event (the missed shots), two interpreters. Jules's reading costs him his career; Vincent's costs Vincent nothing that morning, and everything later. The archive reads the diner scene as the experiment's result published early: the converted man survives his own robbery; the unconvinced man, in story chronology, has days to live.

Retirement as the film's only completed arc

Analysis: every other principal's story ends in flight, bargain or death; Jules's ends in a decision executed on screen — money given, mercy given, profession resigned. The film's nonlinear order lets the audience watch the proof (the diner) before the premise (the apartment) has finished unfolding.

10 / KEY MOMENTS

Key Moments

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
The passage

Before Brett's execution, Jules recites his paraphrased Ezekiel — the ritual in its original, wrathful use.

The missed shots

A fourth man's gun empties at close range and touches neither hitman: the event Jules will call a miracle.

The Bonnie situation

Marvin dead by accident; Jules helps execute Wolfe's cleanup at Jimmie's house against Bonnie's homecoming deadline.

The breakfast argument

Jules announces his retirement and his divine-intervention reading; Vincent declines to share it.

The shepherd

In the diner standoff Jules re-reads his passage aloud, spares Pumpkin and Honey Bunny, pays them from his own wallet, and keeps the briefcase.

11 / BEHIND THE CHARACTER

Behind the Character

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Written for Fishburne, won by Jackson

Wikipedia's cast record: Tarantino wrote Jules for Laurence Fishburne, who declined (later saying he felt the film glamorised heroin); Eddie Murphy was also considered. Jackson's second audition — the diner scene — won the part, and the Jheri-curl wig, bought in error for an afro part, stayed at his insistence.

The passage

The sources describe Jules's Ezekiel text as a paraphrased/fictional passage from the Book of Ezekiel — Tarantino's construction, not a quotation — which is why this page records Jules's interpretations of it as his interpretations throughout.

12 / LEGACY & SIGNIFICANCE

Legacy & Significance

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Jules is the performance that carried Jackson to an Academy Award nomination and into the centre of American screen acting; the diner sermon is among the most quoted scenes of the 1990s. Within the film's afterlife he is its moral proof-of-concept — the one character who changes on purpose.

In the Galaxy he anchors the archive's conversion line: the hitman whose weapon was a text, and who finally read it correctly — by his own account, which is the only account the film endorses.
