01 / CHARACTER FILE

Overview

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Beth Macintyre is the film's ghost of casting future. The company's prima for years and Leroy's Swan Queen, she is retired in the film's opening movement — announced as a tribute, executed as a disposal — and her rage at the eviction is the production's first scandal: the favourite, suddenly fluent in everything the favour cost. Nina, her replacement, does the film's bravest research: she visits Beth in hospital, where the predecessor lies with her legs ruined after being hit by a car — an apparent suicide attempt, in Leroy's account, which the film records as his account — and reads, in the bed and the bitterness, the tenancy's full terms. Beth's function is prophecy with a face: everything she is, Nina is scheduled to become, on the profession's standard lease — favour, use, disposal — and the film's coldest structural fact is that Nina reads the file entire, in person, and dances anyway.

ROLE

The predecessor

The role’s last tenant: the future Nina is shown, in a hospital bed, early enough to choose — and doesn’t.

DEFINING TRAIT

Fluency after favour

Discarded, she says everything the favourite never could: her scenes are the film’s only unguarded testimony about Leroy’s method.

FATE

Hospitalised; career over

Her legs ruined in the traffic accident, she will never dance again — the tenancy, terminated in every sense.

Place in the story

Beth is the film's memento mori with a dressing room: the Swan Queen's lease, photographed at expiry. Within the set, her page pairs with Leroy's as the method's before-picture, and with Nina's as the file the successor read and signed anyway.

03 / BIOGRAPHY

Biography: Their Story

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL FULL CHARACTER STORY

Beth's film is an eviction in three documents. The first is the announcement: Leroy, before the assembled company, retires his longtime Swan Queen with the full liturgy of gratitude — flowers, praise, finality — and opens the casting that will replace her. Her response, delivered in the corridors and at the patrons' events where the liturgy cannot follow, is the film's first unguarded testimony: the favourite's account of the favour, its intimacies and its price, spoken with the freedom of the already-fired. The second document is the road: Beth is hit by a car, her legs — the instruments, the career, the self — ruined beyond dancing. Leroy's account of the accident, relayed to Nina, is that Beth did it to herself; the archive records the account as his, unadjudicated by the film, and notes its convenience to the teller.

The third document is the hospital visit, the film's quietest set-piece: Nina, newly cast and already frightened, comes to the predecessor's bedside — part apology, part research — and Beth receives her as the lease's outgoing tenant receives the incoming: with the ruined legs on display, the bitterness unedited, and the film's whole warning system contained in one room. Whatever Nina came to learn, she learns: the role's favour is a tenancy, the landlord is Leroy, and the eviction, when it comes, is total. She leaves the hospital and dances toward the same bed by the profession's standard route — which is the film's bleakest sentence about the profession, delivered without a word of commentary.

04 / PERSONALITY & PSYCHOLOGY

Personality & Psychology

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

This section is editorial interpretation, grounded in the character's on-screen actions.

The evicted’s fluency

Interpretation: Beth's rage is the film's only honest speech about its world, and it is licensed entirely by her uselessness — she can afford the truth because she no longer has anything it can cost. The archive reads her as the profession's whistleblower, published posthumously to her own career.

The favourite’s knowledge

She knows Leroy's method from the inside of the favour — the intimacies, the uses, the disposal's choreography — and her testimony's authority is precisely that she is not guessing: she is the method's previous result, describing its next one.

No consolation, offered or accepted

She gives Nina nothing usable — no blessing, no strategy, only the facts of the bed. The archive files the refusal as her last professional act: the one dancer in the film who declines to pretend the tenancy is a career.

08 / SYMBOLISM & THEMES

Symbolism & Themes

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

This section is editorial interpretation.

The ruined legs

Beth's injury is the profession's economics made anatomical: a dancer is her legs' tenant as much as her director's, and the film photographs both evictions in one bed. The archive reads her as the ballet's answer to the Wrestler of Aronofsky's companion film — the body, retired by damage, with nothing underneath it.

The flowers

The retirement liturgy's bouquet recurs across the film's economy of disposals — praise as the profession's anaesthetic, administered publicly so the patient cannot scream. Beth's refusal to stay anaesthetised is her whole character.

09 / ANALYSIS & INVESTIGATION

Analysis & Investigation

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

This section is editorial analysis. Documented facts are cited to the sources in Section 13; readings of the character's function are the archive's own.

The warning that cannot work

The archive reads Beth's structural function as the film's bleakest: she is a perfect warning — credible, specific, delivered in person, in time — and she changes nothing, because the thing she warns against is the only thing Nina has ever wanted. The film's argument about ambition is contained in the hospital scene's outcome: information is not the deficit; the deficit is an alternative life, and neither woman has ever been issued one.

Leroy’s account, flagged

The film's one piece of evidence about Beth's accident arrives through Leroy — that it was a suicide attempt — and the archive preserves it as his account, as the film does. The distinction matters to her file: the landlord's version of the eviction's aftermath is the only version in circulation, which is itself a datum about the tenancy.
