01 / CHARACTER FILE

Overview

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Bill is the saga's final name and its first cause: the flute on the chapel steps, the courteous stranger introduced as the bride's father, the voice that orders the massacre and the hand that fires the head-shot — all because the woman he made, loved and owned by turns discovered she was pregnant and chose, without consulting him, a life he was not in. Volume 2 is largely his: he warns Budd (and is refused), he receives the news of Budd's death and Beatrix's in the same phone call and grieves in silence, and he hosts the climax in his own house — an evening of spaghetti-western domesticity with his daughter, a truth serum he calls the Undisputed Truth, and the saga's most famous speech, his Superman thesis: that Beatrix is a natural-born killer who was only ever playing house. Then he concedes the counter-case — he overreacted, most extremely — fights her briefly, receives the technique he believed untaught, straightens his jacket, and takes his five steps like a man leaving a good dinner. Carradine plays him, per the Galaxy card, as perfect manners over bottomless possessiveness: the saga's evil, conducted as aesthetics to the last breath.

ROLE

The final name

Principal antagonist and the campaign's author; the list exists because of his order, and ends at his table.

DEFINING TRAIT

Possession as aesthetics

He experiences Beatrix as his masterpiece and her departure as a theft; his revenge is therefore curated — a massacre at a wedding, a truth serum instead of thumbscrews, a eulogy instead of a plea.

FATE

Five steps

Killed by the Five Point Palm Exploding Heart Technique in his own home, having conceded the argument and lost the duel in the same hour.

Place in the story

Bill is the saga's definition-keeper: he named the Vipers, trained the Bride, and supplies the thesis (she is a killer by nature) that the whole second volume cross-examines. His death is the thesis's funeral: he dies, fittingly, mid-conversation, having lost the argument first.

03 / BIOGRAPHY

Biography: Their Story

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL FULL CHARACTER STORY

Bill's biography arrives in other people's keeping: Esteban Vihaio's Acapulco memories of the orphaned boy raised among the working girls, who grew into the Snake Charmer — founder of the Deadly Viper Assassination Squad, pupil of Pai Mei, master of the flute and the sword, and the maker of Beatrix Kiddo, his protégée and lover. When she vanished from a job in the middle of the life — having discovered, mid-assignment, that she was pregnant, and chosen the child over the squad without a word — Bill's response was the saga's founding atrocity: he tracked her to the Two Pines chapel in El Paso, played the flute on the steps, submitted to introduction as her father, and then ordered the Vipers into the rehearsal and shot her in the head himself. Believing her dead and the child lost, he raised, when the truth surfaced, the daughter he had made an orphan of her mother: B.B., kept in Mexico, raised on spaghetti westerns and the story of her mother told as an epic.

His volume is a long receiving of consequences. He visits Budd's trailer to warn him — Beatrix lives, the list is moving — and is refused protection's premise by his own brother: perhaps, Budd says, they deserve her revenge; and Budd's lie about pawning the Hanzo sword Bill gave him (he kept it; it surfaces in the trailer fight) lands as the brothers' whole history in one insult. Elle's phone call delivers the double news — Budd dead, and the killer of Budd and of Beatrix (a lie) named at last: Beatrix Kiddo — and Bill grieves both in one silence. Then the doorbell: Beatrix, at his home, discovering B.B. alive. His conduct of the climax is entirely his: the evening permitted, the child put to bed, the truth serum — his own invention, the Undisputed Truth — administered not for advantage but for the record: he wants, at the end, the true account of why she left, and he gives his own in exchange, including the concession that he overreacted in the most extreme way possible. The Superman speech is his closing argument for her nature. Her answer is the technique Pai Mei taught no one — except her. Bill receives it standing, understands it completely ("Pai Mei taught you the Five Point Palm Exploding Heart Technique?"), straightens his jacket, says his farewell, and takes five steps across his own room before his heart ends the saga.

The biography above is an editorial paraphrase of the documented plot accounts (Wikipedia and the cited sources), not a scene-by-scene transcript.

04 / PERSONALITY & PSYCHOLOGY

Personality & Psychology

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

This section is editorial interpretation, grounded in the character's on-screen actions.

The aesthete of possession

Interpretation: Bill's evil is curatorial — he collects people (the squad as snakes, the protégée as masterpiece, the daughter as audience) and experiences their independence as damage to the collection. His courtesy is not a mask over the possessiveness; it is the possessiveness, in its preferred medium.

The truth-teller, at the end

His truth serum is the character's summary: where another villain would torture for advantage, Bill drugs for accuracy. He wants the record straight more than he wants to win — which is why he can concede the argument ("I overreacted") and lose the duel in the same hour with his dignity not merely intact but completed.

The father

His raising of B.B. is the film's most inconvenient fact for every party: by all the evidence of the evening — the child's ease, the stories, the movie nights — he has been a good father. The saga refuses to simplify him at exactly the point where simplification would be easiest.

05 / ABILITIES & SKILLS

Abilities, Skills & Weaknesses

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
THE SNAKE CHARMER

Command of killers

He built and led the Deadly Vipers — the profession's apex organisation — and his orders (a massacre, a called-off hospital hit in Volume 1) are the saga's weather.

SWORD AND FLUTE

The master's arts

A pupil of Pai Mei and a swordsman of the first rank; his brief final duel is lost to a technique, not to skill — the film is careful to keep his competence unblemished to the end.

THE UNDISPUTED TRUTH

His own serum

A truth drug of his devising, administered at the climax as an instrument of record rather than interrogation: his pharmacology, like his evil, is aesthetic.

WEAKNESS

The collection's owner

He cannot conceive of Beatrix's departure as anything but a statement about him — the pregnancy, the child, the new life are all read as theft — and the misreading is the massacre, the coma and the list. His flaw is not cruelty but centrality: he believes the story is his.

08 / SYMBOLISM & THEMES

Symbolism & Themes

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

This section is editorial interpretation.

The flute

Bill's bamboo flute — played on the chapel steps, carried through the saga — is his emblem: music as menace, the artist's instrument in the murderer's hands. Interpretation: the film insists his aesthetic sense and his violence are one faculty, which is why his death is choreographed as an exit from a dinner party.

The Superman speech

His thesis — Clark Kent as the disguise, Superman as the truth; Beatrix's normalcy as the costume, the killer as the woman — is the saga's argument stated by its antagonist at its climax. The film's rebuttal is not a speech but a biography: a bathroom floor, a cartoon, a mother laughing.

09 / ANALYSIS & INVESTIGATION

Analysis & Investigation

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

This section is editorial analysis. Documented facts are cited to the sources in Section 13; readings of the character's function are the archive's own.

The talkative climax

Volume 2's final duel is ninety percent conversation — Tarantino's wager that the saga's real combat was always definitional: what is she? Bill's death follows the argument's conclusion as a formality, five steps of punctuation. The archive ranks the structure among the genre's boldest: a revenge film that ends by cross-examining revenge's heroine and letting the villain score points.

Carradine's resurrection

The role revived Carradine — the Kung Fu star of the 1970s — as the saga's elder: the casting is the film's homage and its argument, a western-and-kung-fu relic playing the man who believes the old life is the only real one. His serenity is the sound of a genre remembering itself.

11 / BEHIND THE CHARACTER

Behind the Character

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

David Carradine

Carradine — a genuine flautist and martial artist — played his own flute passages; the instrument on the chapel steps is his, a detail the production valued as the character's authenticity: the menace is not performed, it is practised.

The Superman speech

The speech adapts an analysis of Superman that circulated in comics criticism for decades; Tarantino's placement of it — as a dying argument about a woman, delivered by her would-be murderer — converted a fan essay into the saga's thesis statement.

12 / LEGACY & SIGNIFICANCE

Legacy & Significance

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Bill endures as the revenge genre's most courteous monster — the villain whose climax is a dinner conversation and whose death is a short, dignified walk. The Superman speech remains the saga's most quoted passage, and his concession — that he overreacted, most extremely — its most quoted act of villainous honesty.
