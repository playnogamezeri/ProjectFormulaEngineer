Carroll Oerstadt

A rejected enlistee turned domestic terrorist — turned away by the Marines and the Army after medical screening flagged his instability — who murders a woman for her truck, bombs a ferry of sailors and their families, and is identified by the very surveillance window his crime provoked into existence.

01 / CHARACTER FILE

Overview

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Oerstadt is the film's wound given a grievance: a man who wanted to serve, was screened out as unstable, and converted the rejection into a war on the institution that refused him — bombing a ferry full of the sailors he was not permitted to become. His tradecraft is real (the staged victim, the purchased truck) and his undoing is the film's central irony: the investigation his bombing provokes builds a window into his own last four days, and he is identified by his face in footage that should not exist. Caught, he confesses to both murders with a believer's calm. In the revised timeline he is killed by Carlin on the ferry he came to bomb — shot by a man who had already heard his confession, four days in the future.

ROLE

The bomber

Principal antagonist; a domestic terrorist whose target selection is his biography.

DEFINING TRAIT

The rejected patriot

His violence is an enlistment application, resentenced: the ferry carries the men he was screened out of joining.

FATE

Killed on his own target

Shot dead by Carlin aboard the Stumpf in the revised timeline, his bomb driven into the river by his intended victim.

Place in the story

Oerstadt is the film's study of grievance as identity: every other principal is defined by a competence or a love; he is defined by a refusal, and the plot is the refusal's radius.

03 / BIOGRAPHY

Biography: Their Story

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL FULL CHARACTER STORY

Oerstadt's preparations are reconstructed through the investigation: he answers Claire Kuchever's truck advertisement, murders her, burns her body and stages it in the river as a ferry victim-to-be — a deception that fails on its timings. On Mardi Gras morning he drives the truck, with its bomb, aboard the Sen. Alvin T. Stumpf and detonates it among the Navy families, killing 543.

The Snow White unit's window catches his movements in the bombing's run-up, and facial recognition names him. Before the arrest, Carlin's note — sent back to warn his past self — is found instead by Agent Larry Minuti, who follows it to Oerstadt's bayou shack alone and is murdered there, his body burned: the bomber's second killing, caused by the investigation's first intervention.

Arrested, Oerstadt confesses — to Claire's murder (he needed her truck) and to Minuti's — with composure, and the case is closed around him. In the revised timeline, the travelled Carlin interrupts Claire's murder at the shack; Oerstadt escapes with the bomb, boards the ferry, recaptures Claire and binds her in the truck. Confronted by a stranger who quotes his own future confession back at him, he is rammed by Claire with the truck and shot dead by Carlin on the deck of his target.

The biography above is an editorial paraphrase of the documented plot accounts (Wikipedia and the cited sources), not a scene-by-scene transcript.

08 / SYMBOLISM & THEMES

Symbolism & Themes

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

This section is editorial interpretation.

The ferry as enlistment

Oerstadt bombs the vessel carrying the men he was forbidden to join — the archive reads the target selection as the film's whole psychology of the man: he does not attack the institution that rejected him; he attacks its members, for being permitted.

09 / ANALYSIS & INVESTIGATION

Analysis & Investigation

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

This section is editorial analysis. Documented facts are cited to the sources in Section 13; readings of the character's function are the archive's own.

The confession's use

The film gives Oerstadt a full confession and then makes it worthless — the case it closes is reopened by its own investigator — and then, in the revised timeline, weaponises it: Carlin confronts him with knowledge from an interrogation that, for Oerstadt, has not happened. The archive files the sequence as the film's neatest cruelty: the bomber is the only character who meets his own future, quoted.

11 / BEHIND THE CHARACTER

Behind the Character

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

A casting note, kept open

One secondary summary consulted in research attributes Oerstadt's role to Val Kilmer, who in fact plays Agent Pryzwarra; the film's cast record assigns Oerstadt to Jim Caviezel, and this page follows the cast record, noting the discrepancy here rather than resolving it silently.

Caviezel's register

Coming off The Passion of the Christ, Caviezel's casting as a soft-spoken domestic terrorist was the production's deliberate inversion — the film uses the audience's association with his serenity as the character's camouflage.

12 / LEGACY & SIGNIFICANCE

Legacy & Significance

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Oerstadt stands as the post-2001 procedural's defining domestic bomber: not a foreign agent or a raving loner, but a screened-out applicant whose file the system wrote correctly and then closed.
