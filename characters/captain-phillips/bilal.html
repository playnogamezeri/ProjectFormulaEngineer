01 / CHARACTER FILE

Overview

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Bilal — Adan Bilal in the full cast record — is the youngest of Muse's four pirates, and the film's measure of what the enterprise costs its most junior participant. He comes aboard the Alabama barefoot, a detail the crew's first officer notices and weaponizes: the broken glass in the engine-room corridor cuts Bilal's feet badly, splitting him from Muse's search and setting up the ambush. In the lifeboat he is the gang's weakest vessel — frightened, in pain, khat-worn — and the recipient of the standoff's strangest transaction: Phillips, the hostage, tending the wounds of the boy sent to rob him. When Phillips attempts escape, it is Bilal who subdues him with a gun butt, on Najee's orders and against whatever the tending built. He dies in the simultaneous sniper fire that ends the standoff, still a teenager, in a sealed boat, on the wrong side of every force in the story.

ROLE

The youngest pirate

Muse's junior crewman; the gang's barefoot boy, whose wound becomes the plot's hinge and the lifeboat's conscience-test.

DEFINING TRAIT

Over his head

Bilal is the only pirate the film shows frightened more than committed — a child labourer in a crime designed by adults offstage.

FATE

Killed in the rescue

Shot by the Navy marksmen in the simultaneous volley that kills all three pirates remaining in the lifeboat.

Place in the story

Bilal is the human cost column of the hijacking's ledger. The film distributes its sympathy carefully — Muse gets ambition, Najee gets menace — and Bilal gets the wound: the injury that splits the gang, the face that tests Phillips's decency, and the death that reminds the audience what the rescue's clean geometry cost inside the boat.

03 / BIOGRAPHY

Biography: Their Story

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL FULL CHARACTER STORY

Bilal is recruited into Muse's gang on the Somali shore — the youngest of the men sent to take a ship for a faction boss's ransom business. In the first approach his skiff is swamped by the Alabama's wake; in the second, he climbs the welded ladder onto the deck barefoot. When the crew vanishes into a killed, darkened ship, Bilal goes down with Muse to search the engine room — and walks into the corridor Shane Murphy has lined with broken glass. His feet are cut badly. Muse searches on alone and is ambushed; Bilal's wound has split the gang at the moment of its greatest strength.

Bilal boards the lifeboat with Muse, Najee, Elmi and the hostage Najee demanded. Across the five days he deteriorates with the rest — the khat wearing off, the fleet assembling, the mother ship lost — carrying an untreated wound and a boy's terror in a sealed capsule. Phillips, the man he helped capture, tends his cut feet: the standoff's one act of care across its lines. When Phillips attempts escape and is recaptured, and later when Najee catches him writing a goodbye letter and beats him, it is Bilal who subdues the captain with his gun butt — the tended boy enforcing the beating. When the tow stops and the marksmen's window opens, Bilal dies in the simultaneous volley with Najee and Elmi.

The biography above is an editorial paraphrase of the documented plot accounts (Wikipedia and the cited sources), not a scene-by-scene transcript.

04 / PERSONALITY & PSYCHOLOGY

Personality & Psychology

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

This section is editorial interpretation, grounded in the character's on-screen actions.

Fear without a role for it

Bilal has no function in the gang's theatre of command — too young to lead, too frightened to menace convincingly. Interpretation: he is what the enterprise actually recruits, beneath its captains and enforcers: disposable boys from a ruined coast, priced at nothing by their own side first.

The flicker of shame

The Galaxy's film page records that Bilal, alone among the pirates, flickers with something like shame — around the tended feet, around the beatings he is made to join. The film never converts the flicker into defection; it simply lets it be seen, which is its own judgment on the gang's adults.

08 / SYMBOLISM & THEMES

Symbolism & Themes

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

This section is editorial interpretation.

The bare feet

Bilal symbolises the enterprise's true economics: a ransom business in millions, staffed by a boy without shoes. The glass corridor is the film's thesis in one wound — the global economy's two shores, meeting at the level of footwear.

The tended wound

His feet are where the film's moral argument is conducted: the hostage's decency and the gang's coercion fight over a teenager's injury.

09 / ANALYSIS & INVESTIGATION

Analysis & Investigation

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

This section is editorial analysis. Documented facts are cited to the sources in Section 13; readings of the character's function are the archive's own.

The hinge is a wound

Bilal's cut feet are the plot's load-bearing injury: they isolate Muse for the ambush, motivate Phillips's tenderness, and give the lifeboat its one cross-lines relationship. The analysis the archive files is that the film routes its machinery through its most vulnerable participant deliberately — the hijacking turns on what happens to the boy, not on anything the captains decide.

Shame without redemption

The film resists converting Bilal's flicker of shame into a redemption arc; he dies enforcing the gang's violence, having been shown kindness by its victim. The refusal is the film's honesty about the enterprise: it consumes its boys faster than decency can reach them.

11 / BEHIND THE CHARACTER

Behind the Character

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Cast from the same search

Abdirahman came through the 2011 Minneapolis casting call that produced Abdi and the other Somali pirate actors — more than 700 participants, the chosen men trained for some six weeks in swimming, fighting, weapons and skiff-handling before the shoot.

A debut at documentary temperature

Like Abdi, Abdirahman had no prior acting career; Greengrass's method — real locations, real sea time, the pirate actors kept apart from Hanks until the boarding — put the debutants' unpolished fear to work as the film's realism.
