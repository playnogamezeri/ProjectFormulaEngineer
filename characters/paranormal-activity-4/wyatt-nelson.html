01 / CHARACTER FILE

Overview

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Wyatt is the film's treasure and its target: a six-year-old of unguarded sweetness, adopted into the Nelsons' warmth, around whom a decade-old design has been quietly arranged. The coven's operation — Katie across the street, Robbie placed in the house, the symbol drawn on his back — exists for him, because Wyatt is Hunter: the baby Katie abducted from his murdered parents in 2006, vanished into the coven's keeping, and surfaced, by routes the film deliberately leaves in the coven's files, as an adopted child in Henderson. The film's most famous beat is his: a small boy arguing with an unseen presence over his own name — "My name is not Hunter!" — resisting, in the only terms he has, the identity the dark is handing him.

ROLE

The prize; the brother

The coven's whole Henderson operation is built around him; the family's whole love is built around him; the film is the two claims meeting.

DEFINING TRAIT

Sweetness under claim

Wyatt is not a creepy child but a loved one — the film stages his corruption as a custody dispute between a family's love and a covenant's paperwork.

FATE

Taken

The final footage leaves him in the coven's hands, standing among the assembled women, as the camera falls — his "readiness," in Katie's phrase, still to come.

Place in the story

Wyatt is where the franchise's mythology becomes a child: the stolen infant of the second film, grown into a six-year-old who loves his family and does not know his name is evidence. His page records both claims on him as the film presents them — the Nelsons' son, documented in football matches and treehouses; and Hunter Rey, documented in a prologue's murders and a boy's argument with the dark — and states, as the film does, that the road between the two is the coven's secret.

Wyatt's identity is the film's central contested document: two names, two families, two ontologies. The archive records the film's discipline — the revelation is delivered as Katie's telling and Wyatt's own overheard resistance, never as a narrator's certificate, and the route by which the stolen Hunter became the adopted Wyatt is one the film withholds entirely. What is documented is the boy in front of the camera: a Nelson, loved and loving, being renamed by the dark.

03 / BIOGRAPHY

Biography: Their Story

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL FULL CHARACTER STORY

The film's prologue establishes the claim: in October 2006, the possessed Katie murdered her sister Kristi and Kristi's husband Daniel and abducted their one-year-old son, Hunter, who was never found. Five years later, in Henderson, Wyatt Nelson is six — the adopted son of Doug and Holly, adored brother of Alex, filmed at his football match in the film's opening footage as an ordinary beloved child.

Robbie's tenancy begins Wyatt's second education. He reports Robbie's imaginary friend, Toby; he is found playing with presences the Kinect can see; Robbie draws the coven's symbol on his back — the mark, the teenagers' research says, that prepares a victim for possession. Katie, across the street, tells him the operation's version of his life: that he and Robbie were both adopted, and that his other family needs him back. Wyatt begins speaking to invisible friends. He is heard arguing with an unseen, unheard presence about his true name, declaring: "My name is not Hunter!" Katie slips into the house at night, possessed, and tells him she will wait until he is "ready."

In the finale's single evening, the coven collects its paperwork: Holly is killed in the house, Ben in Alex's room, Doug across the street. Katie leads Wyatt away to her house. Alex, escaping through a window, finds him at last standing among dozens of women wearing the triangle — the coven assembled to receive him — as Katie knocks her down and the footage ends. Wyatt's readiness, and the rite it feeds, are left where the film leaves them: pending, in the coven's hands.

04 / PERSONALITY & PSYCHOLOGY

Personality & Psychology

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

This section is editorial interpretation, grounded in the character's on-screen actions.

The unguarded child

Wyatt's sweetness is the film's chosen instrument of dread: nothing in him is ominous, and the horror is entirely in what the adults around him — seen and unseen — are arranging. Interpretation: the performance and the script protect his innocence so completely that his corruption reads as kidnapping in slow motion, which, on the film's own prologue, is literally what it is.

The resistance

His one act of self-defence is nominative: "My name is not Hunter!" The film gives a six-year-old no weapons but his name, and lets him fight for the adopted one — the Nelsons' love, asserted against the bloodline's covenant, in a sentence shouted at the dark.

08 / SYMBOLISM & THEMES

Symbolism & Themes

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

This section is editorial interpretation.

The contested name

Wyatt/Hunter is the franchise's whole mythology in one child: a covenant written before his birth, a murder that delivered him, an adoption that renamed him, and a boy who must choose — at six, in the dark, aloud — which name is his. Interpretation: the film's horror is bureaucratic at root: two filings for one child, and the older filing is enforced by demons.

The loved one, not the creepy child

The film refuses the genre's possessed-child shorthand for Wyatt: his sweetness is intact to the last frame. The dread is custodial — the audience watches a happy child be repossessed by paperwork written in his great-grandmother's generation.

09 / ANALYSIS & INVESTIGATION

Analysis & Investigation

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

This section is editorial analysis. Documented facts are cited to the sources in Section 13; readings of the character's function are the archive's own.

Function: the MacGuffin with a bedtime

Wyatt is the plot's object who is also its emotional subject — the rare treasure-figure the audience knows as a person first. Analysis: the screenplay's discipline is to keep him ordinary: every scene of Wyatt being loved is evidence entered for the Nelsons' claim, so that the finale's assembly reads not as a twist's payoff but as a custody hearing decided by force.

The withheld road

How Hunter became Wyatt is the film's deliberate gap — the documented criticism notes the question is never answered, and the archive records it as withheld rather than implied: the coven's files are closed to the audience exactly as they are to Alex. The gap is functional: the operation's invisibility is its plausibility, and a shown mechanism would be a catchable one.
