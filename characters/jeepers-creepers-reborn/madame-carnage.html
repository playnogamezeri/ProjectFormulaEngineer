01 / CHARACTER FILE

Overview

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Madame Carnage is a supporting antagonist of Jeepers Creepers: Reborn (2022): the flamboyant organizer and host of Horror Hound, the first-ever horror festival in rural Louisiana that gathers hundreds of the Creeper legend's fans in one place in the cycle's feeding window. Her set piece is the raffle — a staged giveaway whose prize, a night in an abandoned plantation house converted into an escape room, is in truth the cult's selection mechanism: the "winners" are the offering, delivered to the altar with a camera crew to document it. As the survivors discover, Carnage is a worshipper of the Creeper, one of the congregation (with Lady Manilla and others) that has lured victims to the house for the creature's consumption across the cycle's returns. She matters because she is the reboot's thesis at its loudest: the legend's entertainment industry and the legend's religion are the same organization, and she is its frontwoman — the emcee who turns an audience into an inventory.

ROLE

Supporting antagonist — the cult's emcee

She runs the harvest's public face: the festival gathers the food, the raffle selects the offering, and her microphone blesses both.

DEFINING TRAIT

Showmanship as liturgy

Carnage performs devotion as entertainment — the flamboyance is not a cover for the cult but its modern form: worship, staged as a show, for an audience that has paid to attend its own harvest.

FATE

Unresolved on screen

Exposed by the survivors' discovery but given no on-screen reckoning in the documented accounts: the show, presumably, could go on.

Place in the story

Carnage is the film's center of public gravity — its loudest, most visible figure — positioned exactly where the cult needs its visibility: at the front, in costume, holding the microphone, so that no one looks at the machinery. The film sets her against Lady Manilla as the congregation's two registers: the whisper at the road and the shout at the stage, one harvest.

Carnage's identity is a persona with a priesthood inside it: the archive has only the stage name, the costume and the microphone — which is the character's design. Where Manilla's cult membership hides behind a trade, Carnage's hides behind a performance; the film's point about both women is that the modern world gives worship excellent cover — retail for one, entertainment for the other — and that Carnage's cover is the more honest of the two: she tells the audience exactly what she is, in the name she performs under, and they applaud.

03 / BIOGRAPHY

Biography: Their Story

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL FULL CHARACTER STORY

Madame Carnage's documented story is the festival itself. In 2021, as the Creeper's cycle turns, the first Horror Hound festival convenes in rural Louisiana under her organization — hundreds of horror fans, drawn by the local legend she has built the event around. She hosts it as its flamboyant emcee, the public face of a gathering whose private purpose the survivors later establish: the festival is the congregation's harvest machinery, assembling the creature's food in one place, in the feeding window, under cover of fandom.

Her instrument is the raffle. On stage, Carnage hosts the draw for the event's headline prize — a night in an abandoned plantation house, converted into an escape room, to be documented by a camera crew. Laine and Chase enter, and Laine — already marked at Lady Manilla's shop, already receiving visions of a ritual in that house — wins. The archive records the draw as the cult's selection mechanism wearing a show's costume: whether rigged in the mechanics or guided by the congregation's knowledge of its marked offering, the raffle delivers exactly the victim the ritual requires, applauded by the crowd she was selected from. Carnage's film, thereafter, is conducted in absentia: the house, the altar and the hunt are her liturgy's working parts, and when the survivors piece together the frame — Madame Carnage, Lady Manilla and others worshipping the Creeper, luring victims to the house for its consumption — her festival reclassifies itself entire: not an event beside the legend, but the legend's catering, with an emcee.

The documented record gives her no climax and no punishment: the god is impaled and regenerates, the offering escapes, and the organizer's file closes with the festival's lights still on — the show's hostess unreckoned, her congregation exposed only to three survivors whose account the law will file under the impossible.

04 / PERSONALITY & PSYCHOLOGY

Personality & Psychology

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

This section is editorial interpretation, grounded in the character's on-screen actions.

Flamboyance as doctrine

Carnage does not hide her nature; she amplifies it — name, costume, register — on the theory the film endorses: the best cover for a harvest is a show, and the best show is one the food enjoys. Her personality, as documented, is the performance itself, and the performance is total.

The impresario's patience

Like Manilla, she serves a generational cult, but her timescale is the event's: a festival is a year of preparation for a night of delivery. The archive reads her as the congregation's modernizer — the worshipper who understood that the cycle's logistics had become an entertainment business, and incorporated it.

05 / ABILITIES & SKILLS

Abilities, Skills & Weaknesses

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
SKILL

Crowd command

She convenes, entertains and directs hundreds of fans — the harvest's entire supply chain begins with her ability to fill a field in rural Louisiana on the strength of a legend.

SKILL

The rigged ceremony

The raffle is her craft at its purest: a selection performed as chance, an offering chosen in public, applauded by the pool it was chosen from. As stagecraft and as liturgy, it is flawless.

WEAKNESS

Visibility

She is the cult's most public member — its named host, on its posters — and the survivors' discovery names her first among the worshippers. The show's cover is total except for the detail that everyone knows who ran the show.

WEAKNESS

A servant of an indifferent god

As with the whole congregation, the devotion is undocumented as reciprocated: her empire of entertainment feeds a creature that has never, in the record, acknowledged the catering.

08 / SYMBOLISM & THEMES

Symbolism & Themes

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

This section is editorial interpretation.

The raffle — chance as costume

A rigged draw is the film's most economical symbol: the cult's theology (the god chooses) performed as show business (the winner is drawn), each legitimating the other. As interpretation, Carnage's raffle is where the reboot's two subjects — fandom and worship — are revealed as one mechanism with two vocabularies.

The name — confession as branding

She performs under "Carnage": the film's bluntest symbol, hiding in plain sight. The audience hears a stage name; the congregation hears a job description; both are correct, and the applause is the same.

09 / ANALYSIS & INVESTIGATION

Analysis & Investigation

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

This section is editorial analysis. Documented facts are cited to the sources in Section 13; readings of the character's function are the archive's own.

Narrative function: the harvest's frontwoman

Carnage exists to give the cult a public interface and the festival a face: she converts the reboot's premise (the legend has caterers) into a person the audience can watch doing it. Her function is the inverse of Manilla's — where the shopkeeper works by retrospect, Carnage works by misdirection in real time: the louder the show, the less visible the machinery.

Investigation: the raffle's mechanics

The archive records an open file: the documented accounts establish the raffle as the delivery mechanism and Carnage as its host, but the draw's inner mechanics — mechanically rigged, or merely curated around a pre-marked winner — are not specified. The distinction matters to the cult's portrait (stagecraft versus providence) and the film leaves it, like the crows, in its pile of productive or careless blanks; this file declines to choose.

Unresolved questions the film leaves about her

Her fate, her congregation's future, the festival's second edition: the uncontinued trilogy leaves its emcee mid-season. Whether Horror Hound could convene again after its first edition's body count is the kind of question sequels exist to answer and this one, in the documented record, never got.

Critical reception of the character

Jodie McMullen's Carnage was received inside the film's general verdict — the pans covered the enterprise broadly — with the character's conception (the festival-as-harvest, the emcee-cultist) noted among the reboot's few ideas that critics granted were ideas. The execution's notices belong to the film's file; the design's clarity belongs to hers.

11 / BEHIND THE CHARACTER

Behind the Character

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Performance

Jodie McMullen plays Madame Carnage, the reboot's showwoman-cultist. The role is a public-performance part — stage, microphone, crowd — played at the flamboyant register the design requires: the horror must be able to hide behind her volume.

The character's design

Carnage was written as the cult's modernizer: the worshipper who industrialized the cycle's catering into an entertainment business. Her design is the reboot's thesis in a single casting decision — the legend's most dangerous servant is not a robed figure in a cellar but a promoter with a festival, a raffle and a gift for crowds.

12 / LEGACY & SIGNIFICANCE

Legacy & Significance

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Madame Carnage's legacy is the reboot's most quotable construction: the emcee of the harvest — proof, in the franchise's record, that a recurring horror's most effective modern servant is its entertainment industry. Unsequelled, she stands as the first edition's hostess in perpetuity: the show forever about to go on.

Within this Galaxy, Carnage's file is the archive's record of the cult's public face: her card — "cultist · the organizer" — lists the faith before the job; this page documents the festival, the raffle and the naming that showed they were one engagement.
