Lt. Aldo Raine

A Tennessee lieutenant of Apache descent who raises a unit of eight Jewish-American soldiers behind enemy lines with one standing order — one hundred Nazi scalps apiece — and a policy for survivors: a swastika carved in the forehead, so the uniform can never be taken off. He ends the war in a woods, carving his masterpiece.

01 / CHARACTER FILE

Overview

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Raine — "Aldo the Apache" — is the film's blunt instrument and its final judge. A Tennessee hill-country lieutenant with a rope scar across his throat whose story the film never tells, he recruits eight Jewish-American soldiers for a mission of exemplary terror: dropped into France ahead of the invasion, the Basterds ambush patrols, scalp the dead, and release an occasional survivor to spread the fear — marked, permanently, in the forehead. The campaign makes them the Reich's nightmare ("the Bear Jew" among its monsters), carries them into Operation Kino and the basement tavern's wreckage, and ends with Raine captured by Landa, negotiating at one remove through the OSS telephone — and then, the bargain struck and honoured, appending his own clause to it in the woods.

ROLE

The Basterds' commander

Co-protagonist; author of the scalp campaign and of the film's final act of enforcement.

DEFINING TRAIT

The terms, plainly stated

Pitt plays him in a broad Tennessee drawl and a posture of total, unhurried certainty: Aldo announces his policies — scalps, markings, no exceptions — the way other officers read maps.

FATE

Alive, at his masterpiece

He survives the war he helped end in a cinema, and closes the film carving Landa's forehead: "This just might be my masterpiece."

Place in the story

Aldo is the film's folk-avenger: where Shosanna's revenge is cinema and Landa's loyalty is commerce, his is craft — a hill-country trade of terror, conducted by rules he states in advance and enforces to the letter, including on a colonel with a signed pardon.

03 / BIOGRAPHY

Biography: Their Story

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL FULL CHARACTER STORY

Raine is introduced addressing his recruits in a barn-loft briefing that has become the film's most quoted speech: eight Jewish-American soldiers, one mission, and the debt stated plainly — "Each man under my command owes me one hundred Nazi scalps. And I want my scalps." The Basterds operate in occupied France as the Reich's bogeymen: patrols ambushed, the dead scalped, and occasionally a survivor released to carry the story home — after Aldo has carved a swastika into his forehead, on the stated grounds that a man may take off a uniform but will not take off that. The released Private Butz, interrogated by a furious Hitler, gives the campaign its mythology from the receiving end: the Bear Jew, the scalping Apaches, the commander who cannot be killed.

Recruited into Operation Kino — the British plan to place explosives at the Nation's Pride premiere using the German film star Bridget von Hammersmark as the inside contact — Aldo loses three men in the La Louisiane tavern before he ever reaches it, and arrives to extract the wounded actress from the wreckage at gunpoint, interrogating her story with a finger in her bullet wound. The operation proceeds anyway, in increasingly ragged shape: Aldo attends the premiere disguised as an Italian stuntman, "Enzo Gorlomi," with a Sicilian accent that fools no one it needs to fool — least of all Landa, whose fluent Italian turns the introductions into a slow public dismantling. Captured and taken to Landa, Aldo becomes the OSS's man on the telephone line: the bargain is struck over his head, in his presence, and his men's bombs end the war.

In the woods, Landa surrenders to him in the expectation of paperwork and processing. Aldo shoots the radio operator, and then — the pardon, the pension, the Medal of Honor all duly negotiated — does the one thing his whole campaign has promised: he takes out his knife and carves the swastika into Hans Landa's forehead, deep and permanent, while Utivich holds him. "You know somethin', Utivich? I think this just might be my masterpiece." The film ends on the carving: the Apache's policy, enforced on the war's best negotiator, in the war's last hour.

04 / PERSONALITY & PSYCHOLOGY

Personality & Psychology

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

This section is editorial interpretation, grounded in the character's on-screen actions.

Policy as character

Interpretation: Aldo is the rare avenger who publishes his terms in advance — scalps owed, survivors marked, no exceptions negotiated. His horror for the Reich is not unpredictability but the opposite: he does exactly what he said he would do.

The folk register

His drawl, his humour and his hill-country certainties are played straight: the film gives its most theatrical violence to its least theatrical man, a commander whose idea of an atrocity exhibition is a bookkeeping exercise.

The exception he keeps

The Landa carving is not a breach of his word but of everyone else's: the bargain was the OSS's to make, and Aldo never signed it. His consistency is total — which is precisely what the woods are for.

Eight men made into an army's nightmare: ambush, exemplary terror and information warfare conducted through released, marked survivors.

The finger in the wound

Von Hammersmark's cover story is tested by direct application: crude, effective, and entirely characteristic.

COVER, ATTEMPTED

"Enzo Gorlomi"

His Italian disguise at the premiere is the unit's worst performance — delivered with total commitment and a Sicilian accent that convinces nobody, least of all the polyglot across the introductions.

WEAKNESS

The letter of his own law

His rules bind him as they bind others: a bargain made by his superiors in his presence still leaves his own clause outstanding, and he will pay any diplomatic price to collect it.

08 / SYMBOLISM & THEMES

Symbolism & Themes

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

This section is editorial interpretation.

The carved forehead

Interpretation: Aldo's marking is the film's moral signature — a refusal of the postwar amnesia in which uniforms come off and men become neighbours again. His whole campaign is an argument that some service should be permanent, and the ending applies it to the highest-ranking man he ever catches.

The throat scar

Interpretation: unexplained by design — the commander wears a survived atrocity at his collar the way his marked prisoners will wear theirs, a reminder that the carver's authority is itself a scar that healed into a policy.

09 / ANALYSIS & INVESTIGATION

Analysis & Investigation

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

This section is editorial analysis. Documented facts are cited to the sources in Section 13; readings of the character's function are the archive's own.

Function: the enforcement clause

Every other party in the film negotiates — Landa sells, the OSS buys, Shosanna schedules, the British plan. Aldo alone enforces. The archive records his role in the ending as structural: the film gives the war's last word not to the bargain that ended it but to the man who refuses to let the bargain launder its beneficiary — the masterpiece line is spoken over an act of archival correction, the permanent record made in skin because paper, in this film, is exactly what cannot be trusted.

11 / BEHIND THE CHARACTER

Behind the Character

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Brad Pitt

Pitt headlines the film's ensemble and plays Raine as a deliberate throwback — a John Wayne register filtered through hill-country comedy, drawl first. The throat scar was the production's unexplained addition, retained precisely because the film never accounts for it. Raine's stated ancestry — part Apache, and a claimed descent from the mountain man Jim Bridger in the film's briefing materials — frames the character as American frontier mythology inserted into the European war, which the performance leans on throughout.

12 / LEGACY & SIGNIFICANCE

Legacy & Significance

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Aldo Raine is the film's folk hero and its most quoted figure — "bonafide" vernacular and all — and the carving of Landa remains the decade's most debated act of screen justice. In the Galaxy's records his page stands as the campaign's ledger: the terms stated, the scalps collected, the survivors marked, and the final account settled personally, in the woods, outside the paperwork.
