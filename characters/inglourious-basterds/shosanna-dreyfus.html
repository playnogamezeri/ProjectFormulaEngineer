Shosanna Dreyfus

A Jewish teenager who survives the massacre of her family under a farmhouse floor and rebuilds herself, under a false name, as a Paris cinema proprietor — until the Reich, in the person of its most lovesick sniper, delivers its entire leadership into her auditorium and her hands close around the matches.

01 / CHARACTER FILE

Overview

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Shosanna's story is the film's revenge plot in its pure form: no commanders, no orders, no nation — a survivor, a cinema, and three hundred and fifty reels of nitrate film. She escapes the LaPadite floorboards as the only living Dreyfus, resurfaces in Paris as "Emmanuelle Mimieux," proprietor of the Le Gamaar cinema, and wages her private war in silence until Fredrick Zoller — the Reich's sniper-hero, infatuated with her — persuades Goebbels to move the premiere of his propaganda film into her auditorium, with Hitler attending. Her plan, made with her projectionist and lover Marcel, is to lock the doors, pile the nitrate behind the screen, and give the Reich a premiere in her family's honour — with her own face, filmed for the occasion, delivering the introduction from the screen as it burns.

ROLE

The avenger with a cinema

Co-protagonist; the massacre's sole survivor, the premiere plot's author, and the fire's hostess.

DEFINING TRAIT

Patience as a weapon

Laurent plays her in a register of held stillness — years of false name and swallowed rage, spent in a single night. Her revenge is not an outburst; it is a programme, scheduled like a screening.

FATE

Dead in her own booth

Shot by Zoller in the projection booth minutes before her film reaches the screen; her recorded face delivers her message to the burning auditorium without her.

Place in the story

Shosanna is the film's moral centre and its thesis about cinema itself: the medium the Reich uses for propaganda is the medium she uses for execution — a face on a screen, a hall of locked doors, an audience that cannot leave the show. Her page is the record of the survivor as auteur.

03 / BIOGRAPHY

Biography: Their Story

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL FULL CHARACTER STORY

In 1941 Shosanna lies under the LaPadite floorboards with her family while Landa, above them, conducts his courteous interrogation in a language chosen so they will not understand their own betrayal. The farmer nods; the soldiers fire through the floor; Shosanna alone gets out through the cellar and runs, blood-covered, across the fields — with Landa's pistol tracking her and then, inexplicably, lowering, and his voice calling a cheerful farewell after her.

Paris, 1944: she is Emmanuelle Mimieux, and the Le Gamaar cinema is hers — inherited, staffed by her lover Marcel, the projectionist, and run as a going concern in the occupied city. Into it walks Fredrick Zoller, the sniper whose three-day stand in a Sicilian bell tower has made him the Reich's hero and the star of Goebbels's film of his own exploits, Nation's Pride. His infatuation is immediate and, to Shosanna, unendurable and then — as he persuades Goebbels to move the premiere to her cinema, with the entire leadership attending — suddenly legible as an instrument. She and Marcel make their plan: her stock of nitrate prints, three hundred and fifty reels of it, piled behind the screen; the doors locked; the fire set at the film's climax; and a short film of her own, spliced into the print, in which she tells the audience who she is and what is about to happen to them.

On the night, she is interviewed by Landa over strudel and survives it; she dresses in red; she runs her cinema. In the projection booth Zoller forces his way in, pressing his courtship at the worst hour of her life, and when he turns his back she shoots him — and, bending over him as he lies apparently dying, is shot by him in return. They die together in the booth while her film plays on: her giant face appears on the screen, announces itself to the leadership of the Reich, and laughs as Marcel's fire takes the hall and the Basterds' bombs finish it. The avenger does not live to see her premiere. The premiere does not require her to.

04 / PERSONALITY & PSYCHOLOGY

Personality & Psychology

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

This section is editorial interpretation, grounded in the character's on-screen actions.

The survivor's discipline

Interpretation: Shosanna's false life is maintained without a crack for years — name, papers, profession, composure under Landa's own eyes. The film treats her revenge not as a breakdown of that discipline but as its final product.

Grief, scheduled

She does not rage; she programmes. The spliced film, the locked doors, the timing to the climax — her mourning has a projectionist's precision, which is what makes it terrifying and what makes it hers.

The fatal kindness

Her one hesitation — bending over the fallen Zoller — is the booth's tragedy in miniature: the girl who survived by running stops, once, for a dying boy who loves her, and it costs her everything except the plan.

08 / SYMBOLISM & THEMES

Symbolism & Themes

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

This section is editorial interpretation.

The face on the screen

Interpretation: Shosanna's giant projected face, laughing as the hall burns, is the film's thesis in one image — cinema as the weapon of the powerless, propaganda's machinery reversed onto its owners.

The red dress

Interpretation: she dresses for her premiere like a star attending it — because she is: the film gives its avenger the full glamour of the industry that is about to be her weapon, and the colour is not subtle, and is not meant to be.

09 / ANALYSIS & INVESTIGATION

Analysis & Investigation

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

This section is editorial analysis. Documented facts are cited to the sources in Section 13; readings of the character's function are the archive's own.

Function: the auteur of the ending

The Basterds' plot requires Landa's permission to succeed; Shosanna's requires only her own. The archive records the asymmetry as the film's judgment: the soldiers are a subplot in the survivor's film, not the reverse — it is her cinema, her stock, her face and her fire that end the war in this history, with the Basterds' bombs as her supporting feature. Her death in the booth, minutes early, is the design's severity: the auteur does not attend her premiere, because the film — hers — no longer needs her alive to play.

11 / BEHIND THE CHARACTER

Behind the Character

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Mélanie Laurent

Laurent was cast as Shosanna after Tarantino sought a French actress who could carry the film's emotional centre largely in stillness; the strudel interview and the booth sequence are staged as her twin summits — composure and its cost. The character's cinema, the Le Gamaar, and her nitrate stock are the production's central conceit: the film-within-the-film, Nation's Pride, was shot as a complete propaganda pastiche for the premiere sequence.

12 / LEGACY & SIGNIFICANCE

Legacy & Significance

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Shosanna is the film's enduring centre — the survivor as auteur, and the face on the burning screen one of the century's defining cinema images. In the Galaxy's records her page stands as the revenge plot perfected: no army, no orders, one cinema, one night, and a message spliced into history by its author.
