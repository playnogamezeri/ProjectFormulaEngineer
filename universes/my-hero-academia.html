EXPANDED RESEARCH / SERIES DOSSIER

World, mechanics and critical analysis

Kōhei Horikoshi’s superhero series follows Izuku Midoriya, a boy born without a Quirk in a society where most people possess superhuman abilities. After inheriting One For All from All Might, he enters U.A. High School and trains to become a professional hero. The story grows from a school-based training narrative into a social crisis about hero institutions, public trust, discrimination and the consequences of treating heroism as a profession.

World structure and institutions

The setting is a modern society transformed by the widespread emergence of Quirks. Professional heroes are licensed public figures, agencies operate as organizations, and schools train future responders. The Hero Public Safety Commission, police, media and villain networks shape how society defines safety. The collapse of public confidence reveals structural weaknesses beneath the heroic image.

Systems, rules and limitations

Quirks are inherited or developed abilities with individual biological limits and side effects. One For All is a transferable stockpiling Quirk with accumulated power and inherited vestiges; All For One can take and redistribute Quirks. Support equipment, awakening, mutations and Quirk singularity theories add complexity. A Quirk’s usefulness depends on training, compatibility, environment and cost, not just destructive scale.

Factions and organizational dynamics

U.A. and other hero schools, professional agencies, the Hero Public Safety Commission, the League of Villains and the Paranormal Liberation Front are distinct structures. The League’s transformation into a larger movement reflects social grievances and leadership manipulation. Class 1-A is a central peer group, but the story also examines teachers, sidekicks, civilians and public institutions.

Themes and adaptation continuity

My Hero Academia explores heroism, social responsibility, prejudice, inherited power, mentorship, public image and the ethics of rescue. It asks whether a society can rely on exceptional individuals without addressing the conditions that produce harm.
