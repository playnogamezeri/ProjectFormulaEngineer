01 / FILM IDENTITY

Overview

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Superbad is a 2007 American coming-of-age teen comedy directed by Greg Mottola, written by Seth Rogen and Evan Goldberg — who began the script at thirteen and finished it out of their own Grade 12 experience at Vancouver's Point Grey Secondary School, naming the heroes after themselves — and produced by Judd Apatow. It grossed $170.8 million on a budget of $17.5–20 million and is now routinely filed among the greatest comedies ever made. Seth (Jonah Hill) and Evan (Michael Cera) are codependent best friends in the last weeks of high school, facing the fact neither will say plainly: Evan is going to Dartmouth, Seth is not, and the friendship — the whole architecture of both their lives since childhood — has an expiration date neither chose. When Jules (Emma Stone, in her film debut) invites Seth to her graduation party and mentions the drinks situation, Seth volunteers the duo as suppliers — a quest whose real cargo, the film is always honest about, is not alcohol but a theory: that one legendary night can bank enough glory to carry them into the separated future, and get them laid before the bell. The supply chain is their friend Fogell (Christopher Mintz-Plasse, also debuting) and his new fake ID, a Hawaiian license bearing a single name — "McLovin" — and an age of 25. The night then splits into the film's three braided odysseys: Fogell, knocked flat by a liquor-store robber mid-purchase, is adopted by two patrol officers (Bill Hader and Rogen himself) whose idea of policing is a joyride — bar stops, a burning car, target practice on their own cruiser — in the film's portrait of adult authority as two older boys who never got the separation memo; Seth and Evan, booze-less, ricochet through the suburbs (a period-blood stain mistaken for evidence of manhood, a hit-and-run driver who owes them, an adults' party of terrifying cocaine-era decadence, Seth hit by two separate cars) toward Jules's house with a laundry-detergent bottle of stolen alcohol; and underneath every set piece, the real plot keeps surfacing in the quiet scenes — two boys in sleeping bags, on curbs, on a hillside, rehearsing a goodbye they can only approach drunk, sideways, and in the film's most famous confession: Seth, wasted, telling Evan he loves him, and Evan — the film's whole heart in one exchange — saying it back like it's nothing, because to him it is. The party delivers its verdicts with a dramatist's fairness: Jules doesn't drink and never needed the booze; Becca is too drunk for Evan's conscience to allow what he came for; and the morning after, in a mall, the duo separates in the film's final composition — Seth with Jules, Evan with Becca, an escalator between the two halves of the greatest friendship in teen film — upward, apart, and okay.

Place in the culture

The film is the Apatow era's purest artifact and the teen comedy's modern summit: the movie that proved the genre's engine was never sex but separation — two friends, one summer, an expiration date — and that the filthiest dialogue in mainstream comedy could carry its most tender thesis. "McLovin" entered the permanent lexicon (the ID became a genuine cultural document, name-checked by real police and printed on real merchandise for two decades), and the film launched or consolidated a generation: Hill, Cera, Stone, Mintz-Plasse, Hader. Its influence runs through every coming-of-age comedy since that knows the party is a pretext and the friendship is the plot.

The script two teenagers wrote

Rogen and Goldberg began the screenplay at thirteen, out of their own Vancouver adolescence, and developed it for years — Rogen was originally to play Seth himself, until age and build reassigned him to Officer Michaels, a recasting the archive files as the film's happiest accident (the author inside the film, playing the adult who refuses to grow up, opposite Hader). Mottola, an Apatow veteran from Undeclared, directed with a loose hand that let the young cast's timing govern; Mintz-Plasse, seventeen and in his debut, was famously required to have his mother present for his character's big scene. The production was modest — under $20 million, suburban locations, a soundtrack of deep-cut funk — and the film premiered at Grauman's Chinese Theatre on August 13, 2007 before opening August 17 to $33 million and the kind of word of mouth that made it the summer's sleeper: $170.8 million worldwide, 88% on Rotten Tomatoes, and a permanent place in the genre's canon. Its semi-autobiographical furniture is documented: Fogell was a real friend; Steven Glanzberg, the film's legendary lunchroom loner, was a real classmate.

03 / NARRATIVE SUMMARY

Story Synopsis

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL FULL STORY SUMMARY

The film's first scene is its whole plot in a phone call: Seth and Evan, driving to school, inventorying their last weeks — the party opportunities, the girls, the unspoken third item (Dartmouth, and the roommate form Evan has filled in with Fogell's name instead of Seth's) hanging between them unmentioned. Home economics supplies the detonator: Jules, paired with Seth, invites him to her party and asks if he can get alcohol — Seth, spending money she gives him before he has any plan at all, promises everything. Fogell's fake ID is the plan: a Hawaiian license, one name, "McLovin," twenty-five years old — Seth reads it as a death sentence, Evan as their only hope, and the film files both readings as correct. The liquor store is the night's hinge: the clerk, magnificently, accepts the ID — and a robber's fist, mid-transaction, knocks Fogell out and the plan apart, delivering Fogell instead into the custody of Officers Slater and Michaels, who adopt him on the spot as their evening's entertainment and little brother: the ride-along that becomes a joyride (a bar, a shooting of a stop sign, the burning of their own cruiser to destroy the evidence of their negligence) — the film's B-plot and its thesis about adulthood: the grown-ups are just seniors who got issued a car and a gun.

Seth and Evan's odyssey is the suburbs as an obstacle course of humiliations, each one a small filing of the night's real subject: the period stain on Seth's pants at a dance, misread by a group of men as a trophy and celebrated — the film's most excruciating set piece, and its most precise (manhood, to this world, is a stain you didn't earn); the hit-and-run that leaves Seth owed by a driver who promises liquor from an adults' party, where the duo is nearly absorbed into a cocaine-and-violence bacchanal and escapes with a detergent bottle of stolen booze; Seth hit by a second car — Slater and Michaels's cruiser, McLovin in the back seat, the night's two odysseys colliding at an intersection in the film's neatest piece of construction. The fight that follows, on a suburban lawn, is the film's emotional climax disguised as its ugliest scene: the Dartmouth secret, the roommate form, the whole expiration date, shouted at last — and then the reconciliation, conducted in sleeping bags on a friend's floor, drunk and direct in the way the whole film has been promising: the love confessed, reciprocated, and filed as the night's only successful delivery. The party itself is the morning-after exam the film grades gently: Jules, sober by choice, likes Seth anyway and never needed the cargo; Becca, drunk past consent, is tucked in by an Evan whose decency outranks his quest — the film's verdict that its heroes were always better than their plan. The mall coda is the genre's most famous farewell: Jules and Becca, met by chance; the pairs re-sorting themselves; Seth and Evan's last look across an escalator's divide — the friendship not ended but re-founded, at adult scale, on the far side of the divide it spent one night refusing to see.

The full narrative summary is an editorial paraphrase, not dialogue or a scene-by-scene transcript.

THE LOUD HALF

Seth

Jonah Hill. The duo's engine and id: a profane, terrified romantic whose entire graduation strategy — the booze, the party, the girl — is a fortification against the one fact he cannot say (his best friend is leaving and he is not), and whose vulgarity the film consistently files as a dialect of fear. Hill plays him at a pitch of desperate velocity that keeps breaking, in the quiet scenes, into the most exposed acting in the genre (the sleeping-bag confession is performed like a man defusing himself), and Seth's function is the story's portrait of the friend who loves louder because he is losing: his quest fails on every term he set it, and succeeds on the only one that mattered — he is liked, sober, by the girl, and loved, out loud, by the friend. The archive files him as the decade's definitive teen-comedy lead: a loudmouth whose whole film is learning the volume of a whisper.

THE QUIET HALF

Evan

Michael Cera. The duo's conscience and escape velocity: Dartmouth-bound, Becca-fixated, and carrying the separation's paperwork (the roommate form, filled with Fogell's name) as a guilt he manages by omission until a lawn fight files it in the open. Cera plays him in the register that made him the decade's emblem of adolescent decency — the stammer as a moral instrument — and Evan's function is the story's portrait of the leaver's burden: he is the one going somewhere, which the film files as the harder guilt, and his two great acts are refusals (the drunk Becca, tucked in instead of taken; the secret, finally confessed) that define the duo's ethics. The archive files him as the decade's most complete quiet half in a buddy film: a boy whose superpower is the sentence "I can't," deployed at exactly the moments his quest demanded he not.

McLOVIN

Fogell

Christopher Mintz-Plasse. The duo's friend and the night's secret protagonist: a dweeb whose fake ID — one name, Hawaii, twenty-five — becomes a passport into the film's B-plot, where two cops adopt him and the evening's only unqualified triumph is his (the ID works; the cops adore him; he fires a police weapon at a burning cruiser and gets the girl-adjacent glory the heroes quest for). Mintz-Plasse, seventeen and debuting, plays him with a nasal grandeur that made the character immortal, and Fogell's function is the story's portrait of the third friend as the control group: unburdened by the duo's codependence, he has the night they were trying to have — the film's proof that the quest was never about the booze. The archive files him as the decade's most consequential debut in comedy: a McLovin whose fake name became more real than most actors' real ones.

THE HOSTESS

Jules

Emma Stone. Seth's crush and the party's host — and the film's quiet demolition of its own premise: she does not drink, never needed the delivery, likes Seth for the frantic sincerity the quest kept interrupting, and ends the film as the mall-coda's agent of the duo's gentle separation. Stone, in her film debut, plays her with a dry, amused intelligence that signals the entire career to come (the film's adults-in-waiting are all in her scenes), and Jules's function is the story's portrait of the prize as a person: the quest's cargo was a theory about girls, and the girl — sober, funny, unimpressed by cargo — refutes it by existing. The archive files her as the decade's most auspicious debut in a teen film: a hostess whose party's real function was to grade the heroes' homework and pass them anyway.

THE CRUSH

Becca

Martha MacIsaac. Evan's longtime infatuation — the girl of the specific vodka request, whose party night runs on a parallel track of enthusiasm (she likes Evan back, entirely, which the duo's intelligence network never priced in) and whose drunkenness at the critical hour converts Evan's quest into the film's ethical set piece. MacIsaac plays her with a sweetness that makes the bedroom scene's stakes legible in both directions, and Becca's function is the story's portrait of desire's consent clause: she is the test the film sets its quiet hero — the offered thing, at the offered moment, in the wrong condition — and his refusal is the duo's diploma. The archive files her as the decade's most consequential crush in teen comedy: a girl whose blackout is the hero's graduation exam, and whose mall morning-after files the pass as mutual.

THE LAW, OFF DUTY

Officer Slater

Bill Hader. One half of the night's patrol — a cop whose professional formation stopped at the same graduation the heroes are facing, and who adopts McLovin as the little brother the job never issued him: the joyride, the bar, the cruiser bonfire are his mentorship program. Hader plays him with an SNL virtuoso's gleeful commitment (every line a small ceremony of irresponsibility), and Slater's function is the story's portrait of authority as arrested development with a badge: the film's adults are not a warning but a mirror — this is Seth in ten years, if the separation goes badly. The archive files him as the decade's funniest policeman in film: an officer whose report on the night's events would be the film's only work of fiction more brazen than the ID.

THE LAW, AUTHOR

Officer Michaels

Seth Rogen. Slater's partner — the film's co-author, inside his own script, playing the grown version of the boy he wrote: a cop whose entire shift is a senior-year night with a municipal vehicle, and whose affection for McLovin is the film's strangest tenderness (the farewell at the party, where he delivers Fogell to his destiny like a father at a dorm). Rogen plays him with a slouching joy that never winks at the construction, and Michaels's function is the story's portrait of the author as his own cautionary tale: the man who wrote Seth at thirteen, playing at twenty-five the adult Seth becomes if the escalator never comes. The archive files him as the decade's most meta supporting performance in comedy: a screenwriter hiding inside his film as its worst-case scenario.

THE EXCHANGE STUDENT

Nicola

Aviva Baumann. The party's glamorous older presence — the girl Fogell's odyssey is ultimately aimed at, in the film's distribution of rewards: while the duo's quest collapses into ethics and confession, McLovin's night ends in the company the whole expedition was mounted to reach. Baumann plays her in a few scenes as the party's horizon of adult sophistication, and Nicola's function is the story's portrait of the reward's randomness: the night pays its prizes by its own inscrutable allocation — to the friend with the fake name, not the heroes with the plan — which the film files as the truest thing it knows about senior spring. The archive files her as the decade's most efficient minor prize in teen film: a horizon Fogell reaches by being adopted, not by striving.

THE DRIVER

Francis

Joe Lo Truglio. The hit-and-run driver who flattens Seth outside the liquor store and, in lieu of a police report, owes the duo a debt — the IOU that routes them to the adults' party and the detergent-bottle heist. Lo Truglio plays him with a deadbeat's oily contrition, and Francis's function is the story's portrait of the night's economy: every transaction in the film is a debt or a favor (Jules's money, Fogell's ID, the officers' cruiser) — Francis is simply the only debtor honest about the exchange rate, which is one near-death experience per party invitation. The archive files him as the decade's most consequential minor driver in comedy: a man whose bumper writes the plot's middle act.

THE LONER

Steven Glanzberg

The film's legendary background figure: the classmate who eats lunch alone, every day, by a filing the whole school accepts and the film — uniquely — bothers to name. Glanzberg is based on Rogen and Goldberg's real Point Grey classmate, and his single trait is the film's conscience in miniature: in a story about the terror of ending up alone, he is the boy already there, unrescued, unmocked by the script itself (the joke is the school's filing, not the boy), eating his lunch in the film's most quietly radical shot. His function is the story's portrait of the fear beneath the farce: every hero in the film is one graduation from Glanzberg's table, and they know it. The archive files him as the decade's most humane minor character in teen comedy: a loner the authors remembered, by name, for twenty years, and paid the debt in film.

THE PARTY'S OTHER HOST

Jesse

The adult-party host whose house is the night's circle of hell: a grown man's bacchanal of cocaine, menace and a brawl the duo escapes with a detergent bottle — the film's one vision of the adult world as genuinely frightening rather than merely arrested. Jesse's function is the story's portrait of the quest's terminus if the cargo had mattered: this is the party the boys imagine they want, staffed by the men who never left it — the film files the scene as the anti-graduation, and the duo's flight from it as their first adult decision. The archive files him as the decade's most efficient minor host in comedy: a man whose living room is the strongest anti-drug argument the genre ever staged, played entirely for dread.

THE MOTHERS

The Parents

The film's off-screen and half-glimpsed adults: Seth's and Evan's mothers, the morning's phone calls, the homes the duo returns to between sorties — present mostly as the architecture the night is staged against (curfews unmentioned, dinners skipped, the suburban houses whose lawns host the fights). Their function is the story's portrait of the era's parenting as the film remembers it: a perimeter, not a presence — the 1990s suburban arrangement in which the children's civilization (the parties, the IDs, the odysseys) runs in the gaps of the adults' attention, and the film's nostalgia is partly for the gaps. The archive files them as the decade's most honestly absent parents in teen film: a generation raising itself in the suburbs their parents built, one unmonitored night at a time.

The suburbs — the odyssey's terrain

The film's unnamed suburban sprawl (shot in Los Angeles, set in the writers' remembered Vancouver): liquor stores, lawns, house parties, a mall — a landscape engineered for cars the heroes don't have, across distances the night keeps re-measuring. The suburbs' function is the story's portrait of adolescence's geography: a civilization built at adult scale, in which the young are pedestrians in every sense — dependent on drivers, IDs, older siblings and luck — and the odyssey form is simply what a Friday night looks like without a license. The mall — the escalator

The mall's function is the story's portrait of the separation as an ordinary place: no battlefield, no airport — the diaspora begins in a food court's sightline, on a school night, in the most banal architecture in America, which is why it breaks the audience's heart. The archive files the escalator as the decade's most famous piece of mall furniture in film: a machine for carrying best friends apart, at walking pace, in public.

08 / THE FAKE ID, THE BOOZE RUN & THE LAST WEEKS

The Fake ID, the Booze Run & the Last Weeks

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

The fake ID — the document that works

The film's central device: a Hawaiian license with one name and no plausibility, which succeeds at every checkpoint the night presents (the clerk rings it up; the cops adopt its bearer) — the film's running proof that the adult world's security is a confidence ritual. The ID's function is the story's portrait of identity as a performed filing: McLovin is not a disguise but a draft of Fogell's future self (twenty-five, unbothered, from somewhere else), and the night ratifies the draft. The archive files the fake ID as the decade's most influential fictional document: a license that licensed a generation's understanding of how adulthood is actually verified — by nerve.

The booze run — the MacGuffin confessed

The quest's cargo: Jules's money, a specific vodka for Becca, a detergent bottle of stolen liquor as the night's final yield — a supply chain the film keeps auditing and finding irrelevant, since the hostess doesn't drink and the crush can't. The run's function is the story's portrait of the errand as the friendship's last joint venture: the cargo is the excuse for the duo to spend its final weeks as a team with a mission, and the film files every spill and substitution as the mission's mercy — had it been easy, they'd have had nothing to do together but say goodbye. The archive files the booze run as the decade's most honest MacGuffin in comedy: a delivery that fails upward into a farewell tour.

The last weeks — the expiration date

The film's clock: graduation as a scheduled diaspora — Dartmouth for Evan, Fogell as his roommate, Seth unassigned — ticking under every scene and never once announced by an adult. The weeks' function is the story's portrait of adolescence's unique temporality: the first ending in a life that arrives with a printed date, known to everyone, discussable by no one — which is why the film's heroes discuss it exactly twice (a lawn, a sleeping bag) and spend the rest of the runtime outrunning it at party tempo. The archive files the last weeks as the decade's most consequential calendar in teen film: a deadline that turns a booze run into a state funeral for a friendship.

The period stain — manhood's false positive

The film's most excruciating device: a girl's period blood on Seth's pants at a dance, misread by a circle of men as a conquest's evidence and celebrated — the stain as a medal its bearer knows he didn't earn, in a scene the film plays as the whole gender's filing system exposed. The stain's function is the story's portrait of masculinity as a documentation problem: the men do not ask what happened; they read the evidence as their folklore requires — and Seth's silence (accepting the honor) is the duo's ethics at their lowest ebb, the exact debt Evan's bedroom refusal later repays. The archive files the stain as the decade's most brutally efficient set piece in teen comedy: a false positive that grades the whole culture's lab work.

The party is a pretext — separation is the plot

The film's governing theme is the one its heroes refuse to discuss at conversational volume: that the booze, the girls and the legend are a joint venture mounted to avoid the only real event of the spring, which is the friendship's scheduled dissolution. The theme is worked out in the film's structure (every set piece is an errand; every quiet scene is the truth) until the audience holds the film's finding entire: adolescent boys do not say goodbye; they build a quest elaborate enough to fill the goodbye's hours, and say it — if they are lucky, and drunk, and in sleeping bags — sideways. Its proposition reaches every graduate in the audience, of every age, facing a diaspora (the job change, the move, the marriage that re-sorts the friendships): the quest you are mounting with your people this season deserves one audit — is it a plan, or is it a farewell tour that hasn't admitted its name? The reach is the story's question to every Seth with a cargo to deliver: what would you and your Evan do with the night if you spent it on the subject instead of the errand?

Decency is the diploma — the refusals grade the heroes

The film's second theme is its ethical architecture: that both heroes are finally examined not on what they obtain but on what they decline — Evan's refusal of the incapacitated girl he has wanted all year; Seth's acceptance, at the end, of a sober girl's unassisted liking — and that the film's filthiest dialogue exists to make the decency legible as a choice rather than a default. The theme is worked out in the film's parallel set pieces (the stain accepted as a false medal — the low ebb; the bedroom refusal — the summit) until the audience holds the film's grading rubric: character is what you do with the offered thing in the wrong condition. Its proposition reaches every person in the audience whose quest has ever delivered the prize compromised: the tuck-in is the triumph; the legend you decline to take is the one that makes you, in the mall the next morning, able to meet her eyes. The reach is the story's question to every Evan at a bedroom door: which story do you want to be able to tell at the reunion — the one about the night, or the one about the morning?

The adults are a warning with a badge — arrested development

The film's third theme is its patrol: that the grown-up world, as the night reveals it, is not a graduation but a fork — Slater and Michaels (the duo unseparated, issued a cruiser) against Jules's sobriety and the girls' general adulthood — and that the heroes' real curriculum is choosing which adult to become. The theme is worked out in the film's double portrait of authority (the cops' joyride against the dean-less, parent-perimetered suburb) until the audience holds the film's developmental doctrine: nobody issues adulthood; it is assumed, usually in a single declined or accepted moment, and the badge is not evidence. Its proposition reaches every young person in the audience pricing the adults around them: the question is not how old the officers are but how they spend the shift. The reach is the story's question to every McLovin in a cruiser's back seat: the men up front are offering you their whole future as a good time — what, exactly, is your escalator?

Written at thirteen — authenticity as an aesthetic

The film's fourth theme is its own authorship: that a script begun by the age group it depicts, and finished by the same writers a decade later with the feelings intact, carries a documentary authority no adult's reconstruction can fake — the dialogue's profanity is a period dialect, and the tenderness is unmediated because the authors never learned to be embarrassed by it. 11 / FULL SPOILER CONCLUSION

Ending Explained

REVEAL THE ENDING

The booze quest fails and proves unnecessary: Jules doesn't drink and likes Seth anyway; Evan refuses to sleep with the very drunk Becca and tucks her in. After Seth and Evan confess their love for each other in sleeping bags, the morning-after mall meeting re-sorts the pairs — Seth with Jules, Evan with Becca — and the two friends share a last look as an escalator carries them apart.

What the ending establishes

  • The mall escalator files the film's thesis as pure cinema: the friendship is not ended but re-founded — the divide is real, mechanical and survivable, and the last look is an acknowledgment, not a wound.
  • Jules's sobriety files the quest's cargo as the film's final joke on its heroes: the delivery was never required; the deliverymen were the point — she accepts the boy, not the booze.
  • Evan's refusal files the duo's diploma: the quest's stated prize, declined on grounds of the prize's condition, is the film's definition of the manhood the stain counterfeited.
  • McLovin's triumph files the night's allocation as gloriously unfair: the third friend, unburdened by a codependence to mourn, has the legendary night — the film's control experiment, concluding that the mourning was the tax on the love.
  • The sleeping-bag confession files the cargo delivered: the one thing the night was mounted to transport — the sentence "I love you" between two boys — arrives, by hand, in the film's quietest scene.

What remains uncertain

Whether Seth will thrive unassigned, whether the duo's adult friendship will hold its escalator distance, and whether Dartmouth's roommate arrangement survives Fogell — all stand open at the film's close (no sequel was made; the authors have discussed and declined one). Within this film, the archive files the uncertainty as the story's own last word on its theme: the escalator only goes one flight. Everything after that is stairs the friends will have to climb on purpose — which is the film's definition of adulthood, and its benediction.

12 / PURPOSES OF THE STORY

Moral of The Story

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Superbad uses a fake ID, a detergent bottle and a pair of sleeping bags to examine separation, decency and the fork of adulthood: two friends mounting a legendary quest to avoid a scheduled goodbye; a third friend whose fake name has the night they wanted; and two cops who are the future if the goodbye is never faced. Through the liquor store, the lawn fight and the mall escalator, the film argues that the quest is the farewell tour in disguise; that character is graded on refusals, not acquisitions; and that the bravest sentence in a young man's vocabulary is the one his whole culture teaches him to deliver only drunk, sideways and as a joke — which the film delivers straight.

Why tell this story?

Because Rogen and Goldberg wrote down, at thirteen, the thing every adult reconstruction gets wrong — that the last spring of a boyhood friendship feels like an emergency and is one — and Mottola and Apatow had the discipline to film the document rather than improve it. The film tells it as a booze run because that is what the farewell actually looked like from inside it: nobody at seventeen books a goodbye dinner; they book a mission, and the mission's failure is the goodbye's opportunity. The result is the genre's summit, the archive files: a comedy so exact about its moment that it became the moment's permanent record — McLovin's ID framed, forever, next to Glanzberg's lunch tray.

1. Name the farewell tour

The booze run is the film's first counsel, for every person mounting an elaborate joint venture with people they are about to lose — the last trip, the big project, the legendary night: audit the cargo. The counsel is a pretext doctrine with the party as its evidence: if the mission's success would leave you with nothing to say to each other, the mission was the saying. Spend at least one hour of the expedition on the subject itself — the lawn fight is the expensive way to schedule it; the sleeping bag is the cheap way. The confession does not ruin the quest. The confession is the quest, and everything else is the bottle it came in.

2. Take the diploma, not the prize

Evan's bedroom refusal is the film's second counsel, for every quester delivered the thing he wanted in the wrong condition: the offered prize, compromised, is an exam, and its grading is permanent in the only transcript that follows you — your own account of yourself at the reunion of one, nightly. The counsel is a refusal doctrine with the mall morning as its transcript service: Evan can meet Becca's eyes at the food court because of what he declined at midnight. The legend you don't take is the one you get to keep. Tuck the moment in, walk out, and let the morning file the grade.

3. Choose your officer

The patrol is the film's third counsel, for every young person reading the adults for their own future: adulthood is not issued with the badge, the job or the mortgage; it is a fork taken in single moments, and both of the film's futures are on patrol tonight — the cruiser (the duo unseparated, burning the evidence, parenting strangers' little brothers out of homesickness) and the escalator (the separation faced, the pairs re-sorted, the life enlarged). The counsel is a fork doctrine with the farewell at the party as its saddest exhibit: Michaels delivering Fogell to his night is a man handing over the youth he never spent. Spend yours. The cruiser is always hiring.

4. Keep the thirteen-year-old's draft

The screenplay's biography is the film's fourth counsel, for every artist, diarist and keeper of a juvenilia drawer: the document you made before you learned embarrassment is the most honest one you own, and its feelings are data with a longer shelf life than your adult taste. The counsel is an archive doctrine with the film's grosses as its market evidence: two teenagers' Point Grey spring, typeset by their adult selves without correction of its heart, out-earned a decade of adult reconstructions of adolescence. Do not burn the notebooks. The adult's job is not to outgrow the record but to give it a production budget.

5. Eat with your Glanzberg

The lunchroom's loner is the film's fifth counsel, its quietest, for every member of a duo, a clique or a graduating class: your civilization has a boy at a table alone, whose aloneness is the filing your whole spring is organized to avoid becoming — and the film's authors remembered his name for twenty years and put it in a hit movie, which is a debt acknowledged but not, in the event, paid at the time. The counsel is a seating doctrine with the tray as its instrument: the cheapest bravery in any institution is the carried tray, and its interest compounds longer than any legend a party can mint. Sit down once this week. The escalator is coming for everyone's table eventually; the Glanzbergs are just the ones who took it early, alone, in public.

6. Ride the escalator like it's nothing

The mall coda is the film's sixth counsel, its benediction, for every pair of friends at a scheduled divide: the separation is one flight, mechanical, survivable — and the look you exchange on it is the whole art. The counsel is a farewell doctrine with the final shot as its entire liturgy: no chase, no speech, no last-minute reprieve — two boys, two girls, a look that says the thing the sleeping bags already said, at adult volume, in daylight, in a mall. Practice that look with your people before the date arrives. It is the diploma the whole spring was for: the friendship, re-founded at distance, on purpose, with its eyes open.

The moral of the story is…

Name the farewell tour while there's still time to take it together; take the diploma over the prize; choose the escalator over the cruiser — and keep the thirteen-year-old's draft, because he was telling the truth.

Superbad teaches that the quest is the goodbye in disguise and the confession is the cargo; that character is graded on what it declines; and that a friendship does not end at graduation — it is re-founded there, at distance, by the boys brave enough to say so in sleeping bags.

The film's final question is therefore not whether you would get the booze — the film files that under "detergent bottle." It is what your current quest is a farewell tour for; which offered prize is your bedroom exam; and whether your escalator look, when the date arrives, will be ready.
