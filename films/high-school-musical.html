01 / FILM IDENTITY

Overview

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

High School Musical is a 2006 American musical television film — a Disney Channel Original Movie directed and choreographed by Kenny Ortega (Newsies, Hocus Pocus) from a screenplay by Peter Barsocchini, and the most commercially successful production in the network's history. Made for $4.2 million, it premiered January 20, 2006 to 7.7 million U.S. viewers (a network record), reached more than 225 million viewers worldwide, and produced the best-selling album in the United States for 2006. At a New Year's Eve party at a ski lodge, Troy Bolton (Zac Efron), captain of the East High Wildcats basketball team, and Gabriella Montez (Vanessa Hudgens), a brilliant transfer student, are pushed onstage for a karaoke duet — "Start of Something New" — and discover, to their mutual alarm, that they can sing and like it. Gabriella then transfers to East High itself, and the two secretly audition for the winter musical, threatening the school's entire social physics: at East High, everyone has exactly one identity — jock, brainiac, skater, drama kid — and the cafeteria's anthem, "Stick to the Status Quo," is the constitution sung aloud. The audition scandalizes two establishments at once: the drama club's reigning twins, Sharpay and Ryan Evans (Ashley Tisdale and Lucas Grabeel), for whom the leads are a birthright, and Troy's own world — his best friend Chad (Corbin Bleu) and his father, Coach Bolton (Bart Johnson) — for whom the championship game is the only stage that matters. Gabriella's new friend Taylor (Monique Coleman), captain of the scholastic decathlon team, joins the conspiracy: both cliques scheme to break the singers apart before the callbacks, and succeed — a hacked webcam confession, a broken heart, a withdrawn Gabriella. The film's third act is the repentance and the scheduling miracle: the cliques, conscience-stricken, confess and unite; the callbacks, the championship game and the decathlon are all set for the same hour (Sharpay's last maneuver — persuading Ms. Darbus to move the callback onto game day), and East High's whole student body conspires to let two students be in three places at once — the game won, the decathlon won, the leads won, and the finale, "We're All in This Together," staged as the school's constitutional convention: every clique dancing in one gym, in one number, in matching red.

Place in the culture

The film is the defining popular culture event of its young audience's decade: a cable TV movie that outperformed most theatrical releases in reach, launched its six young leads, revived the movie musical for a generation, and built a franchise (two sequels — the third theatrical — plus stage productions in thousands of schools, an ice tour, and a streaming-era successor series). Its soundtrack's chart reign (number one on the Billboard 200, the year's best-seller, multiple singles charting simultaneously — a first since the Beatles era) made it a music-industry event as much as a television one. Critics called it Grease without the cigarettes; its audience simply called it theirs.

The little movie that ate the year

Barsocchini's script — pitched as Romeo and Juliet across the cafeteria's clique lines — was produced in Utah in a matter of weeks for $4.2 million, filming at Salt Lake City's real East High School (whose halls and gym became so iconic the school remains a fan pilgrimage site). Ortega, a choreographer-director whose whole career argued the musical was a living form, staged the numbers as full athletic productions; Efron's singing was blended with Drew Seeley's voice for Troy's higher register, a production detail that became famous only after the phenomenon made every detail famous. Disney Channel premiered the film on January 20, 2006 with modest expectations and got a detonation: repeat broadcasts broke record after record, the soundtrack hit number one twice, "Breaking Free" reached the top five, and the film won the Primetime Emmy for Outstanding Choreography. Sequels followed in 2007 (television; 17.2 million premiere viewers — a cable record) and 2008 (theatrical), and the original became the first Disney Channel movie broadcast by the BBC.

03 / NARRATIVE SUMMARY

Story Synopsis

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL FULL STORY SUMMARY

The film opens with its whole thesis staged as a party accident: two teenagers from different worlds, neither of whom sings, are handed microphones by a New Year's Eve emcee — and the duet works, the room works, the phones come out; then midnight separates them with a name half-exchanged and a photo on a phone. The transfer is the plot's second accident (Gabriella's mother's job moves her to Albuquerque, to East High, to Troy's homeroom), and the audition is its third: Troy, haunting the auditorium's edge, and Gabriella, dragged by curiosity, sing for Ms. Darbus after the official auditions close — overheard by Kelsi, the musical's mousy composer, whose songs the Evans twins have been performing as pageants instead of music. The callback sheet (Troy and Gabriella called back against Sharpay and Ryan) is the film's declaration of war, and the cafeteria answers with the constitution: "Stick to the Status Quo," a full-company number in which every student confesses a secret second self (the jock who bakes, the skater who plays cello, the brainiac who hip-hops) and is shouted back into line — the film's thesis stated by its antagonists, in its best production number, as a warning.

The conspiracy acts is the film's study of peer pressure with choreography: Chad and the Wildcats, terrified of losing their captain's focus before the championship, and Taylor and the decathlon team, terrified of losing their star's, engineer the breakup — Troy, on a webcam he doesn't know is live, tells his teammates what they want to hear ("she's just a girl… the singing thing is nothing"), and Gabriella, watching, withdraws from the callback and from him, in the film's one genuinely wounded number ("When There Was Me and You"). The repentance is mutual and swift (the cliques confess, in both directions, within a day of enjoying their victory — the film's young people have consciences calibrated like smoke detectors), but Sharpay's counter-move is administrative and brilliant: the callbacks are rescheduled onto the exact hour of the championship game and the scholastic decathlon. The finale is the whole school as a single stage-management conspiracy: the decathlon team hacks the gym's scoreboard to force a delay, the game pauses, Troy and Gabriella race through the halls in uniform and lab coat respectively, the callback is sung ("Breaking Free") to a teacher who has just watched two other institutions bend their schedules around her art — and the leads are won, the game is won on the resumed play, the decathlon is won, and East High dances. The film's last image is the constitution amended: the whole student body in the gym, in one number, every clique in the same choreography — with Zeke's crème brûlée, baked for Sharpay, as the treaty's first ratification.

The full narrative summary is an editorial paraphrase, not dialogue or a scene-by-scene transcript.

THE CAPTAIN

Troy Bolton

Zac Efron. East High's basketball captain and most popular student — the school's golden boy, whose single unauthorized desire (to sing, publicly, with the new girl) is treated by every institution he belongs to as a defection. Efron plays him with an earnest, athletic sincerity that made him the decade's defining teen lead, and Troy's function is the story's portrait of the insider as the true rebel: the status quo's most protected citizen is the one with the most to lose by breaking it — which is why his breaking it, rather than an outsider's, amends the constitution. The archive files him as the decade's most consequential teen captain in film: a point guard who led two teams, one of them a school.

THE NEW GIRL

Gabriella Montez

Vanessa Hudgens. A math and science prodigy transferred to East High mid-year — carrying her own status-quo wound (at her last school she was "the freaky math girl," a label she crossed the country to escape) and therefore the film's most reluctant rebel: she does not want a second identity problem; she wants to be normal, once. Hudgens plays her with a shy luminosity that makes her courage legible as courage, and Gabriella's function is the story's portrait of the transfer student as the constitution's natural auditor: only the newcomer can see the cafeteria's law as a choice rather than a climate. The archive files her as the decade's most complete teen heroine in a musical: a brainiac whose bravest act is an encore.

THE DIVA

Sharpay Evans

Ashley Tisdale. The drama club's reigning star — seventeen leads deep, co-president with her twin, and the film's antagonist by professional interest: the leads are her property by precedent, and the newcomers are trespassers. Tisdale plays her with a precision-engineered fabulousness (every entrance a small coronation) that made Sharpay the film's breakout and its most quoted character, and her function is the story's portrait of the incumbent as the system's true believer: she does not hate Troy and Gabriella; she is defending a constitution that has always paid her — and her weapons (flattery, scheduling, her father's golf-club connections) are the establishment's own. The archive files her as the decade's most beloved teen antagonist: a diva defeated by a calendar she weaponized first.

THE TWIN

Ryan Evans

Lucas Grabeel. Sharpay's twin brother and co-star — the drama club's choreographer-in-fact, a gifted performer who has spent his career as his sister's supporting player and hat collection's display stand. Grabeel plays him with a sly, feline competence that keeps stealing scenes the script assigns to others, and Ryan's function is the story's portrait of the lieutenant's quiet audit: he executes Sharpay's schemes with professional polish while visibly recalculating his own account — the film's sequels would cash the recalculation, but the first film already files it in his eyes during "Stick to the Status Quo." The archive files him as the decade's most scene-stealing teen twin in film: a co-president in waiting, in jazz shoes.

THE BEST FRIEND

Chad Danforth

Corbin Bleu. Troy's best friend and fellow Wildcat — the team's enforcer of focus, the conspiracy's field commander, and the film's portrait of loyalty misfiled as control: Chad's sabotage is not malice but terror (of the championship slipping, of his friend changing, of the team's world ending), and his repentance, when it comes, is the team's. Bleu plays him with an athlete's bounce and a conspirator's guilt arriving on schedule, and Chad's function is the story's portrait of the best friend as the constitution's police and its first defector: the boy who polices the status quo in Act II wears the "We're All in This Together" finale like a confession. The archive files him as the decade's most complete teen best friend in a musical: a Wildcat whose arc is from hall monitor to accomplice.

THE CAPTAIN, OTHER SIDE

Taylor McKessie

Monique Coleman. Captain of the scholastic decathlon team — Gabriella's recruiter, friend and co-conspirator: the brainiac mirror of Chad, running the same sabotage from the opposite clique with the same justification (the team's title, the friend's focus) and arriving at the same repentance on the same schedule. Coleman plays her with a brisk, funny authority that makes Taylor the film's most competent student, and her function is the story's portrait of the symmetry of peer pressure: the status quo is not enforced by villains or by one clique — it is enforced, identically, by everyone's best friend, for the best reasons, in both directions. The archive files her as the decade's most essential minor captain in a teen musical: the decathlon's Chad, with better arguments.

THE FATHER-COACH

Coach Jack Bolton

Bart Johnson. East High's basketball coach and Troy's father — the two roles fused into the film's portrait of the inherited identity: Coach Bolton does not merely run the team his son captains; he is the reason the captaincy is a destiny rather than a choice (his own East High glory is the family business), and his suspicion of the musical is a father's audit of a son's distraction. Johnson plays him with a whistle-and-clipboard gruffness that thaws, across the film, into the finale's proud spectator, and Coach Bolton's function is the story's portrait of the parent as the constitution's author: the status quo Troy breaks was written, lovingly, at home. The archive files him as the decade's most complete coach-father in teen film: a man who learns to watch his son sing from the bleachers he commands.

THE TEACHER

Ms. Darbus

Alyson Reed. East High's drama teacher — a theatrical grandee marooned in a public school, at ceremonial war with Coach Bolton over the gym's soul and the students' hours, and the audition's presiding authority. Reed plays her with a delicious, curtain-speech grandeur (every announcement a soliloquy), and Ms. Darbus's function is the story's portrait of the arts as the constitution's loyal opposition: she cannot amend the status quo herself — her theater is its designated reservation — but she can hold the callback, recognize the real thing when it sneaks in after hours, and file the film's verdict on the two leads in a single casting decision. The archive files her as the decade's definitive drama teacher in film: a soliloquy with a casting sheet.

THE COMPOSER

The musical's composer — a shy pianist whose songs the Evans twins perform as pageants, and whose true arrangements (written for voices like Troy's and Gabriella's) are the film's buried score: it is Kelsi who plays for the secret audition, Kelsi who slips them the callback music, Kelsi whose art the whole plot is secretly about. Rulin plays her nearly silent, at a piano, as the film's portrait of the artist inside the machine: the status quo's theater runs on her work while billing her as nobody — and the finale's amendment includes her promotion to the music's public author. The archive files her as the decade's most consequential minor composer in film: the girl at the piano who wrote the revolution's songs.

THE TEAM

The Wildcats

THE STAGE

The Drama Club

Ms. Darbus's theater and the Evans twins' hereditary estate: seventeen productions of precedent, a casting system indistinguishable from a monarchy, and the film's portrait of the arts as the status quo's own reservation — the one place second selves are licensed, provided they arrive through the proper auditions and the proper family. THE BRAINIACS

The Scholastic Decathlon Team

East High's academic establishment under Taylor: the mirror faction — as jealous of its star, as fluent in sabotage, and as convinced of its cause's priority (a national title in the sciences against a school musical) as the Wildcats are of theirs. The Team's function is the story's portrait of the symmetry of the status quo: it is not jocks versus artists, with brains as referees; it is every excellence guarding its own borders with the same fences. The Team ends the film hacking a scoreboard for the musical's sake, the archive files it as the decade's most versatile fictional decathlon squad: champions who win their title and somebody else's callback in the same hour.

THE FACULTY

The Bolton–Darbus War

The adults' cold war over the gym, the calendar and the children's identities: Coach Bolton's program and Ms. Darbus's theater as the faculty's own two cliques, enforcing on the students a division the students inherited rather than chose. The War's function is the story's portrait of the constitution's authors at work: the cafeteria's law is a faculty treaty the children are conscripted to sing — which is why the finale's sight of both teachers in the same gym, watching the same children do everything at once, is the film's true ratification scene. The War ends in an armistice of applause, the archive files it as the decade's most consequential fictional faculty feud: a turf war settled by the turf's occupants.

The constitution is sung by everyone

The film's factional design is a closed system of mutual custody: every clique polices every other clique's borders, the faculty polices the cliques, and the amendment, when it comes, is correspondingly total — no faction is left unconverted, because none could be. The archive files the design as the story's durable civics: a status quo maintained by all can only be amended by all, in one number, together.

08 / THE STATUS QUO, THE CALLBACK & THE DOUBLE BOOKING

The Status Quo, the Callback & the Double Booking

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

The status quo — the one-identity law

East High's unwritten constitution: every student holds exactly one identity, publicly, at an assigned table — jock, brainiac, skater, thespian — and second selves are contraband. The law's function is the story's portrait of the social order as a filing system with an anthem: it is not maintained by force but by chorus (the number is literally the school singing its own police), and its subjects' universal secret doubleness is the system's open secret and structural flaw. The archive files the status quo as the decade's most completely articulated fictional social law: a constitution whose entire text is a song everybody knows and nobody believes.

The callback — the amendment procedure

The musical's second-round audition: the constitution's one licensed mechanism by which the filing system can be revised — a newcomer may, in principle, take a lead from an incumbent, provided she survives the procedure's politics (the incumbents' sabotage, the teacher's scheduling, the whole school's calendar). The callback's function is the story's portrait of reform as an obstacle course run at sprint speed in two costumes: Troy and Gabriella's victory is not a coronation but a logistics miracle performed by their repentant enemies. The archive files the callback as the decade's most consequential fictional audition: a procedure that amends a school by being survived.

The double booking — the triple finale

Sharpay's masterstroke and the plot's engine: the callback, the championship game and the decathlon scheduled for the same hour — a bureaucratic checkmate designed to force the singers to choose their constituencies. The double booking's function is the story's portrait of the calendar as the establishment's last weapon: when the incumbents cannot win the argument, they win the scheduling — and the film's answer (a hacked scoreboard, a paused game, a school-wide conspiracy of stage management) is its doctrine that constitutions are amended by logistics as much as by ideals. The archive files the double booking as the decade's most instructive fictional scheduling conflict: three finales, one hour, and a whole school as the stage manager.

The karaoke accident — the unlicensed stage

The film's founding event: a party emcee, two conscripted strangers, a duet neither would have auditioned for — the discovery of the second self in the one venue with no filing system (a ski lodge, a holiday, a crowd of nobody they know). Karaoke's function is the story's portrait of the accident as the constitution's natural predator: the status quo can police every official stage in a student's life and still be undone by a microphone at a party. The archive files the karaoke stage as the decade's most consequential fictional party game: an unlicensed audition that amended a school.

10 / THEMATIC ANALYSIS

Themes & Analysis

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Stick to the status quo — the one-identity law

The film's governing theme is sung by its antagonists in its best number: that a community will organize itself into single-identity files — one gift, one table, one public self per citizen — and will police the filing with its whole culture, not because the law serves anyone's happiness (the confessions prove it serves no one's) but because it serves everyone's legibility. The theme is worked out in the film's ascending enforcement (cafeteria chorus, best-friend custody, faculty treaty, diva's calendar) until the audience holds the film's doctrine entire: the status quo is not a villain's plot but a chorus — maintained by everyone, believed by no one, and therefore amendable only in the same medium it is maintained: a bigger chorus, in the finale, singing the opposite. Its proposition reaches every member of every filed community in the audience — every professional with a secret second practice, every family with assigned roles at its table: your second self is not your shame; it is your amendment, awaiting its callback. The reach is the story's question to every Zeke with a crème brûlée in his locker: who taught you to whisper it — and what would your cafeteria sing if you said it at full volume?

We're all in this together — the amendment as choreography

The film's second theme is its finale's method: that the constitution is not amended by the heroes' victory alone but by the whole student body's conversion into the heroes' stage crew — the repentant conspirators running the logistics (the hacked scoreboard, the paused game, the sprint routes) that let two people be whole in three places. The theme is worked out in the film's careful distribution of the miracle (Troy and Gabriella supply the talent; everyone else supplies the hour) until the audience holds the film's civics: a person's wholeness is a community project — no one is in two places at once without accomplices — and a community's maturity is measured by whether its members conspire for each other's second selves or against them. Its proposition reaches every best friend, teammate and colleague in the audience currently policing someone's focus: the promotion you are guarding them from may be their callback, and the finest hour of your loyalty is the interference you run for it. The reach is the story's question to every Chad with a conspiracy underway: what would it cost to run the same play in the other direction?

The incumbent's estate — Sharpay and the legitimacy of precedent

The film's third theme is its antagonist's dignity: that Sharpay's claim is, under the constitution as written, legitimate — seventeen leads, a decade of training, a theater run as her family's estate — and that reform which cannot acknowledge the incumbent's earned stake will produce not an amendment but a war. The theme is worked out in the film's scrupulous fairness (Sharpay is defeated by procedure — a fair callback, fairly survived — never by exposure or humiliation; her weapons are the system's own) until the audience holds the film's doctrine of reform: the amendment that lasts is the one the incumbent can survive with her dignity and her dessert intact. Its proposition reaches every reformer in the audience facing a Sharpay — a senior partner, a tenured star, an elder with a title deed: win by the callback, not by the coup; let the procedure be visibly fair; and have the brûlée ready. The reach is the story's question to every Troy with a callback sheet in his hand: are you amending the constitution — or just seizing the theater?

The buried composer — credit as the amendment's signature

The film's fourth theme is Kelsi: that every establishment runs on the uncredited work of its overlooked — the songs composed in obscurity and pageanted by the incumbents — and that a true amendment includes a restoration of authorship. The theme is worked out in the film's quietest arc (the mousy pianist who hears the secret audition, supplies the real arrangements, and takes her bow in the finale as the music's author) until the audience holds the film's finding: the revolution's score is usually already written, by someone the system filed as nobody, waiting for performers who will sing it as written. Its proposition reaches every institution in the audience with a Kelsi in the basement — an analyst, a junior, an accompanist: the audit is not whether you have such a person (you do) but whether your finale will bill her. The reach is the story's question to every Ms. Darbus with a casting sheet: whose name is on the music your theater has been performing — and when did you last read it aloud?

11 / FULL SPOILER CONCLUSION

Ending Explained

REVEAL THE ENDING

With the callback, the championship game and the decathlon set for the same hour, the repentant cliques conspire to help: the scoreboard is hacked to delay the game, Troy and Gabriella reach the callback and sing "Breaking Free," win the leads, and the Wildcats win the resumed game and the decathlon team its title. The film closes with the whole school performing "We're All in This Together" in the gym.

What the ending establishes

  • The triple victory files the amendment as total rather than zero-sum: the singers do not win at the team's or the decathlon's expense — all three titles land in the same hour, the film's refutation of the scarcity the status quo assumed.
  • The hacked scoreboard files the conspiracy's conversion as the finale's true mechanism: the same ingenuity that broke the duet now saves it — peer pressure's tools, reversed, become peer support's.
  • Sharpay's gracious defeat files the reform's fairness as its durability: beaten by a fair callback, she can join the finale — an incumbent absorbed, like Happy Feet's Noah, into the amendment's chorus.
  • Coach Bolton's applause files the deepest status quo amended: the father's bleachers hold a man watching his son be two things at once, and approving — the inherited identity renegotiated without a word of dialogue.
  • Kelsi's bow files the authorship restored: the buried composer takes public credit in the finale — the amendment's signature, in the program, at last.

What remains uncertain

Whether East High's amendment survives the semester, whether Sharpay's junior-year campaign will test it, and what the Wildcats' next season does with a singing captain — all stand open at the film's close (two sequels followed, in 2007 and 2008, the second taking up exactly the status quo's summer-job edition). Within this film, the archive files the uncertainty as the story's own last word on its theme: the constitution held for one glorious hour, in one gym, in matching red — and constitutions, the film notes, are only ever as strong as their next cafeteria.

12 / PURPOSES OF THE STORY

Moral of The Story

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

High School Musical uses a karaoke accident, a cafeteria anthem and a triple-booked finale to examine identity, custody and amendment: a school where every student is filed under one gift and secretly carries two; best friends who police each other's wholeness for love's sake; and an incumbent diva defeated, fairly, by the constitution's own procedure. Through the status quo, the callback and the scoreboard, the film argues that the one-identity law is a chorus maintained by everyone and believed by no one; that a person's second self is a community project, requiring accomplices; and that reform lasts when the incumbent can survive it with dignity.

Why tell this story?

Because Barsocchini and Ortega understood that the audience for a Disney Channel musical — children on the threshold of the filing system themselves — needed the constitution described before they were conscripted to sing it: here is the law, here is its anthem, here is the confession scene where everyone admits the law is a fiction, and here is the amendment, performed as a dance. They told it in the musical form because the musical is the one genre where a community's feelings are its politics, sung in public. The result is the defining teen film of its generation, the archive files: a $4.2 million television movie that out-taught a decade of assemblies on the subject of being two things at once.

1. Confess the second self early

The cafeteria number is the film's first counsel, for every filed citizen — student, professional, family member with an assigned role: your secret second self (the baking, the cello, the hip-hop, the singing) is not an embarrassment to be managed but the raw material of your amendment, and its confession, however shouted down, is the crack through which the finale eventually enters. The counsel is a confession doctrine with Zeke's ramekin as its entire procedure: say it in the cafeteria, at full volume, before the conspiracy forms to say it for you. The shouting is a ritual; nobody believes it; and the second confession is always easier than the first — Martha is waiting for your courage to schedule hers.

2. Run interference for somebody's callback

The finale's logistics are the film's second counsel, for every best friend currently policing a loved one's focus: the finest form of loyalty is not custody but stage management — the hacked scoreboard, the paused game, the held door between the two cathedrals of someone's divided life. The counsel is an accomplice doctrine with Chad's conversion as its precedent: the same ingenuity you were spending on the sabotage is the exact budget the rescue requires, and the friend you are protecting from distraction may be protecting you, in turn, from a smaller life. Audit your conspiracies. Re-aim them. The championship and the callback can both be won, but only by a conspiracy running in the right direction.

3. Win by the callback, not the coup

Sharpay's fair defeat is the film's third counsel, for every reformer facing an incumbent with a legitimate estate: use the constitution's own amendment procedure, visibly and fairly, even when a coup is available — because the incumbent who is beaten fairly can join your finale, and the one who is humiliated will spend the sequel (there is always a sequel) on restoration. The counsel is a reform doctrine with the brûlée as its ratification instrument: acknowledge the estate's earned stake, survive the procedure's politics without poisoning it, and have the dessert ready for the signing. The amendment that lasts is the one its Sharpay can dance in.

4. Bill your Kelsi

The composer's bow is the film's fourth counsel, for every institution running on buried authorship: find the person whose work your establishment performs while filing her as nobody — the analyst behind the partner's deck, the accompanist behind the star's audition, the junior behind the house style — and put her name on the program before the finale forces the audit. The counsel is a credit doctrine with the callback music as its exhibit: the revolution's score in your building is probably already written, in obscurity, by exactly the person your filing system cannot see. Promote the composer. The music improves, and so, measurably, does the supply of future music.

5. Let the faculty watch

The Bolton–Darbus armistice is the film's fifth counsel, for every author of a young person's constitution — parent, coach, teacher, manager of juniors: the division you are enforcing between their identities is your treaty, not their nature, and the evidence is available any afternoon you care to watch them be whole. The counsel is a spectatorship doctrine with the finale's bleachers as its entire faculty lounge: attend the callback. Sit in the other cathedral. Applaud in both. The son you filed under one gift is not betraying the family business by singing; he is diversifying it — and the applause of the constitution's author is the amendment's final ratification, worth more than the casting sheet.

6. Keep the finale's choreography

The closing number is the film's sixth counsel, its benediction, for every community that has amended itself once and would like the amendment to survive the semester: constitutions are maintained by repetition, and the finale's choreography — everyone, in one place, performing the togetherness — is not the celebration of the new law but its enforcement mechanism. The counsel is a ritual doctrine with the matching red as its uniform: schedule the number. Sing the opposite anthem until it is the one everybody knows by heart. The status quo had a song; the amendment needs one too, and it needs to be sung in the cafeteria, at lunch, by the people who used to shout the confessions down.

The moral of the story is…

Confess the second self at full volume; run interference for the callbacks you once sabotaged; win by the fair procedure and bill your composer — and keep singing the amendment until it is the anthem everybody knows.

High School Musical teaches that the one-identity law is a chorus believed by no one and amendable by everyone; that wholeness is a community project requiring accomplices; and that a reform the incumbent can survive is the only kind that survives.

The film's final question is therefore not whether you can sing — the film files that under "karaoke." It is which table you are sitting at and what you are whispering; whose callback your ingenuity is currently scheduled against; and whether your school's Kelsi will be billed in the finale, or only in the archive.
