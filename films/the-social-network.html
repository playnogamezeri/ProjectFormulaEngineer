The Social Network

Harvard, 2003. Dumped in a bar in the film's first five minutes, Mark Zuckerberg goes home drunk and builds a website to rate his classmates' faces — and, in the smoking ruins of every friendship he will ever have, the largest social platform on Earth. Fincher and Sorkin's deposition drama: the creation myth of the connected age, told by the disconnected — a story in which everyone is lying a little, and the truth is whatever the code did.

01 / FILM IDENTITY

Overview

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

The Social Network is a 2010 American biographical drama directed by David Fincher from Aaron Sorkin's screenplay, adapted from Ben Mezrich's The Accidental Billionaires — the story of Facebook's founding, told as two lawsuits. In the frame, Mark Zuckerberg (Jesse Eisenberg) sits through depositions: in one room, the Winklevoss twins and Divya Narendra claim he stole their idea for a Harvard social network; in another, Eduardo Saverin (Andrew Garfield) — his co-founder, best friend and first financier — sues over the dilution that reduced his stake in the company from 34 percent to 0.03 percent. Between the testimony, the film flashes back: a dumped sophomore builds Facemash in a drunken night and crashes Harvard's network; is recruited, perhaps, by the Winklevosses to build their site, and delays them for weeks while building his own with Eduardo's money and algorithm; launches TheFacebook in February 2004 and watches it eat the Ivy League; is seduced west by Napster's fallen founder Sean Parker (Justin Timberlake), whose glamour and paranoia both prove contagious; and presides, step by reasonable step, over the freezing-out of the friend who started it all. It grossed $224.9 million worldwide on a $40 million budget, was named the best film of 2010 by more critics than any other that year, and won three Academy Awards — Sorkin's adapted screenplay, Trent Reznor and Atticus Ross's score, and film editing — losing Best Picture to The King's Speech in a result still argued about as the decade's great Oscar injustice. Its claim on the culture is larger than its prizes: it is the founding text of how the internet age understands itself — a story about a boy who could not get into the rooms that mattered and so built a room of his own and charged the world admission in friendship, ending as the youngest billionaire on Earth, alone in a conference room, refreshing a friend request he sent to the girl who dumped him.

Place in the culture

The definitive film of the internet's founding generation — and, by wide consent, one of the great American screenplays, Sorkin's dialogue running at a speed that turned deposition testimony into thriller mechanics while Reznor and Ross's score invented the sound of the startup era: anxious, electronic, beautiful and cold. Its deposits are a grammar for the tech-founder myth (genius as social wound, disruption as revenge for exclusion), a generation of founder dramas in its image, and a closing shot — the refresh, the waiting — that has become the era's emblem of connected loneliness. It endures because its question has only grown: what does it cost a person to build the thing that connects everyone else?

A screenplay that outran its subject

Sorkin wrote the script from Mezrich's book proposal and his own research — famously without the cooperation of Zuckerberg, who declined involvement — and has been candid that he was writing a story about invention, loyalty and betrayal in the classical mold rather than a documentary record; the film's Mark is a dramatization, a fact the archive notes as the production's central tension and its artistic license. Fincher shot it with his customary precision — the opening breakup scene runs nine pages in under five minutes, performed at a tempo that announces the film's entire method — and cast the Winklevoss twins as a single actor, Armie Hammer, doubled by a body double and digital face replacement, so that the brothers' identical grievance could be played by one face. Premiering at the New York Film Festival in September 2010, the film was received as an instant landmark: 447 critics listed it among the year's best, more than any other film, and its three Oscars — screenplay, score, editing — certified the craft of a movie whose whole subject is a young man typing, and which nonetheless plays like a thriller about the end of the world. The world, in a sense, agreed: it was ending, and being replaced by the thing he typed.

03 / NARRATIVE SUMMARY

Story Synopsis

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL FULL STORY SUMMARY

The film opens at a full sprint: a bar, a breakup, five minutes of dialogue at fencing speed, in which Erica Albright dismantles Mark Zuckerberg — his obsession with final clubs, his social arithmetic, his cruelty dressed as honesty — and leaves him with the sentence the whole film will spend itself testing: you are going to go through life thinking girls don't like you because you're a nerd, when it will be because you're an asshole. Mark goes home, drinks, blogs viciously about her, and in one night builds Facemash — a site that hacks the houses' facebooks and pits students' photographs against each other, girl against girl — which draws 22,000 hits in two hours, crashes Harvard's network, and earns him an administrative board hearing, probation, and the attention of three seniors: Cameron and Tyler Winklevoss, Olympic-rowing aristocrats of the Porcellian, and their partner Divya Narendra, who have an idea — HarvardConnection, an exclusive social network — and need a programmer. What Mark does next is the first deposition's whole subject: he says yes, and then, for six weeks, he stalls them with plausible excuses while building, in Kirkland House with his friend Eduardo Saverin's money and Eduardo's chess-algorithm for the site's matching logic, the thing itself. TheFacebook launches February 4, 2004. It is, instantly and obviously, the better mousetrap — exclusive, clean, alive — and as it spreads school by school, the Winklevosses' cease-and-desist letters begin their long journey toward the lawsuit, and Eduardo — CFO, co-founder, holder of the bank account and 30 percent — begins his toward obsolescence, though neither man can see it yet.

The second half is a seduction and a freezing. In New York, Eduardo chases advertising money the old way, in suits, at meetings; in California, Mark meets Sean Parker — Napster's co-founder, the internet's first rock star, a man who has already destroyed one industry at 19 and arrives trailing venture capital, cocaine and prophecy. Parker's contributions are real and ruinous in the same breath: the name (drop the "The" — just Facebook), the move to Palo Alto, the ethos (a million dollars isn't cool; a billion dollars is), the investors — and a paranoia about Eduardo that finds, in Mark, fertile soil. The film cross-cuts the rise with the depositions' tightening gyre: Eduardo, frozen out of decisions, flies to California to find his name on a lease he never signed and his shares — by a set of documents he signed without reading, at a moment of trust — diluted from 34 percent to 0.03 percent, while Parker and the new hires' shares are protected. The friendship ends in a smashed laptop and the film's most quoted sentence, delivered by Eduardo with the wreckage in his hands: I was your only friend, Mark. You had one friend. Parker himself falls almost immediately after — arrested at a company party with cocaine and underage interns, in circumstances the film lets Eduardo's suspicions decorate without settling — and Mark is left, at 26, the youngest self-made billionaire in history, settling both lawsuits for sums (65 million to the Winklevosses; an undisclosed amount and a restored co-founder title for Eduardo) that the final title cards deliver with a bookkeeper's calm. The last scene is the film's whole meaning in one shot: Mark, alone in the deposition's empty conference room at night, sends a friend request to Erica Albright — the girl from the bar, from before everything — and refreshes the page. And refreshes it. The inventor of the world's friendship sits waiting to be accepted by the one person whose acceptance the entire empire was, in some accounting the film never quite closes, built to earn.

04 / STORY STRUCTURE

Story & Chronology

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

The film unfolds between Harvard in the fall of 2003 and the depositions of 2007–08, cross-cut throughout. The sequence below follows the film's narrative order.

Act I — Facemash and the idea

A breakup, a hack, a hearing — and three seniors with a social network they cannot build, hiring the one boy who can.

Act II — The launch and the seduction

TheFacebook eats the Ivy League; a fallen founder from Napster arrives with a billion-dollar vocabulary — and the CFO's chair begins to empty.

Act III — The depositions

A dilution, a smashed laptop, two settlements — and the youngest billionaire in the world, alone, refreshing.

05 / CHARACTER DATABASE

Character Database

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Search the principal characters documented in this film. Roles and motivations below summarize their function in the 2010 movie.

THE FOUNDER

Mark Zuckerberg

Jesse Eisenberg. A Harvard sophomore of ferocious intelligence and near-total social illiteracy — or is it literacy of a different, colder kind? — whose every act in the film can be read two ways: the stalling of the Winklevosses as theft or as a builder declining to build somebody else's worse idea; the freezing of Eduardo as business or as betrayal; the cruelty as Asperger's-adjacent honesty or as a weapon he aims precisely. Eisenberg plays him at a single, unblinking tempo — the fastest mind in every room, paying for it in the currency the film's first scene names — and his function is the story's central undecidability: the film never rules on whether Mark is a villain, a savant, or a boy whose one gift (seeing systems whole) is constitutionally blind to the thing systems are made of. The archive files him as the decade's defining protagonist: a founder who built the world's friendship as an externality of wanting into rooms — and who ends the film as he began it, waiting on a girl's verdict, with the whole planet's attention at his command and one person's withheld.

THE CO-FOUNDER

Eduardo Saverin

Andrew Garfield. Mark's best friend, first believer and first financier — a Brazilian-born economics student who put up the first thousand dollars, supplied the algorithm the site ranked by, held the bank account, and flew to New York in a suit to sell the ads a 19-year-old's company needed — while the company, in California, was outgrowing the suit. Garfield plays him with a warmth that makes the film's tragedy legible: Eduardo is not a fool (his caution about monetization was, on the merits, defensible) but a man playing an older game correctly while a newer one is invented around him — and his dilution, executed through documents he signed in trust, is the film's coldest sequence of paperwork. His function is the story's heart and its plaintiff: the friendship's ledger-keeper, whose final accounting — I was your only friend — is the deposition the whole film has been conducting. The archive files him as the decade's most sympathetic founder: the man who was right about the money, wrong about the century, and honest about both in a room full of lawyers.

THE SEDUCER

Sean Parker

Justin Timberlake. Napster's co-founder — the internet's first fallen rock star, who destroyed the music industry at 19, lost his company, and arrives in the film trailing venture capital, allergy medication and apocalyptic glamour. Timberlake plays him as pure charisma with a paranoia engine: Parker's gifts to Facebook are real (the name, the move west, the investors, the billion-dollar ambition that re-scales Mark's imagination) and so is his poison — he teaches Mark to see Eduardo as the past, and his own fall, at a party raided with cocaine in the house, is the film's fastest karma. His function is the story's Mephistopheles in a hoodie avant la lettre: the man who shows the founder the size of the prize and the price list in the same gesture — and whose own deposition-era irrelevance is the film's quiet footnote on rock stars: the company kept his ambition and discarded him, as companies do. The archive files him as the decade's most prophetic minor figure: the future of tech charisma, arriving early, leaving by police escort.

THE CLAIMANT

Cameron Winklevoss

Armie Hammer. An Olympic rower, a Porcellian finalist, a gentleman of the old Harvard — and, with his twin, the co-author of the idea Mark may or may not have stolen. Hammer plays both twins (with a body double and digital doubling) as a study in inherited codes: Cameron is the brother who wants to handle the theft as gentlemen — through the university's honor procedures, through President Summers, through every channel the old world provides for exactly this injury — and discovers, channel by channel, that the old world's remedies were built for a world in which the thief needed the rooms the twins were born into. His function is the story's plaintiff of principle: his grievance is real, his idea was genuinely adjacent, and his tragedy is that the code he lives by — never sue, never whine, let the institution handle it — is precisely the delay in which the future was built without him. The archive files him as the decade's most sympathetic loser: a man whose manners were a strategy for a game that had already ended.

THE TWIN

Tyler Winklevoss

Armie Hammer. Cameron's identical twin and doubles partner — the hotter of the two, the one who wants to fight (literally, in the film's best twin joke: let me just knock the kid out; Cameron: I can't let you do that; Tyler: why not? Cameron: because we're being sued, eventually, and we don't want to look like thugs) — and the brother whose instincts keep being right a beat too late. His function is the story's portrait of the old world's muscle: everything the twins have — the bodies, the boats, the billions-to-come — is real except the one thing the moment required, which was code. The archive files him as the film's most efficient double act with himself: two brothers, one face, one grievance — the establishment's claim on the future, rowing in perfect unison, in the wrong event.

THE PARTNER

Divya Narendra

Max Minghella. The Winklevosses' partner in HarvardConnection — the idea's third author and, in the film's filing, its most clear-eyed claimant: it is Divya who first says the word "sue," Divya who reads Mark's stalling correctly earliest, and Divya whose fury at the gentlemen's code (his partners' reluctance to fight a Harvard man in public) is the deposition's most modern voice. His function is the story's reminder that the twins' claim was a partnership's, and that the partnership's tragedy is its politest members: the man who saw the theft first was outvoted by the honor of the men who owned the idea with him. The archive files him as the film's most underrated figure: the plaintiff who was right soonest, in the wrong company, under the wrong code.

THE FIRST SCENE

Erica Albright

Rooney Mara. A Boston University student whose five-minute breakup with Mark opens the film and haunts its entire architecture: she names his wound (the final clubs), his method (cruelty as honesty), and his future's misdiagnosis (the nerd alibi for the asshole's outcomes) — and then exits the story almost entirely, to become its destination: the blog post's target, Facemash's occasion, and the final shot's friend request. Mara plays her with a precision that makes the scene an instant classic of the breakup genre — she is the only person in the film who ever tells Mark the whole truth, at conversational speed, for free. Her function is the story's chorus and its verdict-in-waiting: the empire is built, the billions arrive, the lawsuits settle — and the film ends on her unanswered request, because hers was the only acceptance that was ever the point. The archive files her as the decade's most consequential first scene: the girl who got away with the truth.

THE GIRLFRIEND

Christy Lee

Brenda Song. Eduardo's girlfriend — introduced as a Facebook super-user (she found him through the site; the film's first proof of its product's power) and developed as the story's small, sharp study of the era's new jealousies: the relationship-status anxieties, the text-message vigils, the gift set on fire in a trash can. Song plays her with a comic intensity the film uses to keep its second act human, and her function is the story's product demonstration wearing a person: Christy is what Facebook does to a relationship — surveillance as intimacy, status as evidence — arriving in Eduardo's life at exactly the moment his other partnership is being surveilled and dissolved by paperwork. The archive files her as the film's most efficient minor figure: the platform's first power user, burning brightly, in a dorm room, over a boy the platform is about to make very rich and very served.

THE JUNIOR LAWYER

Marylin Delpy

Rashida Jones. A junior litigator on Mark's defense team — the deposition room's audience surrogate and the film's final voice of assessment: it is Marylin who tells Mark, in the empty conference room, that he's not an asshole, he's just trying so hard to be — the film's thesis delivered as a kindness by its most minor professional. Jones plays her with a dry, humane economy, and her function is the story's frame of judgment: the law's youngest representative, watching the youngest billionaire, filing the verdict the courts cannot (the settlements are money; the character judgment is hers). The archive files her as the decade's most efficient closing argument: two sentences, no fee, and the defendant's whole case — in every sense — decided.

THE PRESIDENT

Larry Summers

Douglas Urbanski. Harvard's president — former Treasury Secretary — who receives the Winklevosses' complaint in the film's most quoted institutional scene and dismisses it with a patrician's economy: the university is not a court for hurt feelings; the twins should let their imaginations run elsewhere; other schools' students manage to invent things without stealing. Summers's function is the story's certificate of the old world's uselessness: the highest authority the gentlemen's code can appeal to regards their grievance as an embarrassment — and his office, wood-paneled and amused, is where the twins' strategy of patience goes to die. The archive files him as the film's most devastating cameo: the establishment, in person, declining to establish anything — the sound of a door closing that the twins mistook for a door.

THE ROOMMATES

Dustin Moskovitz & Chris Hughes

Joseph Mazzello and Patrick Mapel. Mark's Kirkland House roommates — recruited into the dorm-room company as its engineers and its conscience-adjacent staff, who build, test and expand TheFacebook in its first semesters and ride it west to become, in the record beyond the film, billionaires and (in Hughes's case) the era's most famous internal critic of what they built. Their function in the film is the story's workshop chorus: the proof that Facebook was a dorm's work before it was a founder's myth — hands on keyboards at 3 a.m., names the depositions rarely call. The archive files them as the film's most quietly loaded minor pair: the co-founders history kept, standing at the edge of the frame of a story about which founders history keeps.

THE INVESTOR

Peter Thiel

Wallace Langham. The PayPal co-founder and contrarian investor who writes Facebook's first outside check — $500,000 that converts the dorm company into a Silicon Valley property and begins the professional era in which Eduardo becomes redundant. Langham plays him in a single boardroom register of calm certainty, and his function is the story's hinge of eras: Thiel's money is the moment the friendship's company becomes the industry's, with the industry's instruments — the new shares, the restructurings, the paperwork in which a co-founder can be diluted by signature. The archive files him as the film's most consequential minor figure per minute: the adult in the room, whose arrival is the beginning of the end of the room being a dorm.

06 / HARVARD, THE FINAL CLUBS & SILICON VALLEY

Harvard, the Final Clubs & Silicon Valley

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
THE UNIVERSITY

Harvard and its clubs

The old world's citadel: an institution whose real curriculum, in the film's filing, is exclusion — the final clubs (Porcellian, Phoenix, Fly) whose doors are the story's original wound and whose membership is the currency Mark cannot earn and therefore decides to devalue by building a bigger room. Harvard's function is the story's portrait of a gatekeeping order at the moment of its obsolescence: its honor code cannot process the theft, its president will not, its network is about to be eaten by a sophomore's website — and its students, given the choice between the clubs' exclusivity and Facebook's, choose Facebook by the thousands in a semester. The university ends the film as it began, magnificent and irrelevant to the outcome, the archive files it as the story's most poignant faction: the room that wouldn't let him in, preserved forever as the room his room replaced.

THE COMPANY

Facebook, the dorm edition

The Kirkland House startup: four students, a borrowed algorithm, a thousand of Eduardo's dollars and a product whose growth is the film's one uncontested miracle. The dorm company's function is the story's portrait of creation before capital: decisions made at 3 a.m. by friendship's rules, equity divided by handshake arithmetic (the 70/30 that becomes the lawsuits' scripture), and a camaraderie the film photographs like a golden age — because it knows, and shows, the exact documents in which the golden age ends. The company ends the film as an empire with its founders at law, the archive files it as the story's saddest faction: the only version of Facebook that ever fit in one room, dissolved by the paperwork of outgrowing it.

THE VALLEY

Silicon Valley and its money

The new world's court: Palo Alto houses, venture checks, Sean Parker's prophecy-economy — a place whose rules (move fast, scale first, monetize later, loyalty is a rounding error) arrive in the film as seduction and operate as solvent on every Harvard arrangement. The Valley's function is the story's second education of its founder: Harvard taught Mark that rooms matter; the Valley teaches him that rooms can be owned — and that the price of the lesson is the friendship that financed the tuition. The Valley ends the film ascendant and unexamined, the archive files it as the story's most prophetic faction: the future's operating system, installed over a deposition, still running.

THE LAW

The depositions

The legal process as the film's frame and final faction: two conference rooms in which the creation myth is retold under oath, by interested parties, at $890 an hour — a mechanism the film treats with a dramatist's gratitude and a historian's suspicion, since every flashback the depositions produce is somebody's testimony. The law's function is the story's epistemology made furniture: the truth of Facebook's founding is not recoverable, only settleable — and the settlements ($65 million, an undisclosed sum, a restored title) are its verdicts, priced rather than decided. The law ends the film as it began, billing, the archive files it as the story's most honest faction: the only one that admits, in its own paperwork, that it deals in money rather than truth.

Theft, friendship and the price of both

The film's factional design is a set of rooms, each with its own currency: Harvard's is belonging, the dorm's is loyalty, the Valley's is equity, the law's is settlement — and the plot is the story of a fortune being converted, room by room, at worsening rates for the people who started with the unconvertible currencies. Every faction's fate follows its exchange rate: the clubs' belonging is devalued by the bigger room; the dorm's loyalty is diluted to 0.03 percent; the Valley's equity compounds to billions; and the law converts everyone's claims, impartially, into the only currency it holds. The archive files the design as the film's coldest symmetry: a story about the invention of "friend" as a unit of account, told in the rooms where the unit was priced.

07 / WORLD & SETTING

World & Setting

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Harvard, 2003 — the last autumn of the old internet

A campus of house facebooks in print, dial-up-era social rituals and final-club doors — photographed by Fincher in ambers and dark woods, a world lit like a members' club at closing time. The setting's function is the story's before-picture: a social order that is entirely physical (who is seen, where, with whom) on the eve of its digitization by one of its own rejects. The archive files the campus as the decade's most elegiac location: an Eden of exclusion, about to be paved by its serpent's website, and photographed — this is the film's mercy — as genuinely beautiful, so that the audience understands what the clubs' chosen were defending, and what it cost everyone else.

The deposition rooms — where myths go to be billed

The frame world's fluorescent conference rooms, years later: glass, carpet, court reporters, the creation retold at billable speed. The rooms' function is the story's epistemology as set design — the past exists here only as testimony, exhibits and objections, and the film's flashbacks keep the rooms' lighting in their conscience: every golden dorm scene is something a plaintiff or a defendant is saying. The archive files the rooms as the film's most important location after Kirkland House: the place where the world's friendship was converted, line by line, into its settlement value — and where, in the last scene, the converter sits alone, refreshing.

08 / FACEMASH, THE DILUTION & THE DEPOSITIONS

Facemash, the Dilution & the Depositions

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Facemash — the founding cruelty

The film's first system: a night's drunken code that scraped the houses' facebooks and rated women against each other like livestock — 22,000 hits, a crashed network, a disciplinary hearing. Facemash's function is the story's original sin and its product thesis in one: it proves the demand (Harvard will stay up all night to rank and be ranked) and it establishes the founder's method — other people's faces, other people's data, his platform — at the exact scale where it still looks like a prank. The archive files it as the decade's most loaded origin scene: the whole subsequent empire, including its ethics, is present in embryo in a dorm room at 2 a.m., with a blog post about a girl still open in another window.

TheFacebook — exclusivity as a feature

The product itself: a clean, Harvard-only network whose genius, in the film's filing, is the door — you must have the right email, know the right people, be inside — the final clubs' scarcity rebuilt as software, by the boy the clubs excluded. The site's function is the story's central irony given an interface: Mark does not abolish the room system; he scales it, until the room contains the planet and he holds the only key that was ever the point. The archive files the launch as the film's quietest set-piece: no explosion, no speech — a website going live in a dorm room, and the world, school by school, walking in.

The dilution — betrayal by paperwork

The film's coldest mechanism: a restructuring in which new shares are issued to everyone except the CFO — whose stake falls from 34 percent to 0.03 percent — executed through documents Eduardo signed at a moment of trust, on the assurance they were routine. The dilution's function is the story's doctrine of modern betrayal: no confrontation, no raised voices, no theft in the night — just a signature stack, a lawyer's errand, and a friendship converted to a rounding error by instruments its victim believed were housekeeping. The archive files it as the decade's most chilling boardroom sequence: the murder weapon is a signature, the murderer is a process, and the corpse is a percentage.

The algorithm — Eduardo's contribution

The matching logic Eduardo adapted from his chess-rating work — the Elo-style system that ranked Facemash's contests and fed TheFacebook's early machinery — is the film's smallest system and its most contested atom: the co-founder's intellectual fingerprint, produced in the deposition like a birth certificate. Its function is the story's reminder that founding is a plural act whatever the mythology later says: the money, the algorithm, the bank account and the business sense all have one name on them, and it is not the name on the building. The archive files it as the film's most quietly devastating exhibit: a few lines of math, offered in evidence, as proof that a friendship was also a company.

The refresh

10 / THEMATIC ANALYSIS

Themes & Analysis

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Exclusion and the bigger room — invention as revenge

The film's governing theme is its founder's wound: every door in Mark's world is a final club's — guarded, inherited, amused at his knocking — and his life's work is the construction of a room so large that the doorkeepers become his tenants. The theme is worked out in the product's own design: Facebook does not abolish exclusivity; it industrializes it (the Harvard email, the invitation, the friend request as the universal punch card) — so that the revenge and the product are the same object, and the founder's psychology is legible in his interface. The film's sophistication is its refusal to grade the motive: the wound produces both the cruelty (Facemash's rated faces) and the wonder (a planet's friendships, hosted) — the same boy, the same night, the same code. The reach is the story's standing audit for every builder in its audience: what door are you still trying to get into, and is the thing you're building a room of your own — or a bigger version of theirs, with your name on the lease and their rules in the walls?

Friendship as a currency — the invention of the unit

The film's second theme is its title's irony, developed as economics: Facebook's founding act is the conversion of friendship into a countable, tradable, displayable unit — and the film tells that story through friendships being converted, one by one, into their own units: equity percentages, settlement figures, deposition hours. The theme is worked out in the parallel ledgers: as the platform's friend-count compounds into the hundreds of millions, the founder's friend-count depreciates to the final scene's one pending request — the film's arithmetic is exact and pitiless, and it never states it, trusting the audience to run the numbers the title cards provide. Its proposition is the era's central question in narrative form: when a human relation becomes a metric, what is the metric's exchange rate against the relation — and who, in any given company, is holding Eduardo's side of the ledger? The reach is the story's counsel to every founder and every friend of one: the conversion will be offered, in documents, at moments of trust. Read the documents.

Testimony — the truth is whatever the code did

The film's third theme is its structure: a story told entirely through depositions — interested, lawyered, self-serving accounts — in which the flashbacks the audience watches are, formally, somebody's version. The theme is worked out in the film's even-handedness of doubt: the Winklevosses' decency and Eduardo's warmth are as much exhibits as Mark's coldness, and the film's only uncontested evidence is behavioral — the code shipped, the shares moved, the request refreshed. Its proposition is a historian's, delivered as a thriller's: for the founding events of the modern world, there is no record, only testimony and artifacts — and the artifacts (the site, the cap table, the fortune) are the only witnesses that cannot be coached. The reach is the story's standing instruction for consuming every founder myth since, including its own: discount the deposition, audit the artifact. What the code did is the biography; the rest is settlement negotiations.

The asshole and the nerd — Erica's hypothesis

The film's fourth theme is its first scene's sentence, which the entire narrative is structured as an experiment to test: Mark's belief that his social failures are the world's misreading of a nerd, against Erica's counter-claim that they are the accurate reading of a character. The theme is worked out in the evidence's accumulation: every triumph supplies a fresh exhibit — the blog, the stalling, the dilution's silence — and the defense's best witness is Marylin Delpy's closing amendment (not an asshole; trying so hard to be), which the film offers as the most merciful available verdict and lets the audience grade. Its proposition is a moralist's inside a technologist's story: that the traits the new economy rewards (speed, certainty, the treatment of people as systems) are the very traits the old vocabulary filed under character flaws — and that the re-filing, however profitable, does not change the conduct's price in the only rooms that were ever the point. The reach is the story's question to its whole generation of builders: which hypothesis is your own life currently testing, and whose Erica have you stopped refreshing for an answer?

11 / FULL SPOILER CONCLUSION

Ending Explained

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL THE ENDING

The end is two settlements and a refresh. The Winklevoss claim settles for $65 million; Eduardo's settles for an undisclosed sum and the restoration of his name as co-founder — title cards delivered over the final scene, in which Mark, alone in the conference room after the depositions, sends a friend request to Erica Albright and refreshes the page, waiting. The last title card notes he is the world's youngest self-made billionaire. The request is not answered on screen.

What the ending establishes

  • Both lawsuits end as settlements — priced, not decided: the law converts the founding's contested truths into figures, and the figures are the verdicts history gets.
  • Eduardo's restoration is nominal and real at once: his name returns to the masthead he was diluted out of — the friendship's only recoverable asset being, fittingly, a credit line.
  • Parker's fall completes the seduction's arc: the ethos that froze Eduardo discards its own author within the year — the Valley's loyalty filed, at last, as a rounding error even for its prophet.
  • The final refresh establishes the film's arithmetic: 500 million friends, one pending request — the founder's wealth and the founder's want, posted in the same frame, unreconciled.
  • Marylin's verdict stands as the film's closing assessment of its protagonist: not an asshole, but trying so hard to be — the most merciful reading the evidence supports, delivered by the law's youngest officer, for free.

What remains uncertain

The film's events are a dramatization, and its central questions — what Mark intended in the six weeks of stalling, what he understood of the dilution, what the friendship's dissolution cost him in any currency he recognizes — are left, by design and by law, unsettled; the real parties' accounts differ from the film's and from each other's. The archive files the uncertainty as the story's final honesty: a founding myth with two depositions and no verdict, whose only undisputed artifact is the platform itself — still running, still counting friends, in the pocket of everyone who watches the film about it.

12 / PURPOSES OF THE STORY

Moral of The Story

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

The Social Network uses a breakup, a dorm-room launch and two deposition rooms to examine exclusion, invention and the conversion of friendship into a unit of account: a founder who built the world's room because the existing rooms wouldn't have him, a co-founder diluted by signature at a moment of trust, and an empire priced, at the end, against one unanswered friend request. Through the final clubs, the paperwork and the refresh, the film argues that ideas are abundant and execution is the deed; that betrayal in the modern economy arrives as documents, not daggers; and that no accumulation converts, at any rate yet posted, into being liked by the specific people whose liking was the original point.

Why tell this story?

Because the founding of Facebook is the creation myth of the world the audience now lives in, and Fincher and Sorkin tell it as a classical tragedy of friendship rather than a business case — because the business case is the tragedy's camouflage. They tell it through depositions because that is how the modern world actually processes its myths: under oath, at billable rates, into settlements. They decline to settle the theft question because the question is the story — an economy in which the idea, the execution and the ownership have come permanently unstuck from one another, and in which every participant's account is an exhibit. And they end it on a refresh because the platform's founder, waiting for one girl's acceptance, is the whole civilization they are documenting in a single image: infinitely connected, specifically unaccepted, checking again.

1. Read the documents at the moment of trust

The dilution is the film's first counsel, its most practical, for everyone who will ever be handed a signature stack by people they love — in a startup, a family business, a marriage's paperwork: the moment of maximum trust is the moment of maximum exposure, and it is precisely then that reading feels like betrayal. The counsel is a rule of hygiene that the film prices at 34 percent of a fortune: read everything, at signature speed, with counsel, especially when the presenter says it is routine — because routine is the dilution's native costume, and the friendship's health is not protected by your blindness but by the documents' fairness, which your reading is the only audit of. Eduardo's decency is not in question in the film; his reading is. Do not let your decency be quoted, later, as the reason you cannot have read.

2. Audit the artifact, discount the deposition

The film's structure is its second counsel, for everyone consuming a founding myth — in business, in families, in the news: every account arrives as testimony, interested and lawyered, including the film's own. The counsel is an evidentiary discipline: weight the artifacts (what shipped, what the cap table says, who was in the room at the end) over the accounts, and hold the undecidable as undecidable rather than settling it by sympathy — the twins' manners, Eduardo's warmth and Mark's brilliance are all exhibits, not verdicts. Applied to one's own history, the discipline is uncomfortable and clarifying in equal measure: your family's founding stories have plaintiffs too. Check the paperwork of your own mythology before you repeat it at another generation's deposition.

3. Know which room you're building

The final clubs are the film's third counsel, for every builder whose engine is exclusion — the rejected applicant, the passed-over partner, the founder pitching against the incumbents who laughed: the wound is a legitimate fuel and a dangerous architect. The counsel is a design review, conducted early and honestly: is the thing you're building a new room, with new rules — or the old room, enlarged, with the same door and your hand on it? Mark's platform does both, which is the film's whole ambivalence about him, and the audit is available for any venture: examine the interface for the wound's fingerprints — who is rated, who is admitted, whose acceptance the whole structure is silently addressed to. Build the room anyway, if the room is worth building. But know whose verdict the refresh button is for, because you will be pressing it at the end, whatever the valuation.

4. Keep one friend off the cap table

Eduardo's epitaph — I was your only friend; you had one friend — is the film's fourth counsel, addressed to founders and to the merely ambitious: the conversion of every relationship into an instrument is not a strategy but a countdown. The counsel is a portfolio rule for a life: maintain at least one relationship that is structurally useless — no equity, no network value, no audience — and protect its uselessness as the asset it is, because it is the only holding whose value the depositions cannot reach. The film's arithmetic is the tuition: Mark ends with every convertible asset maximized and the unconvertible one pending, unanswered, at the speed of a refresh. Whatever your empire, the audit at the end is the same two columns. Fund the second column on purpose, in advance, while the people in it still answer quickly.

5. Ideas are exhibits; execution is the deed

The Winklevoss claim is the film's fifth counsel, a two-edged instrument for creators and claimants alike: the twins genuinely had the idea, and genuinely could not build it, and the world — this is the film's coldest finding — pays for the building. The counsel cuts both ways, as it should: if you have the idea, ship or be shipped past, because the gentlemen's code of patience is a strategy for a world that no longer clears its throat before eating your lunch; and if you are the builder, know that the idea's authors will be in the deposition room forever, at $65 million of being partly right — so write your own conduct to survive their testimony. Execution is the deed, but the deed is read aloud, later, by everyone you executed past. Build like it.

6. Let your Marylin tell you

Marylin's verdict — not an asshole, trying so hard to be — is the film's sixth counsel, for everyone whose self-account is maintained at deposition quality: keep, in your life, one person licensed to deliver the merciful-accurate assessment — the friend, the partner, the counsel — whose job is neither prosecution nor defense but the sentence that fits the evidence with love in it. The counsel is a hiring decision for a life: Mark has lawyers by the roomful and exactly one Marylin, encountered by accident, in an empty conference room, after the money has settled everything except the question she answers. Do not wait for the accident. The verdict is available earlier, cheaper, and in time to amend the conduct it describes — which is, the film notes in its final kindness, more than its protagonist ever managed to arrange, with all the world's friends at his disposal.

The moral of the story is…

Read the paperwork at the moment of trust; audit artifacts over testimony; know whose room you're building and whose verdict your refresh is for — and keep one friend off the cap table, because the final accounting of every empire is two columns, and only one of them settles.

The Social Network teaches that betrayal in the modern economy arrives as documents at moments of trust; that execution is the deed but conduct is the testimony; and that a man can connect the entire world and still be waiting, alone in a conference room, for the one acceptance the whole structure was built to earn.

The film's final question is therefore not whether the idea was stolen — the settlement priced that, without deciding it. It is what your own Facemash night built; whose Eduardo financed it; what your dilution documents would look like read aloud; and whether, at your own conference table at the end of everything, the request you are refreshing is one you could simply have answered, years ago, by being the friend the empire replaced.

Editorial note: This is an interpretive reading of the film's themes, not a claim about every private intention of its creators or a prediction of future franchise events.
