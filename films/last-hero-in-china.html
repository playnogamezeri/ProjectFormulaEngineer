01 / FILM IDENTITY

Overview

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Last Hero in China (黃飛鴻之鐵雞鬥蜈蚣, literally "Wong Fei-hung: Iron Rooster vs. Centipede"; also released as Claws of Steel, Deadly China Hero and Iron Rooster vs. Centipede) is a 1993 Hong Kong martial arts comedy written and directed by Wong Jing, produced by Jet Li, with martial arts choreography by Yuen Woo-ping. It is a derivative — part spin-off, part parody — of the Once Upon a Time in China series, with Li reprising Wong Fei-hung after his departure from Tsui Hark's films. Rent pressure forces Wong to move his school and the Po-chi-lam clinic, and his disciples Leung Foon (Bryan Leung) and "Bucktooth" So (Dicky Cheung) secure a vacant house that turns out to stand next to a "love hotel" run by Mass Tar Wong (Natalis Chan) — an address the master finds unacceptable and his students do not. Worse, Canton's corrupt police chief Lui Yat-siu (Alan Chui), a member of the Boxer Association, is in league with the monks of Nun-yan Temple, led by Master Liu Hung (Gordon Liu), who kidnap young women and sell them into prostitution in South Asia. The swordswoman Ti Yin-er (Sharla Cheung), hunting her missing sister, is injured investigating the temple; Wong's intervention gets him framed for an unprovoked attack on the monks, and although local businessmen pressure the magistrate into releasing him, a confederate's poison leaves him temporarily deaf. The conflict climaxes at a lion dance competition, where Lui's men operate a giant flame-shooting metal centipede and Wong — deducing that nature provides the answer — fights them dressed as a rooster, iron beak and claws, clucking: a direct homage to the 1956 film of the same Chinese title, in which Kwan Tak-hing played Wong Fei-hung. In the final fight, Wong's toes are broken attempting his Shadowless Kick against Lui, and he finishes the chief with drunken boxing — then rebukes the foreign official Lui tried to assassinate for selling the opium that deafens China's children, and marches away victorious.

RELEASE

April 1, 1993

An April Fools' Day release for the series' comic shadow — distributed by Golden Harvest, which also distributed the films it parodies.

THE HOMAGE

1956, Restaged

The rooster-versus-centipede duel restages the 1956 Kwan Tak-hing film of the same Chinese title — Li, in costume, saluting the Wong Fei-hung of the previous generation.

THE TONE

Parody with Teeth

Broader slapstick and stronger violence than the Tsui Hark films, with Li — amid the antics — playing Wong Fei-hung completely straight, the still center of the farce.

Place in the culture

The film lives in the culture as the beloved heresy of the Wong Fei-hung canon: the installment that admits the series is also a comedy, and proves the folk hero can survive parody because his dignity is structural, not tonal. Its Easter eggs — a Lifebuoy poster in the 19th century, the Monkey King's staff, Ne Zha's Universe Ring — file it as the canon's own wink at itself. For this Galaxy it is the hinge of Li's 1993: the same year produced the sectarian epic, the twin tragedy and the succession farce, and here is the folk master, deafened and dressed as poultry, out-arguing an empire's drug trade in the final scene.

03 / NARRATIVE SUMMARY

Story Synopsis

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL FULL STORY SUMMARY

Po-chi-lam is priced out of its premises. Wong Fei-hung's school has outgrown its rooms, and disciples Leung Foon and Bucktooth So triumphantly secure a vacant house — which stands, to the master's horror and the students' delight, next to a "love hotel" run by the pimp Mass Tar Wong. The neighborhood is the least of it: Canton's police chief Lui Yat-siu, an openly corrupt Boxer Association man, is partnered with the monks of Nun-yan Temple, whose abbot-class master Liu Hung runs a kidnapping pipeline — young women seized and sold into prostitution in South Asia. The swordswoman Ti Yin-er, searching for her missing sister, infiltrates the temple and is badly injured by Lui. Wong's disciples bungle a rescue of the captured girls; Wong intervenes at the temple himself, and Lui frames the havoc as Wong's unprovoked attack on holy men. The magistrate, pressured by local businessmen, releases Wong — but the conspiracy's answer is quieter: a poison that leaves the master temporarily deaf.

The war continues at a disadvantage of one sense. Lui attempts to assassinate a foreign official, and Wong intervenes; and the conflict comes to its famous head at a lion dance competition, where Lui's men take the field inside a giant metallic centipede, flame-shooting and many-legged. Wong deduces that nature provides the answer to a centipede, and enters the contest as its natural predator: a rooster — comb, iron beak and metal spurs — flying, kicking, scratching and clucking his way through the machine in a parody of the lion dance that restages, costume for costume, the 1956 Kwan Tak-hing film of the same name. In the final fight against Lui himself, Wong attempts his signature Shadowless Kick and has his toes broken by the chief — and adapts on the spot, finishing the fight with drunken boxing. Victorious, Wong does not let the rescued foreign official leave unexamined: he rebukes him for selling opium, the trade that is deafening China's children — a deafness the master has now personally sampled — and marches away with his companions.

12 / PURPOSES OF THE STORY

Moral of The Story

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Last Hero in China uses a brothel adjacency, a deafening and a fancy-dress war to examine structural dignity, respectable crime, and the ethics of the rescue: a master who keeps every commitment inside a farce; a syndicate staffed entirely by the honored; a signature kick broken and improved upon; a saved official indicted at his own farewell. Through the clinic, the temple and the festival, the film argues that dignity is a set of commitments, not a tone; that a city's worst commerce hides in its most respected costumes; and that gratitude must never be allowed to settle an indictment.

Why tell this story?

Because the Wong Fei-hung canon needed to prove its hero could survive his own parody — and because Wong Jing, of all directors, understood that the folk master's dignity was never in the solemnity but in the bookkeeping: who he treats, who he refuses, what he says to the drug dealer he just saved. The archive files the result as the canon's most cheerful installment and, in its final scene, one of its most serious.

1. Keep the commitments; let the tone go

Wong's straightness is the film's first counsel, for every professional in farcical conditions: dignity is structural. The counsel is a conduct doctrine with the rooster costume as its standard: when your setting turns absurd — the reorganization, the costume, the undignified assignment — audit what is actually yours to keep: the standard of the work, the treatment of the casualties, the refusal that defines you. Those survive any lighting. The rest — being taken seriously, looking like the master — was always rented. Wong Fei-hung in a chicken suit, indicting an empire's drug trade, is the genre's portrait of a man who owned the right things.

2. Search the honored addresses first
