01 / FILM IDENTITY

Overview

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

The Thunderstorm (雷雨) is a 1957 Hong Kong Cantonese drama directed by Ng Wui, written by Ching Kong (Cheng Kang), produced by Wah Kiu — one of Cantonese cinema's "Big Four" companies, for whom it was a debut production — and released March 14, 1957. It adapts Thunderstorm (1934), Cao Yu's first and most famous play, the foundational tragedy of modern Chinese theatre; where the play compresses its catastrophe into a single day, the film reorders the story chronologically and stretches it across twenty years. Thirty years before the main action, the young mine-owner Chow Pok-yuen (Lo Tun) took the maid Lui Shi-ping (Pak Yin) as his lover, then cast her out — pregnant, on a snowy New Year's Eve, so his family could marry him advantageously — and told the world she had drowned herself. She survived, married the Chow family's servant Lu Kuei (Ng Wui, the director, in the role), and raised a daughter, Tse Fung (Mui Yee), who has now — in the tragedy's central mechanism — become a maid in the Chow house herself, ignorant of her parentage. Of the house's two sons, the elder, Chow Ping (Cheung Ying), Shi-ping's own son by Pok-yuen, raised in the house that expelled his mother, is conducting a desperate affair with his stepmother Fan Yee (Wong Man-lei) and now loves Tse Fung — his own half-sister, unknown to both. The younger son, Chow Chung (Bruce Lee) — the idealistic student, all ardor and reformist innocence — loves Tse Fung too, and means to marry her across the class line his father polices. When Shi-ping returns to the house (drawn by her daughter's employment, as fate files its paperwork) the buried generation surfaces: the recognitions cascade, Fan Yee's jealousy arms itself with the family's secrets, and in the storm of the title, Tse Fung and Chow Chung are electrocuted by a fallen wire in the flooded garden, Chow Ping shoots himself, and Shi-ping and Fan Yee are left mad among the survivors — Pok-yuen presiding over the wreck of the house his one act of expulsion built. Lee's role is supporting but, as the Hong Kong Film Archive notes, singular: the only film in which he wears the Zhongshan suit, playing "an idealistic student" with a "controlled performance" that "showcases the future kungfu fighter's gentler side" — no fight scenes whatsoever, in the role historians call his first adult part. Dubbed into Mandarin and re-released in the 1970s on the strength of his superstardom, the film stands in this Galaxy as the childhood shelf's proof of range: the delinquent of The Orphan and the gentleman of The Thunderstorm are the same actor, three years apart, and the second performance is the one with no fists in it.

The play that founded a theatre, the role that founded an actor

Cao Yu wrote Thunderstorm at twenty-three, and it has been called the most popular play of modern China — a machine of recognitions in which a patriarch's youthful crime returns, one generation later, wearing his own children's faces. Wah Kiu announced the film in August 1956 as one of its first two productions, assembling the era's prestige ensemble (Cheung Ying, Pak Yin, Wong Man-lei) around the adaptation's expansion: the play's single day unfolded into a chronological twenty years, with the original affair and its aftermath dramatized rather than merely confessed. For Lee, sixteen and typecast as delinquents and suffering children (the Archive's summary of his two stock roles), Chow Chung was an exit visa: a refined, idealistic young gentleman, played — in the Archive's phrase — with control, and noticed by historians ever since as the counterpoint performance of his youth. The film's second production, decades later, was its own re-release: dubbed into Mandarin in the 1970s, when Lee's adult superstardom made a supporting performance in a 1957 tragedy a marketable headline — the archive files the dub as the era's plainest admission of what the boy in the Zhongshan suit had become.

03 / NARRATIVE SUMMARY

Story Synopsis

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL FULL STORY SUMMARY

The tragedy's engine is a single expulsion, compounding. Pok-yuen's casting-out of Shi-ping — done for a marriage, papered over with a false drowning — does not end the affair's story; it merely defers it to the children, with interest. The son he kept (Ping) grows up in the house of the crime, drawn first to his stepmother and then, in the play's cruelest gear, to his own unrecognized half-sister; the daughter he never knew (Tse Fung) is delivered by poverty into service in the same house; the son of the second marriage (Chung) falls for the same girl with a reformer's clean intentions — the one innocent love in the building, and therefore the one the machinery is certain to reach. Shi-ping's return — a mother visiting her daughter's place of employment — closes the circuit: every secret in the house is a member of one family, and the recognitions, once started, cannot be stopped at the convenient ones.

Fan Yee, the second wife — married as a girl to an old man's house, in love with her stepson, discarded by him for a maid — is the tragedy's accelerant: her jealousy weaponizes the secrets, forcing the confrontations into the storm night. The catastrophe is electrical and exact: Tse Fung, fleeing the house's revelations, touches a fallen live wire in the flooded garden; Chow Chung, running to save her, takes the current with her — the innocent and the beloved dying of the house's own infrastructure, in its own weather. Ping, confronted with the full registry of his loves (stepmother, half-sister, a mother expelled), shoots himself. The survivors are the two mothers, mad, and the patriarch, sane, in possession of the house — Cao Yu's verdict, preserved by the film: the man who authored the catastrophe is the one left to administer its estate. The archive files the ending as the Chinese stage's founding lesson in compound interest: a buried affair does not decay; it appreciates, in the children's names.

The full narrative summary is an editorial paraphrase, not dialogue or a scene-by-scene transcript.

12 / PURPOSES OF THE STORY

Moral of The Story

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

The Thunderstorm uses a mine-owner's household to examine buried generations, the two burials of women, and the house's own current: a false drowning that appreciates for thirty years; a wife expelled and a wife imprisoned, filed at last in the same column; and a fallen wire that collects the debt from the innocent. Through the parlor, the servants' registry and the flooded garden, the film argues that a buried fact is a loan against the future at an interest rate set by silence; that respectability's procedures dispose of people as surely as its storms do; and that a house's hazards, like its secrets, are collected in bad weather by whoever is standing closest.

Why tell this story?

Because Cao Yu, at twenty-three, built the machine that Chinese theatre has run ever since — and because Ng Wui's film, expanding its day into twenty years, shows the fuse burning at full length, with a sixteen-year-old future legend dying gently in the rain at the end of it. The archive files the result as the childhood shelf's tragedy: a Zhongshan suit, a flooded garden, and a current, earthed.

1. Price the disclosure today

The false drowning is the film's first counsel, for every keeper of a founding secret — the family's, the firm's, the nation's: Pok-yuen's lie was cheap in its first year and cost three lives in its thirtieth, and the difference is the interest. The counsel is a disclosure doctrine with the storm as its standard: for each buried fact in your keeping, maintain a current price for telling it now, voluntarily, in dry weather — and compare it, annually, with the storm price, which is the same fact told by catastrophe, to everyone, at once, in the worst order. Almost every secret in this film was affordable in year one. None was in year thirty.
