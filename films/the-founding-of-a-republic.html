01 / FILM IDENTITY

Overview

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

The Founding of a Republic (Chinese: 建国大业； pinyin: Jiànguó Dàyè) is a 2009 Chinese historical epic co-directed by Huang Jianxin and Han Sanping, chairman of the state-owned China Film Group, commissioned to mark the 60th anniversary of the People's Republic of China. It portrays the final years of the Chinese Communist Revolution, from the end of the Second Sino-Japanese War in 1945 to the proclamation of the PRC on October 1, 1949: the Chongqing negotiations between Mao Zedong (Tang Guoqiang) and Chiang Kai-shek (Zhang Guoli), the collapse of the peace, the civil war's great campaigns, the Nationalists' retreat, and the convening of the Chinese People's Political Consultative Conference in Beiping, where the new state's framework is assembled with the democratic parties. The film's production method is itself its most famous feature: more than 170 prominent actors from mainland China, Hong Kong and Taiwan appear — Andy Lau, Ge You, Jackie Chan, Donnie Yen, Zhang Ziyi, Jiang Wen, Chen Kaige among them — many in cameo roles and reportedly working without pay, turning the epic into what reviewers called the most lavish star-spotting game in mainland cinema history. Jet Li's part is one of these cameos: he appears briefly as a military officer who declares his refusal to fight a civil war, and is gone. This archive records the film in Jet Li's filmography at its true scale — a single scene in an ensemble of hundreds — while documenting the whole, as the Galaxy's other cameo entries do.

Place in the culture

The film lives in the culture as the prototype of the modern Chinese anniversary blockbuster — the state-commissioned "main melody" epic rebuilt as a commercial event, a model its sequels and successors (including The Founding of a Party) would follow. Its box-office record reset expectations for what an official production could earn. For this Galaxy, its place is documentary: it is the slightest of Jet Li's appearances in the run — slighter even than his single movement in The Expendables 2 — and the archive files it as the limiting case of a filmography: a credit that is a gesture, the star lending his face to a national anniversary for one scene and no fee.

03 / NARRATIVE SUMMARY

Story Synopsis

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL FULL STORY SUMMARY

In 1945, with Japan defeated, Mao Zedong flies to Chongqing to negotiate with Chiang Kai-shek — a peace neither side's armies honor for long. The film follows the double track of the next four years: at the tables, truces, assemblies and the Communists' courtship of the democratic parties and figures such as Soong Ching-ling; in the field, the Nationalists' corruption, inflation and collapsing morale against the Communists' advancing campaigns. Chiang Ching-kuo's doomed attempt to reform Shanghai's economy by force stands for the regime's larger failure: a government that can no longer discipline its own. Within this sweep, the film's hundreds of cameos pass like a roll call of the era — among them a Nationalist officer, played by Jet Li, who declares his refusal to fight a civil war.

Chiang, his armies broken in the great campaigns of 1948–49, resigns the presidency; Li Zongren's acting presidency cannot hold the Yangtze line, and the People's Liberation Army crosses the river and takes Nanjing. In Beiping, Mao and the Communists convene the Chinese People's Political Consultative Conference with the democratic parties, settling the new state's name, capital, flag and anthem. On October 1, 1949, from the Tiananmen rostrum, Mao proclaims the People's Republic of China — the founding the film was commissioned, sixty years on, to celebrate.

04 / STORY STRUCTURE

Story & Chronology

The film unfolds as a negotiation, a war and an assembly. The sequence below follows the film's narrative order.

Act I — Chongqing, 1945

Mao negotiates with Chiang under the eyes of the world; the peace is signed and begins failing at once; the Communists court the democratic parties.

Act II — The civil war

Corruption and inflation rot the Nationalist state; Chiang Ching-kuo's Shanghai reforms fail; the great campaigns of 1948–49 break the Nationalist armies; Chiang resigns.

Act III — Beiping, 1949

The Yangtze is crossed and Nanjing falls; the CPPCC assembles the new state; on October 1, Mao proclaims the People's Republic from Tiananmen.

10 / THEMATIC ANALYSIS

Themes & Analysis

Legitimacy is assembled, not seized

The film's governing theme is its double plot: the war is won by armies, but the founding is won by coalition. The theme is worked out in the courtship of the democratic parties until the audience holds its doctrine of the assembled mandate: durable power is ratified by the persuaded, not merely imposed by the victorious — the side that treats the uncommitted as the prize, years before the victory, owns the peace as well as the war. Its proposition reaches every founder and campaigner in the audience. The reach is the story's question to every builder of a new order: are you collecting your signatures in advance, like the men of Beiping — or planning to announce legitimacy from a rostrum nobody agreed to fill?

The incumbent's wasting assets

The film's second theme is the Nationalists' paradox: they hold everything — the state, the army, the recognition, the treasury — and lose. The theme is worked out in inflation figures and failed reforms until the audience holds its doctrine of depreciating incumbency: advantages held by a rotting administration are wasting assets, spent by time itself, while the insurgent's disadvantages compound in the other direction. Its proposition reaches every market leader in the audience. The reach is the story's question to every holder of a commanding position: which of your advantages is a Chiang asset — real on paper, depreciating daily — and what is your Ching-kuo reform, and why is it scheduled for next year?

Reform vetoed by its own house

The film's third theme is Chiang Ching-kuo's Shanghai crackdown: the capable reformer broken by the regime he serves. The theme is worked out in the campaign's collapse until the audience holds its doctrine of the borrowed broom: a cleanup whose authority derives from the corrupt order can clean exactly as far as that order's interests permit — the reformer's ceiling is set by his sponsors, not his courage. Its proposition reaches every hired reformer in the audience. The reach is the story's question to every executive brought in to fix a house: who, precisely, can veto your broom — and have they already, in a meeting you weren't invited to?

The ensemble as the argument

The theme is worked out in the audience's star-spotting pleasure until the audience holds its doctrine of form as evidence: a film about assembly that is itself an assembly makes its claim twice — once in the story, once in the credits — and the second telling may be the more persuasive, because it is verifiable in the room. 12 / PURPOSES OF THE STORY

Moral of The Story

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

The Founding of a Republic uses the years 1945–1949 to examine assembled legitimacy, depreciating incumbency, and reform's ceiling: a revolutionary who courts the uncommitted for years before his victory; an incumbent whose every advantage wastes in his hands; a reformer whose broom is vetoed by his own house; and an industry of 170 stars volunteering to re-enact the coalition that founded their state. Through Chongqing, Shanghai and Beiping, the film argues that durable power is ratified by the persuaded; that a rotting administration's assets depreciate on their own schedule; and that commemorative work persuades most when its making enacts its message.

Why tell this story?

Because a sixtieth anniversary required a founding myth in the form its audience actually consumes — the commercial epic — and because the producers understood that the casting could argue what the script could only state. The archive files the result as the Galaxy's largest ensemble and its most honest cameo: Jet Li's three lines are preserved here at the same scale the film gives them.

1. Collect your signatures in advance

The coalition courtship is the film's first counsel, for every founder: legitimacy is accumulated before it is announced. The counsel is an assembly doctrine with Beiping as its standard: spend the war years persuading the uncommitted whose ratification your victory will need, because a founding announced to a hall you never courted is a press release, not a mandate. Mao's men arrived at the CPPCC with the persuading already done. Arrive with yours done.

2. Audit your advantages for rot

Chiang's collapse is the film's second counsel, for every incumbent: an advantage held by a decaying administration is a wasting asset. The counsel is a depreciation doctrine with the Shanghai prices as its standard: list your commanding assets — the brand, the treasury, the establishment's recognition — and ask of each whether your own house is spending it faster than the market is, because the insurgent across the table is compounding while you deliberate. The Nationalists were richer at every point of the war, including the end. Wealth was not the variable.

3. Know who can veto your broom

Ching-kuo's failure is the film's third counsel, for every hired reformer: your ceiling is set by your sponsors' interests, not your mandate's wording. The counsel is a sponsorship doctrine with the Shanghai crackdown as its standard: before accepting the cleanup, name the interests your broom must eventually touch, and get their owners' exposure agreed in writing — or understand that you have been hired as the regime's alibi, and price your name accordingly. He was the ablest man in his government. It was not enough, and it was never going to be.

4. Let the making enact the message

The unpaid ensemble is the film's fourth counsel, for every maker of institutional work: form is the only evidence an audience can verify from its seat. The counsel is an enactment doctrine with the credits as its standard: when your subject is unity, assemble; when it is thrift, be thrifty on screen and off; when it is courage, take a visible risk — because the audience will believe the production's behavior long after the script's speeches fade. The 170 names are remembered when the dialogue is not. Make your credits argue.

5. Give the small refusal its scene

Li's cameo is the film's fifth counsel, for every teller of large histories: the cost of the grand narrative is carried by unnamed people, and one honest small scene can hold it. The counsel is a scale doctrine with the officer's lines as its standard: inside your epic, budget one moment for the person with no stake in the outcome except his conscience — the clerk, the officer, the bystander who says no — because audiences measure a history's honesty by how it treats its smallest parts. The film gives him seconds. They are among the seconds this archive exists to keep.

6. Found things in halls, not only on fields

The CPPCC is the film's sixth counsel, its benediction, for every victor: the war decides who may convene; the convening decides what the victory means. The counsel is a ratification doctrine with the assembly's minutes as its standard: whatever you win — a market, an argument, an organization — finish it with a procedure the losers and the uncommitted can recognize as a founding, because unratified victories must be re-won every year, and ratified ones compound. The republic was proclaimed in a sentence, but it was founded in the hall. Book the hall.

The moral of the story is…

Collect your signatures in advance; audit your advantages for rot; know who can veto your broom — and let the making enact the message.

The Founding of a Republic teaches that durable power is ratified by the persuaded; that a rotting administration's assets depreciate on their own schedule; and that commemorative work persuades most when its making enacts its message.

The film's final question is therefore not who wins the war — the film files that under "ask the Yangtze." It is whose signatures your own founding is currently missing; which of your advantages is depreciating in your own house's hands; and whether your credits would argue your case if your script were lost.
