01 / FILM IDENTITY

Overview

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Spirited Away (Japanese: Sen to Chihiro no Kamikakushi, "Sen and Chihiro's Spiriting Away") is a 2001 Japanese animated fantasy film written and directed by Hayao Miyazaki for Studio Ghibli. Released in Japan on July 20, 2001, it grossed ¥31.68 billion domestically — the highest-grossing film in Japanese history for nineteen years — and $395.8 million worldwide, and became the first hand-drawn, non-English-language film to win the Academy Award for Best Animated Feature, alongside the Golden Bear at Berlin. Chihiro Ogino (Rumi Hiiragi) is ten years old, moving house, and furious about it — a sullen, frightened, thoroughly ordinary child, which is Miyazaki's point: this is a heroine with no powers, no destiny and no pluck, only a name. Driving to their new home, her parents take a wrong turn into an abandoned theme park — a bubble-economy ruin of fake nostalgia — where a restaurant stall stands loaded with food and no staff; the parents eat, in the film's great primal scene of adult appetite, and are turned into pigs for feasting on the gods' portion. The town wakes at dusk into what it always was: a resort for the kami, the eight million gods of Japanese tradition, and its center is a bathhouse run by the witch Yubaba (Mari Natsuki), who employs the stranded and erases them by contract: she steals Chihiro's name, reissuing her as "Sen," because a worker who forgets her true name can never leave. What follows is the most quietly radical adventure in animation: not a quest for a weapon but a girl's first job — mopping, hauling coal-heated water, serving a stink spirit who turns out to be a polluted river god and pays in gratitude — through which Chihiro accumulates the film's real currency: competence, allies, and an unbroken memory of who she is. Around her labor, the spirit world files its own disorders: Haku (Miyu Irino), the boy-dragon who saved her and serves Yubaba, having forgotten his own name and river; No-Face, the lonely masked spirit who learns the bathhouse's grammar — gold, appetite, being wanted — and rampages through it until Chihiro, the one person who never performed desire for him, leads him out and leaves him, healed, as a spinner's apprentice; and Zeniba, Yubaba's twin, whose cottage at Swamp Bottom is the bathhouse's moral opposite — the same face, run on tea and patience instead of contracts. The climax is not a battle but two acts of memory: Chihiro remembers falling, as a small child, into the Kohaku River — and gives the dragon his name back, Nigihayami Kohaku Nushi — and at the gate, facing Yubaba's final test among a pen of pigs, answers that her parents are not there, which is correct: she has learned to see. The film closes with the story's whole ethic in a single instruction from Haku: don't look back — and with Chihiro, in the car, filed by her parents (who remember nothing) as unchanged, wearing a hair tie woven by her friends, glinting: the one souvenir the spirit world could not tax.

Place in the culture

The film is routinely ranked among the greatest animated films ever made — atop the BBC's poll of the best films of the 21st century's early decades in critics' lists, in the BFI's canon of films for the young — and it carried Japanese animation, permanently, out of the cult category and into the world cinema mainstream: for a generation of viewers everywhere, it was the door. Its heroine's ordinariness became a genre doctrine of its own: the child who prevails by working, remembering and being polite to gods.

The retirement that wasn't, and the river that was

Miyazaki came out of his post-Mononoke retirement to make the film for the ten-year-old daughters of friends — having decided, watching them, that cinema offered young girls no heroine who prevailed by anything but spunk; he would build one whose virtues were labor, manners and memory. The bathhouse was modeled on the historic Dogo Onsen and on the faux-heritage buildings of the Edo-Tokyo Open Air Architectural Museum, where Miyazaki sketched; the abandoned theme park that opens the film was drawn from Japan's bubble-era ruins, and the Kohaku River is autobiography: a real river near Miyazaki's home, paved over for apartments, whose drowning-rescue of a child's shoe the director converted into the film's emotional keystone. Produced for roughly ¥1.9 billion with Ghibli's hand-drawn craft at its zenith (a small, carefully rationed amount of digital assistance), the film became a national phenomenon in Japan — a third of the population saw it — and then, via a Disney-distributed English dub supervised by Pixar's John Lasseter, an international one. Miyazaki did not attend the Academy Awards ceremony in 2003, a silence widely read as protest at the Iraq War; the statuette stands regardless: the only hand-drawn, non-English winner in the category's history.

03 / NARRATIVE SUMMARY

Story Synopsis

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL FULL STORY SUMMARY

The film's first movement is a study in appetite and its taxes: the parents, credit card in hand ("It's an abandoned park — we'll pay later"), eat the gods' food with a consumer's total confidence and are pigs by dusk, while Chihiro — who refuses, out of a child's inarticulate dread — survives by the refusal, and the audience is handed the film's whole moral physics in ten minutes: in this world, you are what you eat, and the bill is species. Haku's bridge-side rescue (breath held, hand held, a berry from the spirit world to keep her from fading) delivers her to the bathhouse's one honest door: Kamaji, the six-armed boiler man, whose gruff machinery of coal and soot-sprites files the film's labor doctrine — everyone here works; the soot carries its own coal or starves — and who sends her up, with a train token forty years kept, to demand a job from Yubaba herself, since asking is the one move the witch's rules cannot refuse. The contract scene is the film's cold center: a signature, a name confiscated character by character until "Chihiro" is "Sen," and Haku's whispered counter-law in the aftermath — they will call you Sen; hold your true name in secret and you hold your way home — which converts the whole adventure into a memory exercise with wages. Sen's apprenticeship under Lin, the bathhouse's weary big-sister attendant, is filmed as pure process: the big tub, the herbal water tokens, the radish spirit's elevator silence — the film finding, in competent work filmed lovingly, a drama most adventure films cannot locate in battle.

The middle movement braids the film's three patients. The stink spirit — a customer so foul the staff revolts — is assigned to Sen, who serves him without flinching, finds the thorn in his side (a bicycle, then the whole archaeology of a landfill: tires, barrels, a river's worth of human rubbish) and pulls it out in the bathhouse's great catharsis, revealing a river god, cleansed, laughing, who pays her with a bitter dango of medicine: the film's thesis as a guest's tip — the pollution was never the god's nature; it was ours, filed in his body. No-Face, admitted by Sen out of rain-night pity, learns the house's grammar too well: gold conjured, frogs eaten, a rampage of appetite that is really a loneliness performing the only desire-language available — until Sen, who alone never wanted his gold, feeds him the river god's medicine and walks him out, vomiting up everything he swallowed, small and quiet again, to be left at Zeniba's cottage doing the one thing the bathhouse never taught: making something, slowly, for no customer. And Haku — stealing Zeniba's seal for Yubaba, cursed by its protection — crashes bleeding into the boiler room, and Sen spends the medicine she was saving for her parents on him instead, the film's moral summit disguised as a logistics decision: the cure goes to the person bleeding in front of you. The train ride that follows — Sen, No-Face, Boh-as-mouse and the Yu-bird, across a flooded plain at dusk, in near silence — is the film's famous still center, Miyazaki's doctrine of ma, the pregnant pause, rendered as a commute; and Zeniba's cottage resolves the seal plot with a grandmother's shrug (the seal is returned; the curse was Yubaba's paperwork; Haku had already stolen her heart's real leverage long ago) and sends Sen home with a woven hair tie: protection, handmade, unpriced. The gate test — a pen of pigs, a contract fulfilled, a witch bound by her own word to let the worker go — is passed not by recognition but by its opposite: "None of these are my parents," the graduate's answer, the eye educated by a season of seeing gods in stink and boys in dragons. Haku, flying her back, receives his name from her memory of a river — Nigihayami Kohaku Nushi — and falls out of the sky in joy, a god repossessed of himself; and the tunnel walk out, the parents unremembering, the car dusty as if an afternoon had passed, closes the film on its doctrine of growth: nothing about Chihiro's world has changed, and the girl walking through it has, entirely — which the film files, in the hair tie's glint, as the only kind of souvenir that cannot be confiscated.

The full narrative summary is an editorial paraphrase, not dialogue or a scene-by-scene transcript.

THE GIRL

Chihiro Ogino ("Sen")

Rumi Hiiragi. A ten-year-old moving house — sullen, timid and entirely unexceptional — who loses her parents, her name and her world in one evening, and recovers all three by the film's radical method: working competently, remembering stubbornly, and being decent to everyone including the monsters. Hiiragi voices her with a whine that seasons, scene by scene, into steadiness, and Chihiro's function is the story's portrait of the heroine as a laborer: no chosen one, no weapon — a mop, a memory, and manners. The archive files her as the decade's most consequential heroine in film: a name, held, against a whole economy of forgetting.

THE DRAGON

Haku (Nigihayami Kohaku Nushi)

Miyu Irino. A boy who is also a white dragon — Yubaba's apprentice and enforcer, who rescues Chihiro at the bridge and teaches her the rules, having long ago traded away the memory of his own name and the river he was god of. Irino voices him with a composed, sorrowful gravity beyond his years, and Haku's function is the story's portrait of the servant as the system's saddest product: the bathhouse's most capable employee is a god who cannot remember being one. The archive files him as the decade's most consequential dragon in film: a river, recovered, from a child's memory of falling.

THE WITCH

Yubaba

Mari Natsuki. The bathhouse's proprietor — a vast-nosed, jewel-loaded witch who runs the gods' resort as a business of contracts, gold and confiscated names, terrifying to her staff and helpless before her giant baby. Natsuki voices her with a chain-smoking, boardroom ferocity, and Yubaba's function is the story's portrait of the employer as a force of nature: she is never defeated, only bound — by her word, her rules and her one maternal weakness — which the film files as the only way such powers are ever handled. The archive files her as the decade's most consequential witch in film: a contract, honored, by its own author.

THE TWIN

Zeniba

Mari Natsuki. Yubaba's identical twin — a witch of the cottage rather than the tower, who receives thieves with tea, forgives the seal's theft in a paragraph, and gives the film's heroine its one handmade gift. Natsuki voices her with the same instrument tuned to patience, and Zeniba's function is the story's portrait of power's other administration: the same face, the same magic, run on hospitality instead of contracts — the film's proof that the witch was a management style, not a species. The archive files her as the decade's most consequential twin in film: a cottage that out-governs a tower.

THE BOILER MAN

Kamaji

Bunta Sugawara. The bathhouse's six-armed boiler keeper — a spider-like old man who feeds the fires with coal and soot-sprites, gruffly adopts Sen at first sight of her persistence, and has kept a train token for forty years against the day someone would need to leave. Sugawara voices him with a gravelly, grandfatherly economy, and Kamaji's function is the story's portrait of the infrastructure worker as the system's secret saint: the whole palace of leisure runs on his basement, and so does its one act of rescue. The archive files him as the decade's most consequential boiler man in film: a token, kept, for forty years, against a granddaughter-shaped emergency.

THE ATTENDANT

Lin

Yumi Tamai. A bathhouse attendant — Sen's assigned big sister, a weasel-spirit in human form who complains about her charge, feeds her, teaches her the work and risks herself in her defense, all while dreaming of a ticket out of the spirit town. Tamai voices her with a brisk, big-sister exasperation that keeps cracking into tenderness, and Lin's function is the story's portrait of the coworker as the workplace's true curriculum: everything Sen survives, she survives the Lin way first. The archive files her as the decade's most consequential attendant in film: a mentor disguised as a colleague with seniority.

THE LONELY SPIRIT

No-Face (Kaonashi)

A masked, translucent spirit — a nobody who drifts into the bathhouse, learns its grammar of gold and appetite from the staff's greed, and swells into its rampaging id, until the one person who wanted nothing from him walks him back to smallness. Voiced in sighs and, when fed, in a frog's stolen croak, No-Face's function is the story's portrait of loneliness in a consumer economy: he is not evil; he is an empty seat at a feast, performing desire because it is the only language the house speaks. The archive files him as the decade's most consequential spirit in film: an appetite, cured, by being wanted for nothing.

THE BABY

Boh

Ryunosuke Kamiki. Yubaba's enormous infant son — kept in a padded nursery, terrified of germs and the outside, who is turned into a mouse by his aunt's spell and, in that small form, sees the world, rides a train and returns home with the film's most quietly complete education. Kamiki voices him with a tyrant's wail that miniaturizes into squeaks, and Boh's function is the story's portrait of the overprotected as the system's hostage class: the proprietor's heir is her one unguarded door, and his shrinking is his rescue. The archive files him as the decade's most consequential baby in film: a prince, improved, by being made small.

THE FATHER

Akio Ogino

Takashi Naito. Chihiro's father — a confident, credit-carded salaryman whose four-wheel-drive curiosity ("Let's explore — we'll pay later") drives the family into the spirit town and whose appetite at the stall signs the contract his daughter must work off. Naito voices him with an affable, unshakeable consumer's assurance, and Akio's function is the story's portrait of the bubble generation's inheritance: a certainty that everything is a service and every bill arrives later, in someone else's name. The archive files him as the decade's most consequential father in an animated film: a feast, charged, to his ten-year-old.

THE MOTHER

Yuko Ogino

Yasuko Sawaguchi. Chihiro's mother — the parent who dismisses her daughter's dread ("Don't be such a scaredy-cat"), joins the feast and exits the film's middle as livestock, returning at the end, memory wiped, to file her changed daughter under "the drive was long." Sawaguchi voices her with a bright, impatient practicality, and Yuko's function is the story's portrait of the adult world's deafness as the child's true starting condition: Chihiro's adventure begins at the exact pitch of not being believed. The archive files her as the decade's most consequential mother in an animated film: a dismissal, answered, by a whole spirit world.

THE FOREMAN

Chichiyaku

Tsunehiko Kamijo. The bathhouse's frog foreman — a bellowing middle manager in a happi coat, who receives guests, bullies staff and files, in one body, the entire hierarchy between the witch and the mop. Kamijo voices him with a festival barker's bluster, and Chichiyaku's function is the story's portrait of the foreman as the system's transmission belt: Yubaba's terror reaches the floor through voices like his, and so does its comedy. The archive files him as the decade's most consequential foreman in film: a hierarchy, audible, at full volume, in an elevator.

THE RIVER GOD

The River Spirit (Okutaresama)

The "stink spirit" — a guest so polluted he is mistaken for a god of filth, who is revealed, when Sen pulls the junk from his body, as a great river god, poisoned by everything his river was made to carry. His function is the story's portrait of the environment as a patient: the film's central miracle is not magic but an extraction — a bicycle, a landfill, a century of dumping, hauled out of a god by a ten-year-old with a rope and a tub. The archive files him as the decade's most consequential river in film: a god, unburdened, by manual labor.

07 / WORLD & SETTING

World & Setting

The spirit town — the abandoned park at dusk

A fake heritage theme park — bubble-era Japan's plywood nostalgia for an Edo that never was — which fills at night with lanterns, restaurants and gods: the film's founding joke and its deepest sadness in one location, since the spirits' town is a ruin of the humans' imitation of their own past. The Town's function is the story's portrait of the counterfeit as a doorway: the fake park is false in daylight and true at dusk, which is more than can be said for the suburb the Oginos are driving to. The archive files this Town as the decade's most completely realized fictional town in film: a forgery that keeps the gods' hours.

The train line — the sea at high tide

A one-way railway across a flooded plain: stations named for swamps, passengers who are shadows, a conductor who takes tokens in silence — the film's great quiet machine, on which its heroine rides from labor to grace. The Line's function is the story's portrait of transit as contemplation: nothing happens on the train, which is the point — it is the one place in either world where Sen is neither working nor fleeing. The archive files this Line as the decade's most consequential fictional railway in film: a commute, through dusk, to a grandmother.

10 / THEMATIC ANALYSIS

Themes & Analysis

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

The name held — identity as memory under contract

The film's governing theme is Yubaba's confiscation system: she does not chain her workers; she renames them, and lets the forgetting accumulate until the exit is unimaginable. The theme is worked out in both directions (Haku, who forgot, serves; Sen, who remembers, leaves) until the audience holds the film's doctrine of the self: identity is not a possession but a practice — a name held in secret, repeated, defended daily against an economy that pays you to drop it. Its proposition reaches every employee in the audience whose workplace has issued them a shorter name — the role, the brand, the job title that eats the person: your Sen is the part of you the contract reissued. The reach is the story's question to every renamed worker: what is your Chihiro, where are you keeping it, and who — like Haku at the bridge — has been assigned to make sure you forget it?

Appetite's taxes — you are what you eat

The film's second theme is consumption and its species-level billing: the parents eat the gods' food and become pigs; No-Face eats the bathhouse's flattery and becomes a monster of wanting; the river god is found stuffed with a landfill. The theme is worked out as a complete moral physics (intake determines form) until the audience holds the film's doctrine of appetite: in a consumer world, the feast is never free and the bill is always your shape. Its proposition reaches every feaster in the audience at every unattended buffet of food, content or outrage: the stall has no staff because the payment is automated. The reach is the story's question to every consumer with a card in hand: what is this meal making of you — and who, exactly, told you it was a restaurant?

Work as the way through — labor's dignity

The film's third theme is its most quietly radical: the adventure is a job, and its heroine's growth is measured in competence — the tub scrubbed, the guest served, the medicine spent correctly. The theme is worked out against the genre's usual currencies (bloodlines, prophecies, weapons, of which Sen has none) until the audience holds the film's doctrine of labor: a ten-year-old with a mop and a memory is a complete hero, and the work is not the obstacle to the story; it is the story. Its proposition reaches every worker in the audience whose job is filed as a waiting room for their real life: Miyazaki's filing is that the bathhouse is the real life, and the way you mop is the way you are. The reach is the story's question to every Sen on a shift: what would change if you treated this contract as the adventure itself — its skills your arsenal, its Lins your fellowship, its exit earned rather than awaited?

The river paved — nature's amnesia and ours

The film's fourth theme is environmental, filed as memory: the Kohaku River is not dead but buried — drained, apartmented, its god employed and amnesiac in a bathhouse — and the stink spirit's body is a landfill with a face. The theme is worked out in the film's two restorations (a river god unburdened by hand; a river's name recovered by a child's fall) until the audience holds the film's doctrine of the environment: nature does not forget us; it is we who are the amnesiacs, and the gods of our childhood landscapes are still on the payroll somewhere, answering to shorter names. Its proposition reaches every citizen in the audience who grew up beside water that is now a parking structure: your river has a Haku. The reach is the story's question to every adult who once fell in: what was its name — and have you, like the bathhouse, been employing what you paved?

11 / FULL SPOILER CONCLUSION

Ending Explained

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL THE ENDING

Chihiro returns Zeniba's seal; Haku, healed by the river god's medicine, flies her back. At the gate, Yubaba's final test asks Chihiro to find her parents among a pen of pigs — she answers, correctly, that none of them are her parents, breaking the contract. Her name restored, she crosses the tunnel with her parents, who remember nothing. Haku's true name — Nigihayami Kohaku Nushi, the god of the Kohaku River — is restored to him by Chihiro's memory of falling into his river as a small child.

What the ending establishes

  • The gate test files perception as the graduate's credential: she passes not by finding her parents but by knowing, now, what a pig is and is not.
  • Haku's restored name files memory as the film's master key: the same act that frees the worker frees the god.
  • Yubaba's compliance files the contract as binding in both directions: the system's word is the exit its author must honor.
  • No-Face's apprenticeship files loneliness as curable by craft: the spirit who only consumed ends the film learning to make.
  • The parents' amnesia files growing up as a one-way gift: she returns their world to them, and keeps the knowledge herself.

What remains uncertain

Whether Haku escapes Yubaba's service with his name restored; whether Chihiro and Haku meet again, as he promises; and what the hair tie's protection will be needed for in the ordinary world — all stand open at the film's close, by its design: the story's last doctrine is Haku's "don't look back," an ending that refuses to let even the audience keep what it loves by staring. Within this film, the archive files the uncertainty as the story's own last word on its theme: she walks out of the tunnel with her name, her parents and her one glinting souvenir — everything else the spirit world keeps, as agreed.

12 / PURPOSES OF THE STORY

Moral of The Story

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Spirited Away uses a bathhouse, a contract and a stolen name to examine identity, appetite and work: a girl who keeps her name in secret while an economy pays her to forget it; parents whose feast is billed to their daughter; and a river god found stuffed with a landfill. Through the big tub, the quiet train and the gate test, the film argues that identity is a practice of remembering; that appetite always collects its payment in form; and that honest work, done competently and kindly, is a complete heroism.

Why tell this story?

Because Miyazaki, watching his friends' ten-year-old daughters, found a cinema full of heroines who won by destiny or spunk and none who won by the means actually available to a child: working, remembering, and being polite to terrifying people. He built the adventure out of a first job. The result is the film that re-founded what a children's hero could be, the archive files: a masterpiece whose dragon is freed by a memory and whose monster is cured by a walk.

1. Keep your name somewhere the contract can't reach

Yubaba's system is the film's first counsel, for every renamed worker — the hire issued a title, a brand, a handle, a shorter self: the confiscation is never announced as theft; it is announced as onboarding, and the forgetting is gradual, paid and comfortable. The counsel is a memory doctrine with Haku's whisper as its standard: hold your true name in one uncontracted place — a journal, a friendship, a practice no employer meters — and repeat it there on schedule. Haku's fate is the audit's other column: a god, employed, who cannot remember his river. Your name is not a vanity. It is the exit's address, and it is only kept by being used.

2. Ask what the feast is making of you

The stall's billing is the film's second counsel, for every consumer at an unattended buffet — of food, of feeds, of flattery, of outrage: the film's physics is that intake becomes form, and the absence of staff is not a convenience but the mechanism. The counsel is an appetite doctrine with the pig pen as its evidence: before the next portion, ask Sen's question in reverse — not "is this allowed?" but "what will I be, at dusk, if I eat the whole season of this?" Some feasts are meals. Some are contracts with the species clause in the fine print. The tell is whether anyone is willing to show you a menu.

3. Spend the medicine on the person bleeding now

The dango's address is the film's third counsel, for every holder of a scarce resource reserved for a worthier emergency — the savings, the favor, the cure hoarded for the parents while a Haku bleeds in the boiler room: Sen's greatness is not that she had the medicine but that she re-addressed it, mid-crisis, to the present-tense need, trusting the future to re-supply her. The counsel is a triage doctrine with the boiler room as its standard: a resource's value is realized only in its spending, and the worthiest use is rarely the one in the plan. Audit your dango annually: what are you saving, for whom, and who is bleeding in front of you this week, unlisted?

4. Treat the stink spirit as a river god until proven otherwise

The big tub's miracle is the film's fourth counsel, for every worker assigned the foul client, the hopeless case, the colleague everyone files as a stink spirit: Sen's method is not optimism but procedure — serve him properly, look for the thorn, pull. The counsel is a service doctrine with the bicycle as its evidence: what presents as a person's filth is very often a foreign body — a grief, a dumping, a history — lodged in them by other hands. Your job is rarely to fix the god. It is to find the handle of the thing stuck in him and organize the pulling. Bring rope. Bring help. Do not bring contempt; it has never extracted anything.

5. Give one lonely spirit a trade, not a feast

No-Face's cure is the film's fifth counsel, for every feeder of appetites — the manager buying loyalty with perks, the friend answering loneliness with consumption, the platform feeding a masked crowd: the bathhouse's gold made him a monster; Zeniba's spinning made him a neighbor. The counsel is a craft doctrine with the cottage as its standard: what the empty actually need is not more intake but a making — a skill, a use, a place at a table where they produce. When you find your No-Face (he is identifiable by the gold he offers for company), do not cater the rampage. Offer the walk out, the quiet house, and the loom.

6. Don't look back — carry it woven instead

Haku's instruction is the film's sixth counsel, its benediction, for every graduate of an intense season — the job, the city, the version of yourself that a place made necessary: the tunnel's law is that the way out is walked facing forward, and that what you are permitted to keep is not the place but the proof, worn small — a hair tie, a habit, a name pronounced correctly. The counsel is a departure doctrine with the glint as its evidence: looking back is an attempt to keep the bathhouse; the tie is keeping yourself. Leave your spirit towns properly: contract fulfilled, debts honored, one handmade thing in your hair. Then drive, and let your passengers file the new you under "the drive was long." You will know.

The moral of the story is…

Keep your name where the contract can't reach; ask what the feast is making of you; spend the medicine on the person bleeding now — and treat the stink spirit as a river god until proven otherwise.

Spirited Away teaches that identity is a practice of remembering, defended daily against every economy of forgetting; that appetite collects its payment in form; and that honest work, a good memory and one act of uncalculated pity are a complete equipment for a life.

The film's final question is therefore not whether you could find the bathhouse — the film files that under "wrong turns, taken by fathers." It is what your true name is and where you are keeping it; what your river was called before they paved it; and who is bleeding in your boiler room this week, holding your dango's true address.
