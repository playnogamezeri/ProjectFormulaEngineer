01 / FILM IDENTITY

Overview

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Golden Gate Girl （金門女； also Tears in San Francisco, Jinmen Nü) is a 1941 drama made in San Francisco by the Golden Gate Film Company, directed by Esther Eng — China's first woman film director — with veteran filmmaker Kwan Man-ching (Moon Kwan), who also wrote the script and plays the father; produced by Charlie Low; photographed by Joseph Sunn; 110 minutes, in Cantonese, with six songs; premiered in San Francisco the week of May 27, 1941, reviewed warmly by Variety, and not shown in Asia until 1946. The AFI Catalog files it as the first feature-length Chinese film made in San Francisco's Chinatown. Its story is the diaspora's own melodrama: the widower Jien-Sien Ho (Moon Kwan), a Chinatown shopkeeper known as the Gin Man, worries that his sixteen-year-old daughter Chain-ying (Tso Yee-man) spends too much time at the Chinese opera; taken to a performance, she falls for the visiting actor Wong Fay-tien (Wong Hok Sing), takes singing lessons, is reported by a friend for truancy, and defies her father in the film's most quoted line of doctrine — in America, fathers have no rights over their daughters. The father answers with the old world's instruments: he disowns her, and gets the theater to cancel Wong's contract, sending the actor back to China — though Chain-ying is already pregnant. Abandoned and penniless, she is sheltered by her father's cook, who attends the birth of her baby girl; she dies after the birth. The baby — Lulu — is played, uncredited, by Bruce Lee, then about three months old, dressed as a girl: the opera star Lee Hoi-chuen, a friend of Eng's, was touring America with the Cantonese Opera Company, and his infant son was the baby at hand. The dismissed cook and the shop salesman raise Lulu, opening a laundry to support her; grown (Tso Yee-man again, playing her own character's daughter), she inherits her mother's passion for the theater, and when her father Wong returns to America to perform in a national-defense play for China's war effort, daughter and father are reunited and the aging, remorseful grandfather is reconciled — the estranged family reassembled, in the end, by the theater that divided it and by the war that summoned it home. The film survives only in fragments, and lives in the culture at three addresses at once: as Esther Eng's pioneering work (the subject of S. Louisa Wei's 2014 documentary Golden Gate Girls), as the first Chinese feature of San Francisco's Chinatown, and as the first frame of the most famous filmography in Asian cinema — a baby girl in a laundry basket, played by a boy from the Year of the Dragon.

A pioneer, an exile company, a borrowed baby

The production is a wartime improvisation of geniuses. Esther Eng — San Francisco-born, Hong Kong-trained, the first Chinese woman to direct features — returned to her home city as war closed the Hong Kong industry, and made, with exile colleagues including Kwan Man-ching (a Griffith veteran of Broken Blossoms days) and cameraman Joseph Sunn, a Cantonese feature in Chinatown with local color as its documentary wealth: shops, laundries, the opera house, the working immigrant day. The credit itself carries a historiographic caution this file preserves: contemporary trade press (Variety) credits Eng as principal director; Kwan's memoirs later claimed the whole — the record's modern consensus restores Eng, and the AFI files her as director. And the baby: Lee Hoi-chuen, Eng's friend, was in America with the opera tour on which his son had been born in San Francisco the previous November; an infant was needed for certain scenes, and the tight-knit community supplied its own. The boy was dressed as the baby girl — a common practice of the era's casting, and the debut of the century's most masculine icon in a bonnet, uncredited, at three months.

03 / NARRATIVE SUMMARY

Story Synopsis

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL FULL STORY SUMMARY

In San Francisco's Chinatown, the widowed shopkeeper Jien-Sien Ho — the Gin Man — worries that his sixteen-year-old daughter Chain-ying spends too much time at the Chinese opera. His shop salesman Duck Sook takes her to see the visiting actor Wong Fay-tien perform; backstage, Wong invites her to the Golden Gate Music Club, and at her request gives her singing lessons. The meetings continue until Chain-ying's friend Sia-Lien tells the father of the excursions and the truancy. Confronted, Chain-ying declares that in America fathers cannot tell their teenage children what to do; she says she loves Wong. Jien-Sien disowns her and blames Duck Sook; Wong's offer of marriage, meant to soften the father, backfires — the old man will never forgive them. He uses his influence to have the theater cancel Wong's contract, and Wong returns to China alone, though his bride is pregnant.

Abandoned and without money, Chain-ying is aided by Mao Lee, her father's cook, who gives her money and attends the birth of her baby girl — and is fired for it, as Duck Sook is dismissed too. Chain-ying dies after the birth. The two dismissed employees take the baby in and open a laundry to raise her. The girl, Lulu, grows up — like her mother — with a passion for the theater. After a series of complications, tears and comic moments, she is reunited with her father when Wong visits the United States to perform in a national-defense play to aid China, then at war; and the family is finally reconciled with her aging, remorseful maternal grandfather — the theater that divided them, and the war that summoned them, closing the circle the father's contract had broken.

The full narrative summary is an editorial paraphrase of the documented accounts (Wikipedia, the AFI Catalog, the Dennis Schwartz review), not dialogue or a scene-by-scene transcript.

Two countries in one shop — the diaspora's generation gap

The reach is the story's question to every father and mother raising children under a new flag: which of your instruments were issued by the old country — and have you renegotiated them, at the kitchen table, before the theater does it for you?

12 / PURPOSES OF THE STORY

Moral of The Story

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Golden Gate Girl uses two generations of one Chinatown family to examine the diaspora's generation gap, kinship by conduct, and the theater as a community's fate: a daughter who defies her father with America's doctrine and dies of his answer; two dismissed servants who raise her baby on laundry money; an actor deported by contract and recalled by a war's benefit night; and a grandfather educated by remorse into reconciliation. Through the shop, the stage and the laundry, the film argues that a patriarch's instruments expire at the border unless renegotiated; that parenthood is a verb audited by conduct; and that a community's central institution will finish every story its members try to cancel.

Why tell this story?

Because San Francisco's Chinatown, in 1941, needed to see itself — its shops, its laundries, its opera, its daughters' new grammar — on its own screen, in its own language, while its homeland burned; and Esther Eng, the pioneer's pioneer, was the director the moment had prepared. The archive files the result as the Galaxy's foundation stone: Chinatown's first feature, a woman director's exile masterpiece, and the first frame of Bruce Lee.

1. Renegotiate the instruments at the border

Jien-Sien Ho is the film's first counsel, for every emigrant parent — and every parent of a generation raised under new rules: his instruments (disowning, influence, the cancelled contract) were issued by the old jurisdiction and executed, catastrophically, in the new one. The counsel is a renegotiation doctrine with the shop as its standard: when the country changes — or the era does — sit down and re-issue your authority in the new grammar, at the kitchen table, while the renegotiation is still a conversation, because the alternative is the film's: the instruments used once, at full power, and the tuition collected in the standard currency. Chain-ying told him the new constitution in one sentence. Hear yours the first time.

2. Audit parenthood by conduct

The laundry is the film's second counsel, for every holder of a family title: the cook and the salesman, fired for mercy, become the child's family in every sense the audit recognizes. The counsel is a conduct doctrine with the tub as its standard: in any family dispute over who has "been there" for a child, keep the laundry's ledger — who fed, who attended the birth, who paid, who stayed — and let the titles argue with the ledger, not the reverse, because children, like archives, are kept by whoever does the keeping. The patriarch owned the shop. The servants owned the granddaughter's childhood. Both facts are in the film's ledger. Keep yours as honestly.

3. Do not cancel the artist

The cancelled contract is the film's third counsel, for every elder, employer and authority tempted to end a romance or a career by paperwork: Jien-Sien's instrument works perfectly — the actor is gone within the reel — and the perfection is the catastrophe. The counsel is a restraint doctrine with the deportation as its standard: before you use institutional power against a young person's attachment or vocation, price the film's arithmetic — the contract is one signature; the consequences are two generations — and remember that the institution you weaponize (the theater, the school, the platform) will outlast your grievance and schedule the reunion without you, in its own time, at a benefit you will attend in remorse. Sign slower.

4. Protect the pioneer's credit in the contemporary record

Esther Eng is the film's fourth counsel, for every collaborator, employer and historian standing near a first: her direction was reassigned in a colleague's memoir and restored by the trade press of 1941. The counsel is a credit doctrine with Variety as its standard: when you work with the era's outsiders — the first woman, the minority studio, the foreign-language production — get the credits right in the contemporary record, in print, at the time, because the memoir wars are won by whoever's version was filed first with a date on it. The pioneer's file is the community's deed to its own history. File it like one.

5. Raise the child in the community's keeping

Lulu's laundry childhood is the film's fifth counsel, for every community with an orphan, a foster child or a dismissed servant's baby in its care: the film's welfare state is two employees and a business, and it suffices — because it is staffed by the merciful. The counsel is a keeping doctrine with the laundry as its standard: a community's true safety net is not its patriarchs' estates but its cooks' and salesmen's willingness to convert a dismissal into a parenthood — honor that willingness publicly, fund it when you can, and never fire a man for attending a birth. The child in the basket becomes the family's reconciliation. Baskets are infrastructure. Treat the carriers accordingly.

6. Let the second generation re-run the story

The doubling is the film's sixth counsel, its benediction, for every family with an unfinished first act: Tso Yee-man plays the mother and then the daughter, and the daughter's run at the same passion ends in reunion instead of death. The counsel is a reprise doctrine with the casting as its standard: give the next generation the family's forbidden thing — the theater, the trade, the emigration, the love — under new management (theirs), because a story re-run by its heirs is the only known mechanism by which a family's verdicts get appealed. Chain-ying's defiance, in Lulu, becomes the family's healing. Schedule the reprise. Attend it in remorse if you must, but attend.

The moral of the story is…

Renegotiate the instruments at the border; audit parenthood by conduct; do not cancel the artist — and let the second generation re-run the story.

Golden Gate Girl teaches that a patriarch's power expires at the border unless it is renegotiated; that family is a verb audited in feedings and attendances; and that a community's stage will finish every story its members try to cancel.

The film's final question is therefore not whether the family reconciles — the film files that under "ask the benefit night." It is which of your instruments were issued by a country you no longer live in; whose laundry is currently raising your family's future; and which pioneer's credit, in your own production, the memoirs are already revising.
