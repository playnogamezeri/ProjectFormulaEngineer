01 / FILM IDENTITY

Overview

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

The Kid （細路祥； also Kid Cheung and My Son, Ah Chung) is a 1950 Hong Kong drama directed by Fung Fung, written by Cho Kei (Tso Kea), adapted from Yuen Po-wan's popular comic strip Kid Cheung, produced by Leung Biu for the Datong Film Company, and released May 30, 1950. It is Bruce Lee's first leading role — the nine-year-old (ten by some reckonings) in the title part — and the film that named him: credited as Li Long (Lee Lung), he took from the role his enduring screen name, Li Xiao-long, "Little Dragon Lee." Kid Cheung (Ho Cheung) is an orphan of the slums, running a little stall with his younger cousins Ngau and Chu under the care of their Uncle Ho (Yee Chau-sui), a former teacher who rents and sells comic books — the Kid's own medium, supporting the Kid's own household. The plot is the era's class fable built as a boy's adventure: the tycoon Hung Pak-ho (Lee Hoi-chuen — Lee's real father, playing the film's villain of wealth), a medicine-factory owner and chairman of the Children's Health and Education Association, pledges a school for poor children purely for reputation, expecting the promise to be forgotten. Robbed in the slums by Flash Knife Lee (Fung Fung, the director doubling as the honorable robber chief) — his wallet, his watch, and his daughter So Mui's necklace taken — Hung sets the story in motion: the fleeing Lee hides in Kid Cheung's house, sheltered by the boy's instinctive loyalty, and leaves the necklace behind. Uncle Ho returns it, hoping for a reward, and the tycoon's daughter's pleading wins the household its rise: Ho becomes Hung's secretary and the girl's tutor, and Cheung is enrolled in a good school. There the slum boy is teased, fights, and is expelled — the film's thesis in one arc: the charity that admits him cannot keep him — and he falls in with Flash Knife Lee's gang as a thief, to his uncle's grief. Around the boy's fall the adults' plot completes the fable: Hung's spoiled son Boaster Chiu (played by Yuen Po-wan, the comic's own creator) and the clerk Four-Eyed Tsui scheme to rob the factory, frame its women workers — who are striking for better conditions — and betray the gang to the police in the same motion; and the Kid, per the documented accounts' agreed shape, helps save the day, leaves the life of crime, and returns to honest work and usefulness — the morality tale's required homecoming, earned through the gang chief's own code of honor. A critical and commercial success, the film stands in the culture as Hong Kong's counterpart to Shanghai's Three Hairs — the opening credits run Yuen's five hand-drawn caricatures of the three-bunched-hair Kid across the screen before Lee himself appears — and as the foundation document of a career: everything in the Galaxy's Lee shelf, from the tenement's Wah to the bully of 1953, is a variation on this boy.

Place in the culture

The film lives in the culture as the Lee filmography's foundation stone and the Cantonese cinema's beloved street-child classic — Hong Kong's Three Hairs, drawn by Yuen Po-wan and embodied, at nine, with the naturalness every later account remarks. Its afterlife is double: a social comedy of class conflict that still plays, and the answer to the trivia of a century — where the name Bruce Lee's Chinese self came from. It came from here: a comic strip, a stall, and a boy who sold the comics he starred in.

A family firm, a comic strip, a name

The production is a palimpsest of the era's intimacies. The director plays the robber chief; the comic's creator plays the tycoon's villainous son; the tycoon is the star's real father — the second and last film in which Lee Hoi-chuen and his son worked together, after The Birth of Mankind (1946), and this time as antagonists across the class line: the father as hoarding wealth, the son as the slum's conscience. And the opening credits perform the adaptation's thesis: Yuen's five hand-drawn caricatures of Kid Cheung — three bunches of hair, Hong Kong's answer to Three Hairs — evolve across the sequence from cute naughty kid to little gangster with a knife, the whole film summarized in drawings before the boy himself walks on. Lee is credited as Li Long; the production's records and the biographies agree that the role fixed his name thereafter — Li Xiao-long, Little Dragon — first as a nickname from the set and the strip, then as his private and screen identity, over his birth name Li Jun-fan.

03 / NARRATIVE SUMMARY

Story Synopsis

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL FULL STORY SUMMARY

Kid Cheung — Ho Cheung — is an orphan of the slums who runs a small stall with his younger cousins Ngau and Chu, in the household of their Uncle Ho, a former teacher reduced to renting and selling comic books. He is not interested in education and often gets into fights, but his code is sound: when the robber Flash Knife Lee, fleeing the police after robbing the tycoon Hung Pak-ho in the slums — wallet, watch and the necklace of Hung's daughter So Mui — ducks into the Kid's house, the boy shelters him rather than betray him for a reward, and earns the gang chief's respect and friendship. Lee leaves the stolen necklace behind in the house; Uncle Ho finds it and brings Cheung to Hung's mansion to return it, hoping for a reward. So Mui's earnest pleading converts the return into the household's fortune: Ho is hired as Hung's private secretary and the daughter's tutor, and Cheung is enrolled in a good school.

The rise fails at its center. At the good school the slum boy is teased by the other students and expelled for fighting — the charity's door opens and cannot hold him — and he drifts to the patron who never mocked him: Flash Knife Lee, who takes him as a disciple and a thief. Uncle Ho is disheartened at the descent, and the two fall out terribly. Meanwhile the factory plot matures: Hung's women workers strike for better conditions; Hung's spoiled elder son Boaster Chiu, with Four-Eyed Tsui, schemes to steal from the factory stores for the gang to smuggle, then to have the gang rob the factory, frame the women workers for it, and betray the gang to the police in the same stroke — a triple-cross worthy of the tycoon's own household. The documented accounts agree on the resolution's shape, which the film's title guarantees: the Kid helps save the day — the schemes exposed, the honest chief's code vindicated against the respectable villains' — and Cheung leaves the life of crime behind, returning to honest work and usefulness: the morality tale's homecoming, in which the slum's boy proves the conscience of every class that failed him.

The full narrative summary is an editorial paraphrase of the documented accounts (Wikipedia's plot, the LoveHKFilm review), not dialogue or a scene-by-scene transcript.

10 / THEMATIC ANALYSIS

Themes & Analysis

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Access without belonging — the opened door that cannot hold

The film's governing theme is the good school's arc: the Kid is admitted by charity and expelled by the corridor. The theme is worked out in the rise and fall until the audience holds its doctrine of the two doors: every institution has a door of admission and a corridor of belonging, and philanthropy that funds the first while ignoring the second manufactures exactly the fall it deplores — the gang, which offers belonging first, collects the expelled. Its proposition reaches every funder of access in the audience. The reach is the story's question to every scholarship board, admissions office and diversity programme: which of your corridors is currently expelling, by mockery, the people your door so proudly admitted — and is the gang across the street keeping better faith with them than you are?

Philanthropy as public relations — the promised school

The film's second theme is Hung's pledge: a school for poor children, funded for reputation and meant to be forgotten. The theme is worked out in the tycoon's household until the audience holds its doctrine of the press-release gift: charity performed for the giver's name is a loan the poor are expected to repay in gratitude, and its true accounts are kept in the giver's own home — where the heir is a thief and the philanthropy's chairman cannot run his own nursery. Its proposition reaches every public giver in the audience. The reach is the story's question to every named donor and ribbon-cut chairman: if your household's private ledger were published beside the plaque, would the gift still read as generosity — and is the school you promised last year a building, or a paragraph?

Loyalty before morality — the sheltered robber

The film's third theme is the Kid's first instinct: hiding the fleeing chief without bargaining. The theme is worked out in the apprenticeship until the audience holds its doctrine of the prior virtue: a child's loyalty precedes his morality — he will keep faith before he can judge causes — and every institution competing for him is really bidding for the loyalty, with the morality to be installed afterward by whoever wins. Its proposition reaches every recruiter of the young in the audience. The reach is the story's question to every school, gang, team and movement enrolling children: are you bidding for their loyalty with belonging, as the gang does — or with press releases, as the mansion does — and do you understand that the child will keep faith with the winner long after he learns to judge the cause?

The true delinquent — the mansion's heir

The film's fourth theme is its inversion: the criminal juvenile is Boaster Chiu, not Kid Cheung. The theme is worked out in the factory plot until the audience holds its doctrine of the misfiled delinquent: a society files its youth crime under the slum's address because the slum's boy is visible, while the heir's thefts are administered from an office and discovered, if at all, as bookkeeping. Its proposition reaches every auditor of youth and crime in the audience. The reach is the story's question to every magistrate, headmaster and headline writer: in your current caseload, which delinquent is filed under his address — and which is filed, like Chiu, under his father's respectability, where the factory's own stores are quietly walking out?

11 / FULL SPOILER CONCLUSION

Ending Explained

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL THE ENDING

Per the documented accounts' agreed shape: the factory schemes — the thefts, the planned robbery and the frame-up of the women workers and the gang — are exposed; Kid Cheung helps save the day; and the Kid leaves the life of crime behind, returning to honest work and usefulness, reconciled with the household of the stall.

What the ending establishes

  • The exposure files the respectable conspiracy as the plot's true crime: the heir and the clerk, not the gang, are its criminals of design.
  • The Kid's homecoming files the morality tale's requirement as earned: the boy who sheltered a robber ends by out-honoring every class that failed him.
  • The gang chief's code files honor's address as the fable's embarrassment: the underworld kept the faith the mansion advertised.
  • The school's absence from the resolution files the film's reserved judgment: the Kid is saved by the stall's household, not by the charity that expelled him.
  • The name files the afterlife: the boy of the resolution is Li Xiao-long from this film forward — the homecoming is also a christening.

What remains uncertain

The precise staging of the factory climax and the terms of each schemer's punishment vary in emphasis across the documented accounts; the broad resolution above follows Wikipedia's plot and the LoveHKFilm review. Within this film, the archive files the uncertainty as the story's own last word on its theme: a fable's ending is a shape, and the shape here is a boy coming home to a bookstall.

12 / PURPOSES OF THE STORY

Moral of The Story

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

The Kid uses a comic-strip orphan to examine access without belonging, philanthropy as public relations, and loyalty as the first virtue a child trades in: a slum boy who shelters a robber and returns a necklace; a good school that admits him and expels him by corridor; a gang that offers him belonging and a trade; and a tycoon's heir who commits the picture's real crimes from an office. Through the stall, the necklace and the knife, the film argues that institutions keep the young by belonging, not by admission; that charity performed for a name is a loan disguised as a gift; and that a child's loyalty, once won by whoever offers him a place, will be kept long after he learns to judge the cause.

Why tell this story?

Because Yuen Po-wan's strip had already made the Kid the slum's ambassador to the city's breakfast tables, and Fung Fung understood that embodying him — at nine, by the opera star's son, opposite the opera star as the villain — would turn a beloved comic into the era's class fable in its most durable form. The archive files the result as the Galaxy's foundation stone: the boy every later Lee role in this shelf is a variation on, and the film that named the century's most famous martial artist.

1. Fund the corridor, not only the door

The good school is the film's first counsel, for every funder of access — the scholarship, the admissions office, the outreach programme: Hung's charity admits the Kid and the corridor expels him, and the gang collects the difference. The counsel is a belonging doctrine with the expulsion as its standard: budget the second door — the mentors, the anti-mockery discipline, the cohort, the first term's close company — at parity with the first, because an access programme without a belonging budget is a recruitment pipeline for whoever across the street offers the belonging instead. The Kid's fists were a corridor's report card. Read yours before the expulsion letters go out.

2. Audit the philanthropy at home

Hung Pak-ho is the film's second counsel, for every public giver: his promised school is a paragraph, his heir is a thief, and the film files the two facts in one ledger. The counsel is a household doctrine with the plaque as its standard: before your name goes on a children's institution, run the audit the film runs — the state of your own nursery, your own succession, your own factory's stores — because a gift that advertises what the giver's house contradicts will be read, by the Kidders of the world, as the loan it is. The Kid returned the necklace. He did not return the verdict.

3. Bid for loyalty with belonging

Flash Knife Lee is the film's third counsel, for every institution enrolling the young — the school, the club, the team, the faith: the gang wins the expelled boy because it offers him, instantly, a place, a master and a debt of honor. The counsel is a recruitment doctrine with the apprenticeship as its standard: a child's loyalty is contracted by whoever first makes him necessary — so make him necessary early, by name, with a role and a debt of gratitude flowing the right way — because the competition for the corridor's casualties is always open, and it does not advertise in press releases. The chief knocked once, from inside the boy's own house. Be the house.

4. Look for the delinquent in the office

Boaster Chiu is the film's fourth counsel, for every auditor of youth, crime and loss: the picture's real juvenile crimes are administered by the heir and the clerk, against the workers and the gang alike. The counsel is a filing doctrine with the factory stores as its standard: when losses mount, audit the respectable addresses with the same presumption you audit the slum's — the heir's access, the clerk's keys, the office's overtime — because delinquency filed by address is a subsidy to the office, paid by the stall. The Kid was expelled for fighting. Chiu was never expelled for anything. That is the film's whole police report.

5. Keep a stall — an honest small trade

Uncle Ho's bookstall is the film's fifth counsel, for every household raising children at the economy's edge: the stall feeds the orphans, stocks the neighborhood's reading, and anchors the boy's homecoming. The counsel is a livelihood doctrine with the stall as its standard: a small honest trade, held steadily in the children's sight, is itself an education — the Kid's code was learned across that counter, from an ex-teacher selling stories — and its dignity out-teaches any school that admits a child to mockery. When the mansion and the gang have both finished with your child, the stall is what he comes home to. Keep one, and keep it honest.

6. Let the boy keep his name

Li Xiao-long is the film's sixth counsel, its benediction, for every adult in charge of a child's identity — the parent, the teacher, the first employer: the set's nickname for the nine-year-old, drawn from a comic strip's diminutive, became the name he chose over his birth name for life. The counsel is a naming doctrine with the credit as its standard: the names the young earn in their first real work — the nickname from the crew, the byname from the street — are often the truest instruments they will ever be handed; ratify them, use them, let the child sign with them, because a person who has chosen his own name has begun the authorship of himself. The century's most famous martial artist was christened by a children's picture. Attend to the christenings in your own house.

The moral of the story is…

Fund the corridor, not only the door; audit the philanthropy at home; bid for loyalty with belonging — and look for the delinquent in the office.

The Kid teaches that institutions keep the young by belonging rather than admission; that charity performed for a name is a loan disguised as a gift; and that a child's loyalty, won early, outlasts his judgment of the cause.

The film's final question is therefore not whether the Kid was saved — the film files that under "ask the stall." It is which of your admitted children your corridor is currently expelling; what your own heir is carrying out of the factory; and who, this term, is bidding for your expelled with a place, a master and a knife.
