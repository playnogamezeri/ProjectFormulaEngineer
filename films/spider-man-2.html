01 / FILM IDENTITY

Overview

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Spider-Man 2 is a 2004 American superhero film directed by Sam Raimi, written by Alvin Sargent from a story by Alfred Gough, Miles Millar and Michael Chabon. Released June 30, 2004, it grossed $795.9 million worldwide on a $200 million budget — the third-highest-grossing film of 2004 — and won the Academy Award for Best Visual Effects. It is widely regarded as one of the finest superhero films ever made. Two years after the first film, Peter Parker (Tobey Maguire) is failing at everything except the secret: fired from his pizza job for lateness, failing his physics course, broke, about to lose his apartment, watching Mary Jane Watson (Kirsten Dunst) succeed on Broadway and accept a proposal from astronaut John Jameson — while his best friend Harry Osborn (James Franco), now running Oscorp, funds the fusion research of Dr. Otto Octavius (Alfred Molina) and nurses a vendetta against Spider-Man, whom he holds responsible for his father's death, unaware that the vendetta's target sleeps on his own couch. Peter's body files its own grievance: his webbing fails mid-swing, his strength flickers — a psychosomatic strike by a gift its owner no longer wants — and after a doctor's banal diagnosis ("a hero in all of us" is the film's refrain, distributed to its most ordinary mouths) and a dream visitation from Uncle Ben, Peter resigns: the suit goes into a dumpster, in the film's most famous single image, a panel-for-panel homage to the comics' "Spider-Man No More." The vacancy is filled by the film's other life: Octavius, a kindly, happily married scientist whose fusion demonstration — conducted with four AI-driven mechanical arms grafted to his spine — collapses, kills his wife Rosalie (Donna Murphy), fuses the arms to his body and destroys the inhibitor chip that kept the arms' intelligence subordinate; the arms rebuild his experiment by criminal means (a bank vault robbery interrupted by Peter and Aunt May, in the film's thesis scene of the old lady's umbrella and the hero's choice) and drive him toward a larger reactor that will drown half the city in its own ambition. Peter's return is engineered by the film's women: Aunt May (Rosemary Harris), robbed of her home and nearly of her faith, delivers the film's thesis as a moving-day monologue — there is a hero in all of us, who keeps us honest, gives us strength, and makes sacrifices — while withholding, with an actress's perfect timing, whether she knows the secret she is plainly circling; and MJ, kidnapped as the experiment's bait, becomes the occasion of the film's defining set piece: the elevated train, its controls smashed, Spider-Man unmasked by the effort of stopping it, and a car full of New Yorkers who carry his unconscious body over their heads, look at his young face — "he's just a kid" — return his mask, and promise, to a man, that they will not tell. Unmasked a second time — to Octavius, in the reactor's chamber — Peter talks the scientist back into himself ("sometimes, to do what's right, we have to be steady and give up the thing we want the most — even our dreams"), and Octavius drowns his machine and himself in the river, dying, in his own verdict, "not a monster." The coda redistributes the secrets: Harry, discovering his father's Goblin arsenal behind a mirror, inherits the vendetta's machinery; and MJ, having learned the truth on the train's evidence and at the reactor, leaves her wedding at the altar and comes to Peter's door with the film's settlement — she knows, she chooses, she accepts the risk as hers to take: "Go get 'em, tiger."

Place in the culture

The film is the standard against which superhero sequels are measured — routinely placed at or near the top of all-time rankings of the genre — for proving that a comic-book film could carry a full character drama (a hero's resignation, a villain's marriage and grief) inside its spectacle. The train sequence remains the genre's most beloved set piece, Molina's Octavius its most beloved tragic villain of the era, and the film's thesis — heroism as a daily, costly, chosen practice rather than a power — became the template the genre's best entries still follow. Its "Spider-Man No More" dumpster shot is among the most reproduced images in comics adaptation.

The sequel as a character film

Raimi and producer Laura Ziskin built the sequel around the comics' most famous Peter Parker storyline — the hero's resignation — hiring Chabon (a Pulitzer-winning novelist and lifelong comics reader) for the story and Sargent, a two-time Oscar winner then in his seventies, to write a screenplay whose spine is a young man's burnout rather than a villain's scheme. Molina was cast for warmth as much as menace (Raimi wanted the audience to grieve Octavius before fearing him), and his arms were realized as a hybrid of puppeteered rigs and CGI, giving the performance a physical sparring partner. The train sequence was shot on a real Chicago elevated line (standing in for New York) with Maguire performing much of the unmasking aftermath practically. Maguire's back problems nearly cost him the role in pre-production (Jake Gyllenhaal was briefly in negotiation) before a recovery program returned him. Released June 30, 2004, the film was a critical summit for the genre — Roger Ebert called it the best superhero movie since the modern genre began — finished third for the year worldwide, and won the Academy Award for Best Visual Effects.

03 / NARRATIVE SUMMARY

Story Synopsis

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL FULL STORY SUMMARY

The film opens with its hero's accounts in arrears: pizzas delivered by web-swing and still late, a landlord (Elya Baskin's Mr. Ditkovich) collecting rent in humiliations, a professor (Dylan Baker's Dr. Connors) failing the brightest student he has, and a birthday party at Aunt May's where every thread of Peter's life is present and every one is fraying — Harry's Oscorp ascension and vendetta, MJ's engagement announcement days away, May's foreclosure letter hidden in a drawer. The Octavius demonstration is staged as the film's idyll before the fall: a scientist who quotes poetry to his wife, who tells Peter that intelligence is a gift to be used for the good of mankind, and whose fusion sun rises beautifully for ninety seconds before it eats the room — Rosalie killed by the glass, the arms fused, the inhibitor gone, and the hospital sequence that follows (Raimi's horror-film grammar, chainsaws and dragged nurses, played without a note of camp) delivering the arms' first independent decisions while their owner lies unconscious. Peter's parallel collapse is quieter and funnier and sadder: the powers' flicker, the rooftop confession to a hallucinated Ben ("I am Spider-Man, no more"), the dumpster, and then the film's most daring passage — the montage of the ordinary life, scored to "Raindrops Keep Fallin' on My Head," in which Peter eats hot dogs, ignores a mugging with a pang he talks himself out of, and discovers that resignation is a job too, with its own overtime of guilt. The crime wave the montage papers over is the film's bookkeeping: the city without him is measurably worse, and his one intervention — running into a burning building as a civilian, and being told by the firemen that someone else did not make it out — is the resignation's audit, delivered by strangers.

The return is assembled from the film's two great speeches and one great silence. May's moving-day monologue gives the thesis its domestic form — the hero as the person who keeps us honest and nobler, "and finally allows us to die with dignity" — and her parting gift, a held look and a change of subject, is the film's most debated silence: she knows, or she has decided to act as if not knowing is her gift to him. The train gives the thesis its public form: Spider-Man spent to unconsciousness across the locomotive's nose, the passengers' hands under his body like a saint's procession, the mask returned with a vow — the city's side of the covenant, kept. And the reactor gives it its final form: the unmasking as an argument, Peter showing Octavius the face of the student he lectured on gifts, and the scientist — addressed, for the first time since the accident, as a man rather than a machine's chauffeur — reasserting ownership of his own arms in the film's most quietly heroic line of dialogue: "I will not die a monster." The settlements cascade: Harry's mirror, shattered, reveals the Goblin's arsenal and the vendetta's inheritance, in a single held shot of a son finding his father's second face; and MJ, at her own wedding, performs the film's last and largest act of agency — leaving the astronaut at the altar, running through the city in her wedding dress, and arriving at Peter's door to claim the life she has chosen with its risks itemized and accepted. The final shot is the saga's most honest: her face at the window as he swings away to sirens — joy and apprehension in the same expression, held, as the credits take the film.

The full narrative summary is an editorial paraphrase, not dialogue or a scene-by-scene transcript.

04 / STORY STRUCTURE

Story & Chronology

The film unfolds across Peter Parker's second year as Spider-Man. The sequence below follows the film's narrative order.

Act I — The failing gift

A hero broke, fired and losing his powers — and a kind scientist's sun, rising beautifully, for ninety seconds.

Act II — No more

A suit in a dumpster, a city measurably worse — and an aunt's monologue, a burning building, and a debt of guilt coming due.

A car full of New Yorkers keeps a secret, a scientist is talked back into himself — and a bride leaves a wedding, running.

THE HERO

Peter Parker / Spider-Man

Tobey Maguire. A gifted, broke, exhausted young man — whose double life has consumed the single one: the film's subject is not his heroism but its invoice, itemized in fired jobs, failed courses, a foreclosure and a wedding invitation to someone else's marriage. Maguire plays him with a hangdog, Chaplinesque pathos that makes the resignation legible as self-preservation rather than cowardice, and Peter's function is the story's portrait of the gift as a job with no wage: his powers' failure is his body's union action, and his return is not a recovery of strength but a renegotiation of terms. The archive files him as the decade's definitive screen superhero: a hero whose greatest enemy, for two acts, is his own schedule.

THE SCIENTIST

Dr. Otto Octavius / Doctor Octopus

Alfred Molina. A brilliant, warm, happily married physicist — whose life's work kills his wife, grafts itself to his spine, and spends the film ventriloquizing his grief as ambition: the arms' whispers are his own unprocessed loss, outsourced to machinery. Molina plays him with a genial, bearlike warmth that survives, horribly, into the villainy — the menace is the kindness, continued by other means — and Octavius's function is the story's portrait of the villain as a widower: his fusion sun is a monument he is building over a grave he has not visited. The archive files him as the decade's finest screen supervillain: a monster, self-diagnosed, in the last ninety seconds, correctly.

THE ACTRESS

Mary Jane Watson

Kirsten Dunst. A Broadway actress engaged to an astronaut — whose patience with Peter's absences has expired into a decision the film treats with unusual respect: she is not waiting to be rescued from her engagement; she is auditing a man who will not tell her the truth, and her final choice is made with the truth in hand, as an adult's acceptance of itemized risk. Dunst plays her with a wounded directness that keeps the love story from sentimentality, and MJ's function is the story's portrait of the beloved as the covenant's other signatory: the saga's secrets are kept from her for her protection, and the film's verdict is that the protection was the injury. The archive files her as the decade's most complete screen superhero's partner: a bride who left the altar under her own power, running.

THE HEIR

Harry Osborn

James Franco. Oscorp's young chief and Peter's best friend — whose vendetta against Spider-Man is a grief with a budget: he funds Octavius to get at the hero, drinks through his father's shadow, and ends the film inheriting the arsenal behind the mirror, the vendetta's machinery made literal. Franco plays him with a golden-boy charm curdling into obsession, and Harry's function is the story's portrait of the inheritance as the sequel's true engine: Norman is dead, but the estate — money, grievance, glider — is in probate, and the film files the mirror scene as the will being read. The archive files him as the decade's most consequential screen best friend in a superhero film: an heir, reading his father's second face, in a mirror.

THE AUNT

May Parker

Rosemary Harris. Peter's widowed aunt — facing foreclosure with a dignity that refuses every offer of help, surviving a bank robbery and a rooftop's height in the same week, and delivering, over packed boxes, the film's thesis as a moving-day monologue about the hero in all of us. Harris plays her with a steel-threaded warmth that makes her the film's moral center of gravity, and May's function is the story's portrait of the elder as the covenant's keeper: she has lost the most to the secret and services it most gracefully — whether she knows is the film's kindest ambiguity, and her not-asking is its own form of protection, returned. The archive files her as the decade's finest screen aunt: a widow whose monologue re-enlists a superhero.

THE EDITOR

J. Jonah Jameson

J. K. Simmons. The Daily Bugle's publisher — whose vendetta against Spider-Man is a circulation strategy conducted at auction volume, and whose two honest moments (his son's engagement; his confession, at the altar's wreckage, that the hero he smeared was perhaps the real thing) are the film's most efficient character work. Simmons plays him at a machine-gun tempo that steals every scene he files, and Jameson's function is the story's portrait of the press as the hero's shadow economy: he manufactures the public's Spider-Man while the city's passengers manufacture the real one, and the film files the contrast as its media criticism. The archive files him as the decade's definitive screen editor: a publisher whose finest headline is the one he never prints — the secret, kept by a train.

THE WIFE

Rosalie Octavius

Donna Murphy. Otto's wife and collaborator — the marriage the film offers as its image of the balanced life (science and poetry, work and devotion, in one laboratory and one kitchen), whose death in the demonstration is the accident's true payload: everything Octavius does afterward is grief with a reactor attached. Murphy plays her in a handful of scenes with such warmth that her absence governs the film, and Rosalie's function is the story's portrait of the beloved as the villain's deleted thesis: the arms can rebuild the experiment; they cannot rebuild the audience it was for. The archive files her as the decade's most consequential minor wife in a superhero film: a marriage, cited, in every scene of its widower's crimes.

THE ASTRONAUT

John Jameson

Daniel Gillies. Jonah's son — a national hero of the legitimate kind (the moon, a ticker-tape resume) and MJ's fiancé, whose function is to be the life Peter is losing to, at specification: handsome, decorated, solvent, present. Gillies plays him with an uncomplicated decency that keeps the triangle honest, and John's function is the story's portrait of the rival as a control group: he is what a hero looks like with the secret removed and the salary added, and MJ's leaving him is therefore a verdict about information, not quality. The archive files him as the decade's most instructive screen fiancé: an astronaut, left at the altar, by the truth.

THE FATHER

Norman Osborn

Willem Dafoe. The first film's villain, appearing in Harry's mirror as a hallucinated demand — "Avenge me" — the dead father's voice ventriloquized by the son's grief. Dafoe's single scene is the vendetta's transmission made visible, and Norman's function is the story's portrait of the inheritance as a haunting with paperwork: the arsenal is real, the voice is Harry's, and the film files the distinction as the tragedy's mechanism. The archive files him as the decade's most efficient screen ghost in a superhero film: a father, survived, by his own arsenal.

THE UNCLE

Ben Parker

Cliff Robertson. Peter's murdered uncle, appearing in a dream at the resignation's hinge — not to command but to remind: the gift and the curse are the same fact, and the dream's car ride is the film's most direct restatement of its founding text. Robertson plays him with the same plain warmth as the first film, and Ben's function is the story's portrait of the dead as the conscience's casting department: Peter's guilt and his vocation share a voice, and the film files the dream as the one meeting his schedule cannot make him late for. The archive files him as the decade's most consequential screen uncle: a passenger, in a dream, restating the terms.

THE PROFESSOR

Dr. Curt Connors

Dylan Baker. Peter's one-armed physics professor — a kind man failing his best student with genuine regret, and Octavius's colleague, whose few scenes carry the film's academic world's whole weather. Baker plays him with a tired decency, and Connors's function is the story's portrait of the institution as the gift's other auditor: the university, like the city, keeps grading the hero on the life he is not living. The archive files him as the decade's most consequential minor professor in a superhero film: a one-armed physicist, in a film about arms, grading on attendance.

THE LANDLORD

Mr. Ditkovich

Elya Baskin. Peter's landlord — a rent-collecting chorus of one, whose doorway appearances ("Rent!") are the film's most reliable metronome of the ordinary life's arrears, and whose daughter Ursula's small kindnesses (cake, milk, a crush) are the building's counter-theme. Baskin plays him with a gravelly, vaudevillian persistence, and Ditkovich's function is the story's portrait of the mundane as the hero's true recurring villain: no master plan, no arms — just the rent, monthly, in a world that does not know it is being saved. The archive files him as the decade's most essential screen landlord: a creditor, unbothered, by the apocalypse's schedule.

08 / THE GIFT, THE FUSION & THE TRAIN

The Gift, the Fusion & the Train

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

The gift — powers on a conscience's terms

Peter's abilities, in this film, are psychosomatically conditional: they fail as his will fails and return as it returns — the genre's most direct statement that the power is a practice, not a possession. The gift's function is the story's portrait of vocation as a renewable contract: it must be re-signed daily, at the price of the signer's whole schedule. The archive files the gift as the decade's most consequential fictional power set: a strength that files grievances.

The fusion reactor — ambition with a gravity well

Octavius's life's work: a self-sustaining fusion reaction — "the power of the sun, in the palm of my hand" — whose second attempt grows by feeding, toward a mass that will drown the city's waterfront in its correction. The reactor's function is the story's portrait of the experiment as a grief's monument: it is rebuilt not for science but because stopping would mean attending a funeral. The archive files the reactor as the decade's most consequential fictional experiment: a small sun, extinguished, in a river, by its maker's recovered will.

The arms — the outsourced will

Four AI-driven actuators, grafted to a spine, whose inhibitor chip's loss lets them administer their owner's life: they commit the crimes, rebuild the lab, and whisper — the film's image of a mind's darker counsels given their own hands. The arms' function is the story's portrait of delegation as the villainy's mechanism: Octavius is never more dangerous than when he lets the machinery decide, nor more heroic than when he takes the decisions back. The archive files the arms as the decade's most consequential fictional prosthetics: a will, outsourced, repossessed at the bottom of a river.

The train stop — heroism as a public work

The set piece as a system: a hero's body spent as a brake, a public's hands as the stretcher, a mask returned as a ballot — the covenant's whole machinery in one sequence. The stop's function is the story's portrait of rescue as a two-way infrastructure: he holds the train; they hold him. The archive files the train stop as the decade's most consequential fictional rescue: a brake, made of a person, serviced by a city.

10 / THEMATIC ANALYSIS

Themes & Analysis

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

A hero in all of us — heroism as a practice, not a power

The film's governing theme is its refrain, placed in the mouths of its least powered characters — a doctor, an aunt, a scientist — and demonstrated by its most ordinary crowds: that heroism is not the gift but the use, renewed daily at retail price. The theme is worked out in the film's whole accounting (Peter's powers fail precisely when the practice lapses; the passengers, with no powers at all, perform the film's most heroic act — a kept secret) until the audience holds the film's doctrine: the gift is common; the practice is the miracle. Its proposition reaches every gifted person in the audience coasting on the gift — the talent, the title, the head start: your powers are on a conscience's terms, and they file grievances. The reach is the story's question to every Peter with a flickering strength: is your gift failing — or is your practice, and which of you is on strike?

The invoice — sacrifice itemized in rent and grades

The film's second theme is its bookkeeping: that the superhero's sacrifice, honestly kept, is not the climactic death the genre advertises but the daily arrears — the fired job, the failed course, the landlord's doorway, the beloved's engagement to a solvent man. The theme is worked out in the resignation's legitimacy (Peter's "no more" is not cowardice; it is an accountant's verdict on an unsustainable life) until the audience holds the film's economics: any vocation that cannot itemize its costs will eventually be resigned from, and the resignation will be misread as a failure of character by everyone who never saw the books. Its proposition reaches every burned-out practitioner in the audience — the caregiver, the founder, the public servant: your dumpster moment is a budget document. The reach is the story's question to every hero at a landlord's door: what would your gift cost if you charged your life honestly — and who, besides an aunt, is keeping your books?

Give up the thing you want most — the steady hand

The film's third theme is its climax's doctrine, spoken by the hero to the villain as the terms of both their salvations: that doing right requires, at the hinge, surrendering the dearest object — Peter's normal life, Otto's sun, Harry's vendetta (refused, ominously) — and that the surrender, not the power, is the heroic act. The theme is worked out in the film's symmetries (every major character is offered the bargain; the film grades them by their answers) until the audience holds its moral physics: the arms, the reactor and the arsenal are all machines for not giving something up. Its proposition reaches every holder of a dearest object in the audience — the dream, the grievance, the person: your Octavius moment is scheduled. The reach is the story's question to every scientist at a reactor: what are you building over a grave you have not visited?

The secret kept by others — the covenant's other signature

The film's fourth theme is its train: that a secret hero's survival depends, finally, not on his concealment but on his community's discretion — the passengers' vow is the film's thesis about how societies actually protect their servants: not with laws but with a conspiracy of gratitude. The theme is worked out in the film's doublings (May's tact, the passengers' silence, Jameson's unwitting protection racket of misdirection) until the audience holds the film's civics: every public servant's secret — the nurse's, the officer's, the teacher's private costs — is held, or dropped, by the public it serves. Its proposition reaches every beneficiary of an invisible labor in the audience: you have seen a face you were not supposed to see. The reach is the story's question to every passenger on the train: whose mask are you carrying, and did you give it back?

11 / FULL SPOILER CONCLUSION

Ending Explained

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL THE ENDING

Peter unmasks before Octavius and talks him back to himself; Octavius drowns his reactor and himself in the river, refusing to die a monster. Harry discovers his father's Goblin arsenal behind a mirror. Mary Jane, having learned Peter's secret, leaves John Jameson at the altar and chooses Peter with full knowledge of the risk.

What the ending establishes

  • Octavius's drowning files the villain's redemption as an act of resumed ownership: the arms do not defeat him; he out-ranks them, once, at the price of everything.
  • MJ's arrival files the love story's settlement as an informed signature: the secret is no longer kept from her but with her, and the risk is hers by choice.
  • Harry's mirror files the sequel's engine as an inheritance accepted: the vendetta has a new host, with better funding and a family arsenal.
  • The passengers' kept secret files the city's covenant as ratified: the hero's identity survives its exposure because the exposed-to choose its survival.
  • MJ's face at the window files the ending's honesty: the choice is made and the apprehension is real, in the same expression — the film declines to separate them.

What remains uncertain

Whether Harry's inheritance can be interrupted, whether Peter's renegotiated terms (a partner who knows) can hold against the life's arithmetic, and whether Octavius's redemption can survive its own secrecy — all stand open at the film's close (the third film takes them up). Within this film, the archive files the uncertainty as the story's own last word on its theme: the gift is re-signed, the train is stopped, the sun is drowned — and the rent, the film's last laugh and first principle, is still due.

12 / PURPOSES OF THE STORY

Moral of The Story

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Spider-Man 2 uses a resigning hero, a widowed scientist and a train full of witnesses to examine vocation, sacrifice and discretion: a gift that fails with its owner's will; a villain whose machinery is grief with a power source; and a city that repays its hero in the only currency he needs — his secret, kept. Through the dumpster, the monologue and the river, the film argues that heroism is a practice renewed daily at retail price; that any calling's costs, unitemized, will be collected as burnout; and that doing right, at the hinge, means giving up the thing you want the most — even your dreams.

Why tell this story?

Because Raimi and Sargent understood that the second film about a hero had to be about the heroism's price list — that the genre's founding fantasy (powers as pure gift) survives contact with a rent bill only if the film keeps the books honestly. They told it as a drama with set pieces rather than the reverse because the train only matters if the passengers have watched the driver fail his classes. The result is the decade's defining superhero film, the archive files: a blockbuster whose climax is a conversation and whose most heroic act is a silence.

1. Itemize the gift's invoice before it resigns

Peter's arrears are the film's first counsel, for every practitioner of a costly calling — the caregiver, the founder, the artist, the officer: keep the books of your vocation honestly and currently (the rent, the grades, the relationships, the sleep), because an unitemized sacrifice does not disappear; it accrues, and collects itself as a resignation you will misdiagnose as a failure of character. The counsel is an accounting doctrine with the dumpster as its ledger: Peter's "no more" was a budget document in a costume. Renegotiate your terms while you still have the strength to sign — the flicker in your powers is a memo, not a mystery.

2. Renegotiate; do not just endure

The return's terms are the film's second counsel, for every burned-out hero considering the dumpster: Peter does not come back to the same life — he comes back to a renegotiated one (a partner who knows, an aunt whose terms are grace, a practice resumed as a choice rather than a sentence), and the film files the distinction as the difference between recovery and relapse. The counsel is a labor doctrine with the re-signed gift as its contract: if your return requires the identical schedule that broke you, you have not returned; you have reported for the next collapse. Change one term that matters. A secret shared is a load halved, even when the sharing is the scariest item on the agenda.

3. Address the widower, not the machine

The reactor scene is the film's third counsel, for every negotiator facing a person administered by their own machinery — the addict's habit, the executive's metrics, the friend's grievance with its arsenal: the way back is rarely through the machinery's arguments; it is through the person the machinery is protecting from a funeral. The counsel is a negotiation doctrine with Octavius's recovered will as its proof: Peter wins by quoting the man's own doctrine to the man, in the man's own student's voice. Learn the sentence your opponent taught in their better days. It is the one key their arms do not hold.

4. Keep the secrets your city entrusts you

The train is the film's fourth counsel, for every beneficiary of an invisible service — the patient, the passenger, the citizen: you will, in the course of being served, see faces you were not meant to see (the exhausted nurse's, the young officer's, the teacher's private arithmetic), and your discretion is the wage that costs you nothing and pays them everything. The counsel is a civic doctrine with the returned mask as its instrument: the passengers' vow is the film's model of a public's honor. Carry the body gently. Give the mask back. Tell no one. It is the only tax the covenant levies, and it is levied in silence.

5. Deliver the monologue; withhold the interrogation

May's moving day is the film's fifth counsel, for every elder, mentor and friend of a person whose secret life is failing: the intervention that works is a testimony, not an investigation — she states what heroes are for, in the hearing of the one person who needs it, and then, with an actress's timing, changes the subject and lets him keep his secret and his dignity in the same gesture. The counsel is a mentorship doctrine with the packed boxes as its chamber: she does not ask; she tells, and trusts the telling. If you know, or half-know, consider that your knowing may be most useful unannounced. Some re-enlistments are effected entirely by monologue.

6. Run from the altar if the signature isn't informed

MJ's wedding exit is the film's sixth counsel, its benediction, for every person approaching a life's signature — a marriage, a partnership, a merger — on incomplete information: the ceremony can wait; the information cannot, and a choice made without the material fact is not a commitment but a clerical error with flowers. The counsel is a consent doctrine with the wedding dress as its running gear: she does not leave John because he is wanting; she leaves because the true choice was never presented, and she runs — literally, through the city — to make the real one. Whatever your altar is, get the fact first. Then sign like she signs: in person, at a run, with the risks itemized and accepted.

The moral of the story is…

Keep the gift's books honestly; renegotiate the terms instead of resigning; address the widower, not the machine — and keep, in silence, the secrets your city shows you.

Spider-Man 2 teaches that there is a hero in all of us, and that he is kept honest not by his powers but by his practice; that sacrifice unitemized becomes resignation; and that the bravest sentence in the world is a scientist's, at the bottom of his own sun: I will not die a monster.

The film's final question is therefore not whether you would stop the train — the film files that under "gift." It is what your invoice currently says; which of your arms is making your decisions; and whether, handed a young man's unconscious body and his secret in the same moment, your hands would know what the passengers' knew.

Editorial note: This is an interpretive reading of the film's themes, not a claim about every private intention of its creators or a prediction of future franchise events.
