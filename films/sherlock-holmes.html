Sherlock Holmes

Lord Blackwood has risen from the grave — or says he has — and is murdering his way toward a coup of Parliament by black magic. The only obstacle is a bohemian detective who fights like a bare-knuckle chemist and reasons like a machine, and the army doctor who keeps him, barely, on the rails. Guy Ritchie's kinetic reinvention of Doyle: Holmes as action hero, Watson as partner rather than narrator, and every "miracle" in London awaiting its explanation.

01 / FILM IDENTITY

Overview

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Sherlock Holmes is a 2009 period mystery action film directed by Guy Ritchie, starring Robert Downey Jr. as Arthur Conan Doyle's detective, reimagined — from Lionel Wigram's original comic-book-style treatment — as a bohemian, bare-knuckle, explosively physical man of science. The film opens at the end of a case: Holmes and Dr. John Watson (Jude Law) stop the aristocratic occultist Lord Henry Blackwood (Mark Strong) mid-ritual-murder, and Blackwood is tried and hanged — after summoning Holmes to his cell to promise, with a condemned man's serenity, that death is only the beginning and three more deaths will follow. When Blackwood's tomb is found shattered from the inside, his coffin occupied by a dead dwarf, and a terrified gravedigger swears he saw the lord walk out of it, London acquires a resurrected devil and Holmes acquires the film's real subject: a conspiracy — the Temple of the Four Orders, a secret society of aristocrats and officials practicing ceremonial magic as a technology of rule — that has hired Blackwood's "supernatural" return as the cover for a coup: a machine, assembled from components stolen across the film (a chemical weapon triggered by radio signal), to be detonated beneath Parliament and presented to a terrified nation as sorcery. Around the case, the film runs its second plot, which is its heart: Watson is leaving. Engaged to Mary Morstan (Kelly Reilly), moving out of Baker Street, taking his revolver, his medical bag and his stabilizing influence with him — and Holmes, who cannot file his partner under "solved," sabotages the transition with the dedication of a man dismantling a bomb he refuses to admit is a wedding. Complicating everything is Irene Adler (Rachel McAdams), the only person ever to outwit Holmes, now working for a shadowed employer whose interest in the machine's components will, in the film's final minutes, acquire a name the audience supplies: Moriarty. It grossed $524 million worldwide on a $90 million budget — the eighth-highest-grossing film of 2009 and Ritchie's biggest hit — won Downey the Golden Globe for Best Actor in a Musical or Comedy, and earned Oscar nominations for Hans Zimmer's score and for art direction. It endures as the adaptation that restored Doyle's own forgotten Holmes — the boxer, chemist and monograph-writing eccentric of the original stories — beneath a century of deerstalker gentility, and proved the detective could carry an action franchise without surrendering the deduction that makes him Holmes.

Place in the culture

The film that detonated the Holmes renaissance of the following decade — arriving a year before the BBC's Sherlock, it established the modern template: a young, physical, socially catastrophic Holmes whose deductions are staged as action sequences and whose partnership with Watson is the story's emotional plot. Its deposits are the slow-motion fight-planning sequence (Holmes simulating a beating in advance, blow by costed blow) as a permanent action-cinema device, Zimmer's detuned-piano score as the sound of Victorian London rebuilt as a brawl, and Downey's Globe-winning performance as the keystone of his post-comeback reign — proof, a year after Iron Man, that the decade's defining movie star would be a fast-talking addict-genius in any century. It endures because it understood Doyle better than its critics did: the original Holmes always was an action hero — the stories merely lacked the budget to show the fights he narrates in a clause.

Principal cast

The detective rebuilt as a brawler

The production originated with producer Lionel Wigram's conviction that a century of stage-and-screen gentility had buried Doyle's actual character — a man the stories describe boxing, bending fire-pokers and experimenting on himself — and his treatment, developed as a comic-book-style reimagining, became the film's charter. Ritchie shot London as his earlier crime films' ancestor: a mud-and-steam metropolis under construction (Tower Bridge rising half-built over the climax, as the era itself is under construction), photographed by Philippe Rousselot in browns and gaslight. Downey, cast at the height of his comeback, trained in Wing Chun for the fight sequences and played the deduction scenes at the tempo of the brawls, so that mind and body run at one speed. Zimmer's score — built on a detuned pub piano bought for a few hundred pounds, banjo, cimbalom and scraping strings — was nominated for the Academy Award, as was the production design. Released on Christmas Day 2009 into the teeth of Avatar's second weekend, the film nonetheless grossed $524 million, finished eighth for the year, and launched a franchise whose second installment followed in 2011.

03 / NARRATIVE SUMMARY

Story Synopsis

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL FULL STORY SUMMARY

The film opens in a crypt beneath London, at the climax of a case the audience joins in its last ninety seconds: Holmes and Watson race through torchlight to stop Lord Henry Blackwood — aristocrat, occultist, and serial murderer of five young women — from completing a sixth ritual killing, which they do in a brawl that establishes the film's grammar at once (Holmes planning fights in slow-motion voiceover, each blow pre-costed like a chess line; Watson, ex-Afghanistan, fighting beside him as an equal). Blackwood is tried, sentenced, and hanged at Pentonville — but not before summoning Holmes to the condemned cell to deliver the plot: death, Blackwood promises, is a doorway; he will return, and three more deaths will remake the world. Three months later the partnership that caught him is dissolving on schedule: Watson is engaged to Mary Morstan, touring the new house, packing his things out of 221B while Holmes — marooned in the flat among experiments, a dog repeatedly anesthetized for science, and a violin he plays at flies to study their flight — responds to the loss of his Boswell with sabotage, spite and a disastrous dinner at the Royal Café in which he deduces Mary's entire past off her jewelry and is rewarded with wine in his face. Then Blackwood's tomb is found burst open from inside, the coffin holds the corpse of Luke Reordan — a red-haired dwarf, a scientist in Blackwood's employ — the gravedigger has fled raving, and London's newspapers have a resurrection. Inspector Lestrade, whose career is staked on the case, drags Holmes back: and Holmes, delighted past decency to have the game again, sets about demonstrating that every element of the "miracle" is a mechanism — the tomb's stone shattered by a chemical treatment, the "risen" lord a living man who never died: the hanging itself was the fraud's foundation, accomplished with a trick harness and a paralytic, the execution bought and staged by the conspiracy that employs him.

The conspiracy assembles itself clue by clue, each clue a set-piece. Reordan's laboratory — a slaughterhouse district workshop of cages, chemicals and a giant French enforcer named Dredger — yields the components list: Blackwood's staged deaths (a drowning in a bath that was a paralytic and a pump; a self-immolation that was a treated coat and a chemical rain) are demonstrations, advertising a power the Temple of the Four Orders intends to spend on its coup. The Temple — a secret order of the powerful, fronted by Sir Thomas Rotheram and administered in government by the Home Secretary, Lord Coward — practices magic as statecraft and has built, from Reordan's science, a device: a chemical weapon to be triggered by radio signal beneath the House of Parliament, killing every member who has not taken the Order's antidote, and leaving a terrified nation to be ruled by the men who alone were spared by the "sorcery." Irene Adler threads through the case as its second author: hired by a shadowed professor to obtain the machine's key component — a fact she conceals from Holmes even as she saves him, betrays him, and is saved by him in the film's best running exchange of professional courtesies — and her strand ends with the component stolen from her unconscious body by her employer's own hand, and the employer still unseen. The climax is staged on the half-built Tower Bridge, where Blackwood — his Parliament device discovered and disabled by Watson in the sewers below, in a race against the radio signal — attempts to announce his new order to the city and is fought, chained and finally hanged in earnest by the bridge's own machinery, Holmes reciting the fraud's whole explanation as the noose does the state's work properly this time. The coda restores the board and names the next game: Watson will marry and move out — Holmes attends the engagement with his blessing half-given and his deductions, for once, kind — Adler is gone, and the component is in the hands of the professor whose name Holmes speaks to the empty room as the film's last deduction: Moriarty. The game, the detective notes with something indistinguishable from joy, is afoot again.

The full narrative summary is an editorial paraphrase, not dialogue or a scene-by-scene transcript.

04 / STORY STRUCTURE

Story & Chronology

The film unfolds in London in 1890. The sequence below follows the film's narrative order.

Act I — The hanging and the tomb

A ritual stopped, a lord hanged with a promise — and a coffin found holding the wrong corpse.

Act II — The mechanisms

A dwarf's laboratory, a lady's employer, a partner's engagement — every miracle itemized, every farewell resisted.

Act III — The bridge

A device under Parliament disarmed in the dark — and the magician hanged by his own machinery, while an unseen professor collects the prize.

05 / CHARACTER DATABASE

Character Database

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Search the principal characters documented in this film. Roles and motivations below summarize their function in the 2009 movie.

THE DETECTIVE

Sherlock Holmes

Robert Downey Jr. A consulting detective of genius and chaos — a chemist, boxer, violinist and monograph author whose rooms at 221B are a laboratory, a gymnasium and a slow-motion domestic disaster, and whose method the film restores to Doyle's original specification: observation as a contact sport. Downey plays him as the decade's definitive brilliant addict — of cases, not only of the tinctures Watson confiscates — a man whose mind is a machine that must be fed problems and whose response to his partner's engagement is the film's truest portrait of him: he cannot solve Watson, so he sabotages the furniture. His function is the story's portrait of reason as a temperament rather than a procedure: Holmes's deductions are staged as fights (planned in slow motion, blow by costed blow) because in this telling the mind and the body are one instrument, and both are useless — and dangerous — when unemployed. The archive files him as the adaptation's founding argument: that Doyle's detective was always an action hero, and the century of armchair versions was the abridgement.

THE PARTNER

Dr. John Watson

Jude Law. An army surgeon of Afghanistan, wounded, decorated and indispensable — Holmes's roommate, chronicler, medical officer, conscience and right hand, now engaged to be married and in the process of moving out, which the film treats, correctly, as the case beneath the case. Law plays him as the adaptation's quiet revolution: not the bumbling narrator of a hundred films but a crack shot, a gambler, a brawler and the only man alive who can manage Holmes — a partner whose competence the detective's genius requires and whose departure the detective's heart refuses to process. His function is the story's portrait of friendship as an operating system: Watson is the interface between Holmes and the world (landladies, clients, fiancées, consequences), and the film's plot is what happens when the interface announces its resignation. The archive files him as the decade's most corrective Watson: the proof that the great detective's greatest deduction was choosing, once, the right man to stand next to.

THE WOMAN

Irene Adler

Rachel McAdams. An American adventuress — opera singer, jewel thief, professional survivor — and the only person in the canon ever to have outwitted Holmes, a distinction the film honors by having her beat him, charm him, rob him and save him in rotation, while conducting her own commission for an employer she will not name. McAdams plays her with a bright, transactional warmth that keeps every scene double-exposed: Adler's affection for Holmes is genuine, and so is her professionalism, and the film's respect for her is that it never requires her to choose between them on camera. Her function is the story's portrait of the equal: Adler is the one character whose operating system matches Holmes's — observation, improvisation, disguise — and whose presence therefore converts every scene into a negotiation between sovereigns. The archive files her as the decade's most faithful Adler: not a love interest pasted onto the detective, but the canon's own verdict — the woman — filed by Holmes himself, in the superlative, forever.

THE MAGICIAN

Lord Henry Blackwood

Mark Strong. An aristocratic occultist and serial murderer — hanged for five ritual killings and apparently risen to commit the three more his prophecy requires — who is, beneath the theater, a fraud of genius: a showman of death whose "miracles" are chemistry, machinery and bribery, deployed by a secret order as the advertising for a coup. Strong plays him with a sepulchral calm that makes him the film's most disciplined performer: Blackwood never breaks character, because the character is the weapon — the terror of the supernatural is the coup's entire first phase. His function is the story's portrait of magic as a technology of rule: Blackwood does not believe his own liturgy (his employers barely do either), and the film's cold point is that belief is unnecessary — a population's belief is sufficient, and can be manufactured with a trick harness, a treated coat and a dwarf's corpse. The archive files him as the decade's most instructive charlatan: a man hanged twice by the same state, the second time by accident of architecture, which is the fate his fraud had earned the first time.

THE INSPECTOR

Inspector Lestrade

Eddie Marsan. Scotland Yard's man on the Blackwood case — a competent, exhausted professional whose relationship with Holmes is the film's portrait of institutional pride under a genius's tenancy: Lestrade arrests him, uses him, resents him, protects him and finally conspires with him, in a single case, without any of the five postures canceling the others. Marsan plays him with a put-upon dignity that keeps him human at the exact size the role requires, and his function is the story's portrait of the official as the unofficial's necessary foil: Holmes can be Holmes because Lestrade holds the warrant, the uniform and the blame — the state's paperwork given a face, a temper and, at the climax, a conspirator's courage. The archive files him as the decade's most honest policeman: a man who knows precisely what the detective is worth and precisely what he costs, and pays both bills, grumbling, in full.

THE FIANCÉE

Mary Morstan

Kelly Reilly. A governess of poise and hidden depths — Watson's intended, whose introduction to Holmes is a dinner at which the detective, auditioning his worst self, deduces her whole history (a fiancé lost, a fortune's shadow, a governess's post) off her person and receives her wine in his face for the performance. Reilly plays her with a steel that retroactively justifies the wine: Mary is the film's only character who audits Holmes at his own game and declines to be intimidated by the instrument, and her function is the story's portrait of the future arriving in person — she is not an obstacle to the partnership but its next chapter, and the film's wisdom is to cast her as someone Watson's world gains rather than someone Holmes's world loses. The archive files her as the decade's most efficient fiancée: a woman who meets the great detective at his most impossible, throws wine at him, and is filed by the narrative — correctly — as the adult in the room.

THE MINISTER

Lord Coward

Hans Matheson. The Home Secretary — the Temple of the Four Orders' man inside the government, Blackwood's political handler, and the conspiracy's interface with the state it intends to inherit. Matheson plays him with a smooth, ministerial menace — Coward is the coup's administrative class given a face: no liturgy, no theater, just the scheduling of a massacre as a policy outcome. His function is the story's portrait of treason as a civil-service function: the device beneath Parliament requires permits, budgets and a minister to keep the police looking elsewhere, and Coward is the proof that every coup is, at the working level, a paperwork achievement. The archive files him as the decade's most quietly frightening official: a man whose name is the film's one joke at his expense, and whose conduct — arranging the murder of Parliament between meetings — earns it in full.

THE GRAND MASTER

Sir Thomas Rotheram

James Fox. The Temple of the Four Orders' senior figure — an establishment eminence (and, the film notes in passing, Blackwood's father) who presides over the order's rites with the serenity of a man for whom secret government is a family business. Fox plays him in a few scenes of perfect, patrician stillness, and Sir Thomas's function is the story's portrait of the conspiracy's true altitude: beneath Blackwood's theater and Coward's paperwork sits the permanent establishment, for whom the coup is not a crime but a succession plan. His death — in his bath, by Blackwood's staged "sorcery," as the Order's own demonstrations consume its master — is the archive's chosen verdict on him: the man who hired magic as a contractor, drowned by the show.

THE ENFORCER

Dredger

Robert Maillet. A giant French strongman in Blackwood's employ — the conspiracy's muscle, a dockside mountain who fights Holmes and Watson together through a shipyard and a half-launched vessel in the film's most purely physical set-piece. Maillet, a former professional wrestler, plays him with a silent, professional enormity, and Dredger's function is the story's portrait of scale as an argument: against Holmes's costed, choreographed science, the film sets a man who is simply too large for the arithmetic — the one opponent the slow-motion planning cannot fully price — until the shipyard's own machinery (a launching hull, a chain, gravity) is recruited as the detective's second. The archive files him as the decade's most honest heavy: a mountain with a French accent, defeated not by a better fighter but by a better reading of the building.

THE LANDLADY

Mrs. Hudson

Geraldine James. The keeper of 221B Baker Street — landlord, housekeeper and long-suffering witness to a tenancy that includes explosions, anesthetized dogs and a lodger who shoots the walls — whose appearances are the film's domestic baseline: the ordinary London that the chaos upstairs is, in theory, defending. James plays her with an exasperated fondness that the film uses as a measuring instrument (Mrs. Hudson's tolerance is the partnership's credit rating with the real world), and her function is the story's portrait of the home front of genius: every great man's legend is underwritten by somebody who brings the tea, absorbs the damage and declines, on the evidence, to evict the whirlwind. The archive files her as the decade's most patient landlady: the woman who has read the same evidence as everyone else — the holes, the smells, the dog — and reached the detective's own conclusion, that the man is worth the repairs.

THE CONSTABLE

Constable Clark

William Houston. A Scotland Yard constable assigned, to his visible cost, to Holmes — escort, minder and occasional arresting officer — whose deadpan endurance of the detective's escapes, disguises and reappearances makes him the film's portrait of the working policeman's actual war: not crime, but Holmes. Houston plays him in a handful of scenes of perfect comic timing, and Clark's function is the story's ground-level verification of its thesis: the state's machinery and the detective's genius are not rivals but colleagues in a long marriage, and the marriage's daily texture is a constable opening a carriage door to find the prisoner gone and the evening ruined. The archive files him as the decade's most put-upon officer: the man who has arrested Sherlock Holmes more times than anyone alive, with a success rate the film files, respectfully, under "pending."

THE PROFESSOR

Professor Moriarty

Unseen. Irene Adler's employer — a shadow in a carriage, a voice, a hand that lifts the stolen component from her unconscious body — whose existence the film withholds as its final card, dealt face-down: the Napoleon of crime, present throughout as an interest in the machine and a price on its parts. Moriarty's function, in absentia, is the story's portrait of the true altitude of the game: Blackwood, the Temple and the coup are, by the coda's arithmetic, a provincial affair — a device built by amateurs, whose one valuable component is collected, at the end, by the professional. The archive files him as the decade's most economical villain: a character who never appears, never speaks above a murmur, and walks out of the film with its prize and its sequel — the only figure in the story who out-plans the detective, achieved entirely by declining, this time, to be in it.

06 / SCOTLAND YARD, THE TEMPLE & THE UNDERWORLD

Scotland Yard, the Temple & the Underworld

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
THE LAW

Scotland Yard

The Metropolitan Police as the film draws it — earnest, outmatched, and in a permanent negotiated settlement with the amateur it cannot do without. The Yard's function is the story's portrait of institutions under a genius's tenancy: it holds the warrants, the cells and the blame, and it rents the brilliance it cannot employ, at the price of its dignity, case by case. The Yard ends the film with its case closed and its detective unarrested, the archive files it as the decade's most realistic police force: an organization whose most effective officer is a civilian it periodically handcuffs.

THE CONSPIRACY

The Temple of the Four Orders

A secret society of the powerful — peers, ministers, eminences — practicing ceremonial magic as a philosophy of rule and preparing a chemical coup beneath Parliament. The Temple's function is the story's portrait of the establishment's occult self-image: men who already govern Britain, conspiring to govern it forever, and dressing the ambition in liturgy because naked ambition would embarrass the minutes. The Temple ends the film decapitated by its own demonstrations, the archive files it as the decade's most complete secret society: an order whose magic was fraud, whose fraud was policy, and whose policy was the oldest one — that the men in the room should remain the men in the room.

THE CITY BELOW

London's underworld

The docks, slaughterhouses, opium dens and prize-fights through which the case travels — the London of Dredger and Reordan's laboratory, where the conspiracy's components are built by the city's invisible trades. The underworld's function is the story's portrait of the capital's second economy: every miracle the Temple stages is manufactured down here, by craftsmen the establishment will never meet, in workshops the police will never find without a guide. The archive files it as the decade's most atmospheric London: a city whose official map is a courtesy, and whose real geography — like its real government — is drawn by whoever knows the workshops.

THE PARTNERSHIP

221B Baker Street

A two-man institution — detective and doctor, laboratory and surgery, chaos and order — whose dissolution-by-engagement is the film's emotional case and whose survival, re-negotiated, is its verdict. 221B's function is the story's portrait of the working household as a technology: the partnership is not a friendship with a practice attached but a single distributed mind, one half of which is currently packing. The household ends the film amended rather than ended, the archive files it as the decade's most consequential address: the rooms where the modern world learned that genius is a team sport with terrible manners.

Magic is a procurement process

The film's factional design is a supply chain: the Temple supplies the money and the minister, Blackwood supplies the performance, Reordan's workshop supplies the mechanisms, and Scotland Yard supplies — unwillingly, indispensably — the audience of officials whose terror is the product. Against the chain stands a two-man firm with no budget and perfect information flow. The archive files the design as the story's thesis in organization: conspiracies are procurement processes with liturgy, and they are beaten not by larger conspiracies but by smaller units with shorter distances between the eye and the hand.

07 / WORLD & SETTING

World & Setting

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

London, 1890 — the city under construction

A capital photographed as a building site of empire: mud, steam, gaslight, half-risen bridges and a skyline of scaffolds — Victorian London rendered not as heritage but as a going concern, dirty and accelerating. The setting's function is the story's argument in urban form: this is a world mid-manufacture, in which a detective can plausibly be an action hero because the city itself is — everything in it (the bridge, the Parliament, the chemical industry, the radio age approaching) is half-built and therefore contestable. The archive files the setting as the decade's most influential Victorian London: the one that taught a generation of productions to film the nineteenth century as a construction zone rather than a museum.

Tower Bridge, half-built — the climax's scaffold

The film's final stage: the great bridge at mid-assembly, its towers risen and its span incomplete — a machine for crossing that cannot yet be crossed, on which Blackwood attempts to announce his new order and is hanged by the construction's own chains. The bridge's function is the story's portrait of the era judging its magician: the coup's theater was a tomb, a bathhouse and a Parliament cellar, but its executioner is the age's signature artifact — engineering, unfinished and unimpressed. The archive files the bridge as the decade's most symbolic building site: the future, at half-completion, already strong enough to hang the past's performance from its scaffolding.

08 / THE RESURRECTION FRAUD, THE DEVICES & DEDUCTION

The Resurrection Fraud, the Devices & Deduction

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

The fraud — every miracle itemized

The film's central mechanism, and its epistemology: Blackwood's resurrection is a stack of purchasable mechanisms — a trick harness and a bribed hangman for the execution; a chemically weakened tombstone for the "bursting" grave; a corpse (Reordan's) planted in the coffin; paralytics, pumps and treated fabrics for the staged deaths that advertise the power. The fraud's function is the story's doctrine performed as plot: in Holmes's world there are no miracles, only mechanisms whose invoices haven't been read — and the detective's whole method is invoice-reading, conducted at speed, in disguise, under fire. The archive files the fraud as the decade's most satisfying demystification: a resurrection with a supply chain, defeated by the one man in London who insists on seeing the receipts.

The device — sorcery by radio signal

The Temple's weapon: a chemical dispenser installed in the sewers beneath Parliament, triggered remotely by radio — 1890's cutting edge deployed as the coup's "magic" — with the Order's members pre-dosed with the antidote so that survival itself reads as election by the powers. The device's function is the story's portrait of technology's oldest political use: the presentation of a mechanism as a miracle, so that a massacre reads as a judgment and its survivors as the chosen. The archive files the device as the decade's most prescient fictional weapon: a false-flag atrocity with a liturgy attached, disarmed in the dark by a doctor with a revolver and a working knowledge of sewers — the unglamorous logistics by which such miracles are actually stopped.

Deduction as choreography — the planned fight

The film's signature device: Holmes's fights are simulated in advance, in slow motion, with each blow's target, effect and cost narrated — then executed at full speed, exactly as planned, when the simulation's author judges the moment right. The choreography's function is the story's thesis about its hero's mind: deduction is not a parlor narration after the fact but a real-time physics of bodies, and the same instrument that reads a watch's scratches prices a jaw's breaking point. The archive files the device as the decade's most imitated action grammar: a fight the audience watches twice — once as thought, once as deed — and thereby understands as the detective's true native medium.

The engagement — the unsolvable case

The film's second engine, disguised as its comedy: Watson's departure for marriage, against which Holmes deploys his entire arsenal (sabotage, insult, the disastrous dinner) with a success rate of zero — because the case is not a puzzle but a loss, and his method has no department for it. The engagement's function is the story's portrait of genius's limit: the detective who can itemize a resurrection cannot itemize his own objection to his friend's happiness, and the film's maturity is to let the objection stand unitemized — felt, behaved, never solved — until it matures, at the end, into a blessing given badly and meant entirely. The archive files the engagement as the decade's most honest subplot: the one mystery in the film that is closed not by an explanation but by an acceptance.

10 / THEMATIC ANALYSIS

Themes & Analysis

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Every miracle has an invoice — demystification as heroism

The film's governing theme is its detective's doctrine: the supernatural is a supply chain that hasn't been audited yet. The theme is worked out in the plot's whole structure — each of Blackwood's wonders (the resurrection, the drownings, the immolation) is granted its full terror first, and then itemized: the harness, the pump, the treated coat, the planted corpse. Its proposition is a standing instrument for the audience's own century of manufactured wonders — viral miracles, engineered panics, atrocities with liturgies attached: the correct response to a marvel that arrives with a political program is not belief or debunking-by-sneer but procurement analysis: who built it, who paid, whose antidote was issued in advance. The reach is the story's audit for every citizen presented with a wonder that conveniently requires obedience: ask for the workshop. There is always a workshop, and it is always in a district the powerful never visit, staffed by craftsmen whose names — like Reordan's — the liturgy omits.

The unsolvable partner — genius and the limits of method

The film's second theme is its comedy's serious core: Holmes's method, omnipotent against fraud, is useless against change. The theme is worked out in the engagement plot's grading: every technique in the detective's arsenal — deduction (the dinner), sabotage (the sabotaged courtship), surveillance, spite — is deployed against Watson's marriage and scores zero, because the problem is not information but attachment, and attachment is the one substance his laboratory cannot process. Its proposition reaches every brilliant person in the audience whose competence has become their only instrument: the method that solves the world's cases will not solve the household's, and the attempt to apply it — deducing the fiancée at dinner — produces the wine in the face that the film files as data. The reach is the story's question to every Holmes at every threshold: which of your current cases is actually a farewell you are refusing to attend, and what would it cost you to put down the magnifying glass and simply go to dinner as a guest?

Magic as statecraft — the coup that needs a liturgy

The film's third theme is the Temple's insight, which the film treats as the era's most modern idea: that a massacre, to be politically useful, must be presentable as a miracle. The theme is worked out in the coup's design — the device is real, but its packaging is the weapon: the antidote issued to the elect, the terror pre-advertised by Blackwood's demonstrations, the succession framed as sorcery's judgment — so that the survivors govern not by consent or even by force but by the population's interpretation of an event. Its proposition generalizes grimly: the powerful rarely lack mechanisms; what they purchase, at Blackwood prices, is interpretation — the story in which their violence is heaven's. The reach is the story's standing instruction for reading any catastrophe that arrives suspiciously well-narrated: separate the device from the liturgy, price each, and notice which one the survivors are selling.

The city half-built — an age that hasn't decided

The film's fourth theme is its setting's: London in 1890 is photographed as scaffolding — the bridge unfinished, the century unfinished — because the film's real subject is a world between dispensations, in which magic and mechanism are still contesting the franchise on reality. The theme is worked out in the film's twin technologies: the Temple's rites (ancient forms, modern procurement) and Holmes's science (modern method, bohemian forms) are both, recognizably, startups — and the climax's verdict, delivered by the half-built bridge's own chains, is that engineering gets the last word not because it is truer but because it is load-bearing. Its proposition is a comfort and a warning to every transitional age, including the audience's: when the old magic and the new mechanism are both performing for the franchise, the deciding artifact is the one that holds weight. The reach is the story's question to every citizen of a scaffolding era: which of the wonders currently on offer would you trust to hang a man — or to hold a bridge — and have you checked whether it was built by the Temple or the workshop?

11 / FULL SPOILER CONCLUSION

Ending Explained

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL THE ENDING

The end is a hanging and a name. Watson disarms the Parliament device in the sewers; on the half-built Tower Bridge, Holmes itemizes Blackwood's entire fraud as they fight, and the bridge's chains hang the magician in earnest. Watson's engagement stands, with Holmes's blessing; Adler is gone; and the machine's key component has been taken by her employer — Professor Moriarty, whose war with Holmes the film leaves as its final, smiling deduction.

What the ending establishes

  • The fraud is fully itemized: Blackwood never died, never rose, and worked no magic — the resurrection was a harness, a bribed execution and a planted corpse, and the coup's "sorcery" was a chemical device with a radio trigger and a pre-issued antidote.
  • The partnership is amended, not dissolved: Watson marries and moves out, and the firm survives the amendment — the film's verdict that the great friendship was a kinship, not a lease, and therefore portable.
  • Adler's strand closes the case's true account: the Temple was never the highest bidder — the component's collection by Moriarty re-prices the entire plot as a skirmish in a larger war the detective has not yet been shown.
  • Blackwood's death by the bridge's machinery establishes the era's verdict in the film's own terms: the magician is executed by engineering — unfinished, unglamorous, load-bearing — without a word of liturgy being required.
  • Holmes's final deduction — the professor's name, spoken to an empty room with evident pleasure — establishes the saga's constitution: the detective does not fear the greater game; he has been, the film suggests, slightly bored without it.

What remains uncertain

Moriarty's design for the component, Adler's ultimate allegiance, and whether the Temple's surviving members will be prosecuted or merely embarrassed — all stand open at the film's close, deliberately. Within this film, the archive files the uncertainty as the detective's own preferred climate: a board reset with one piece known to be in the enemy's hand, a partner married, a landlady owed repairs — and the game, by the only judgment Holmes trusts, afoot.

12 / PURPOSES OF THE STORY

Moral of The Story

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Sherlock Holmes uses a faked resurrection, a chemical coup and a resisted engagement to examine demystification, partnership and the politics of wonder: a magician whose miracles are procurement, a conspiracy that purchases a liturgy for its massacre, and a detective whose method can itemize a resurrection but cannot — and must not — itemize his friend's farewell. Through the tomb, the laboratory and the half-built bridge, the film argues that every miracle has an invoice; that the powerful buy interpretation, not just mechanisms; and that a partnership, like a city, is not diminished by being amended — only by being refused.

Why tell this story?

Because Doyle's detective had spent a century being embalmed by his admirers — the reasoning machine in the dressing gown — and Ritchie and Wigram told it as a brawl to recover the original: the boxer-chemist of the stories, whose deductions were always physical and whose heart was always the unsolved case. They set it against a magician because Holmes's true antagonist was never a criminal but a claim — the claim that the world runs on wonders — and they gave the detective a partner's wedding to lose because the film's argument about reason requires its limit on screen: a mind that can explain a resurrection, defeated at a dinner table by a governess with a wine glass, is the only kind of mind the audience can safely be asked to admire.

1. Ask for the workshop

The fraud is the film's first counsel, for everyone presented with a wonder that arrives with instructions to obey: miracles of political convenience are manufactured, and the manufacture has an address. The counsel is a procurement discipline for citizens: before debating the wonder's meaning, itemize its mechanism — the harness, the pump, the treated coat, the planted evidence — and identify the craftsman, whose name the liturgy always omits and whose workshop is always in a district the powerful never visit. Most manufactured terror collapses at exactly this joint: not under argument, which it is designed to survive, but under inventory, which it is not. The resurrection that cannot produce its receipts is a coup in costume, and the correct response is Holmes's — delight, curiosity, and a request to see the laboratory.

2. Separate the device from the liturgy

The Temple's design is the film's second counsel, for every reader of catastrophes: a massacre's mechanism and its meaning are separate products, separately purchased. The counsel is a two-column audit for any event that arrives suspiciously well-narrated: in one column, the device (what actually happened, by what means, at whose order); in the other, the liturgy (the story in which it was heaven's judgment, history's verdict, or the elect's survival) — and notice that the survivors' antidote was issued before the event, which is the liturgy's signature. Governments and movements rarely lack devices. What they buy, at Blackwood prices, is the interpretation — and the citizen's one reliable countermeasure is to refuse the bundle: grieve the dead in column one, and let column two be priced like the advertising it is.

3. Don't deduce the people you love at dinner

The Royal Café is the film's third counsel, for every brilliant person whose instrument is always in their hand: competence, applied to attachment, is sabotage with footnotes. The counsel is a jurisdiction doctrine: Holmes's deduction of Mary is accurate in every particular and catastrophic in total, because the dinner was not a case and she was not a suspect — she was the future, arriving for inspection, and the inspection was the insult. The discipline is to know which rooms are not laboratories: the farewell, the engagement, the grief, the apology — occasions where the correct instrument is not the magnifying glass but presence, and where the wine in the face is not an injustice but a grading. Put the method down at the threshold of the household. It will keep until morning; the people may not.

4. Let the partnership be amended

Watson's engagement is the film's fourth counsel, for every working household — firm, band, marriage of minds — facing a member's new chapter: the amendment is not the dissolution, unless the founder files it so. The counsel is Holmes's arc, itemized as policy: his sabotage assumes the partnership is a lease on Watson's whole life; the film's verdict is that it is a kinship, which survives addresses, spouses and schedules because its asset was never proximity but election — Watson returns at every summons not because he lives upstairs but because he chooses to, which is the stronger instrument in every particular. Founders who bless the amendment keep the partner. Founders who fight it keep the rooms, the dog and the silence — a laboratory of one, in which the great mind's only remaining case is the echo.

5. Hire no magic you cannot itemize

Sir Thomas's bath is the film's fifth counsel, for every establishment tempted to rent a wonder-worker — the consultant with the proprietary method, the campaign with the dark arts, the board with the visionary whose results cannot be audited: the Temple's fate is the standard outcome. The counsel is a procurement rule with a body count: the Order hired Blackwood for his effects and declined to inspect his mechanisms, and so could not price his ambitions, his loyalties or his demonstrations' tendency to consume the client list. Any supplier whose value depends on your not understanding the method is not a supplier but a successor in training. Itemize, or be itemized — the magician's last demonstration is traditionally performed on the management.

6. Build the bridge while you fight on it

Tower Bridge is the film's sixth counsel, its benediction, for everyone working in a half-finished age — which is to say, in every age on record: the future does not arrive completed and then get used; it is used, at half-completion, as the scaffold for the present's emergencies. The counsel is an engineer's comfort: the bridge that hangs the magician cannot yet carry a carriage, and it is load-bearing anyway — which is the correct expectation for every institution, technology and reform the audience is currently standing on. Do not wait for the span to close before trusting the towers. Check the chains, price the load, and conduct the necessary hangings from the strongest available structure, while the builders — who are also you — continue the work overhead.

The moral of the story is…

Every miracle has an invoice: ask for the workshop, separate the device from the liturgy, and hire no magic you cannot itemize — and at home, put the magnifying glass down, because a partnership is a kinship, not a lease, and it is amended by blessing or dissolved by sabotage.

Sherlock Holmes teaches that demystification is a form of public defense; that the powerful purchase interpretation more eagerly than mechanisms; and that the detective's hardest case — a friend's happiness, proceeding without his permission — is closed not by an explanation but by an acceptance, delivered badly and meant entirely.

The film's final question is therefore not whether you believe in the resurrection — the film files belief as a procurement outcome. It is whose workshop is currently manufacturing your wonders; which of your dinners is actually a farewell you are sabotaging; and whether the bridge you are standing on, half-built as it is, has been honestly load-tested — by you, in advance, before the magician books it for his announcement.
