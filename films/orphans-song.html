01 / FILM IDENTITY

Overview

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Orphan's Song (孤兒行 — "the orphan's progress") is a 1955 Hong Kong Cantonese drama released September 8, 1955. Its surviving English synopsis — MyDramaList's, translated from the Chinese record (Baidu) — lays out a rich-house melodrama of the era's classic pattern: "The wealthy businessman Wu Xiang Fu's family was trying to win the family's wealth. The young and widowed [daughter-in-law] did not obey the woman's way, and she was driven out of the house. She even wanted to poison her son, and she was fortunate to be saved by the old servant Zhong Bo and raise the adult. After a lot of hard work, the mother and child can finally reunite." Through the translation's static, the design is legible and strong: a succession fight in a rich house; a widow expelled for un-womanly conduct; a child — the heir, Wu Zhong-xing — marked by a poison plot (the synopsis's pronoun leaves the poisoner's identity ambiguous in translation: a rival for the inheritance is the melodrama's usual hand); the old servant Zhong Bo as the rescue and the raising; and the long road to a mother-and-child reunion as the ending's promise. Lee, fourteen, plays the young Wu Zhong-xing (少年吳中興) — the heir as a boy, before an adult counterpart takes the role's later years — in support to a distinguished cast: Tang Pik-wan, one of the era's great Cantonese opera singing actresses (the "Song" of the Galaxy's genre line is hers), and Leung Sing-po, the beloved character actor, in the leads, with Lau Hak-suen (the framer of An Orphan's Tragedy, the same year), Ma Siu-ying, Sai Gwa-pau and Wong Cho-san in support. Catalogue listings credit the direction to Chin Tai-suk and Lee Kai, the writing to Lam Fei and Cheng Ban-kiu, and the production to Yiu Ting — credits this archive reports as listed, the aggregators being the record available in English. The Hong Kong Film Archive's Lee retrospective counts Orphan's Song among the "several great Cantonese pictures" of his apprenticeship, and the archive files the film as the year's second orphan for Lee — after Dickens's Frank, a rich house's heir: the same boy, in one season, playing poverty's orphan and property's, as if the industry were testing which end of the ledger his face belonged to.

Place in the culture

The film lives in the culture as a specimen of the rich-house succession melodrama — the genre in which the era's Cantonese cinema processed inheritance, widowhood and the servant's loyalty — carried by opera royalty (Tang Pik-wan's singing is the "Song") and by Leung Sing-po's beloved-servant persona. For the Lee filmography it is the heir credit: the second of his three 1955 orphans-by-circumstance (Frank the foundling, Zhong-xing the heir, and the surrendered children around Ah Cai's office), a season in which Hong Kong's studios kept casting the same boy at every station of the era's family catastrophes.

12 / PURPOSES OF THE STORY

Moral of The Story

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Orphan's Song, as documented, uses a rich house's succession war to examine the disinterested trustee, the hazardous inheritance and the moralized eviction: a widow expelled on a conduct charge that is also a property claim; an heir marked for poison by his own relatives; and an old servant whose exclusion from the prize makes him the only safe pair of hands in the house. Through the mansion, the back door and the servant's quarters, the film argues that custody belongs to those who cannot inherit; that an unsettled fortune endangers its beneficiary; and that a house's truest trustee is usually on its payroll, at its lowest rate.

Why tell this story?

Because the Cantonese melodrama understood the family firm before the family firm had a literature: it knew that an unsettled estate turns kin into claimants, morality into an instrument, and staff into trustees. The translated paragraph preserved the whole machine, and the archive files the result as the shelf's succession audit: a cup, a back door, and a reunion.

1. Give custody to the disinterested

Zhong Bo is the film's first counsel, for every executor, board and family council appointing a custodian amid a contested prize: the servant is safe because he is excluded. The counsel is a trusteeship doctrine with the back door as its standard: when you appoint the guardian of a child, a company or an estate whose beneficiaries are also its suspects, weight disinterest above intimacy — choose the custodian the prize cannot corrupt because the prize cannot reach him — and write his exclusion into the instrument as the qualification it is. The melodrama's audiences understood this instantly. Governance consultants charge for it by the hour.

The poison plot is the film's second counsel, for every patriarch delaying a succession document: Wu Xiang-fu's unsettled fortune is the hazard; the cup is merely its instrument. The counsel is a settlement doctrine with the embrace as its standard: when your own house's long contest finally resolves, let its public closing entry be a restored relationship rather than a distributed asset — the reunion is the only line of the settlement that appreciates — because the contestants' shares will be spent and forgotten at the usual rates, while the reunion, like this film's, is the part of the story still being retold at seventy years. Settle in the currency that survives.

The moral of the story is…

Give custody to the disinterested; settle the fortune before it settles the heir; audit conduct charges near money — and pay the trustee at his true value.

Orphan's Song teaches that the only safe custodian of a contested prize is the person it cannot reach; that an unsettled inheritance endangers its own heir; and that a house's truest love is frequently on its payroll, at its lowest rate.

The film's final question is therefore not who won the fortune — the film files that under "ask the back door." It is who your Zhong Bo is; whether your estate's settlement is written or merely intended; and what your house's trustee will be entered at, when the accounts are finally read aloud.
