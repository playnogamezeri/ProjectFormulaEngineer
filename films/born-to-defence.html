01 / FILM IDENTITY

Overview

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Born to Defence (中華英雄, "Chinese Hero"; also released as Born to Defend and Final Fight) is a 1988 action film that marked the directorial debut of Jet Li, who also stars, with fight choreography by Tsui Siu-Ming. Produced in mainland China by Sil-Metropole and released on February 16, 1988, it is set in Qingdao just after the Second World War. Jet (Li), a soldier returning from the front, finds American servicemen taking the glory for the victory and the run of his hometown. He lodges with Zhang (Zhao Erkang), the comrade who saved his life and was crippled by shrapnel, now pulling a rickshaw — and who tells Jet his daughter is dead, though she lives, unacknowledged, as the bar girl Rui (Song Jia). When Captain Hans (Kurt Roland Petersson) drives recklessly through the streets, the crowd burns his car; in the foreigners' bar, the sailor Bailey (Paulo Tocha) challenges Jet to a formal boxing match. Jet, forbidden his kicks, struggles with the rules, adapts mid-fight, and wins — giving his prize money to the poor and taking rickshaw shifts for Zhang. Bailey's revenge is methodical: he has Zhang beaten and his rickshaw destroyed, and engineers a scheme to install Jet at the bar as a human punching target, beaten bloody for the sailors' practice. Hans, disgusted at the dishonor, attacks Bailey for the scheme and challenges Jet himself; their long match, in torrential rain, dissolves into a mass brawl. Jet reunites Zhang with his daughter — and Bailey kidnaps her: Zhang is shot dead, Rui falls to her death, and Jet is framed and jailed. He escapes, lures Hans and Bailey to a factory, sends Bailey into the furnace — and spares Hans, leaving him a way out.

RELEASE

February 16, 1988

Ninety-two minutes; HK$11,456,731 in Hong Kong, the year's 38th top-grossing film — Li's sole directing credit, before or since.

THE PRODUCTION

The Injured Director

Li, in his early twenties, struggled to complete the film after injuring his nose; choreographer Tsui Siu-ming was recruited to help finish it — the disjointedness reviewers note is partly a medical record.

THE VIEWPOINT

Agitprop, Filed Honestly

Contemporary reference works describe the film's anti-American slant as bordering on agitprop; the archive records the film as the era's most direct statement of the returning soldier's grievance.

Place in the culture

The film lives in the culture as the anomaly of Li's filmography: the one time the most disciplined performer of his generation sat in the director's chair, and chose — not a monk, not a folk hero — a brawling veteran in a occupied-feeling port, played with an anger his later personas sublimated. Its boxing-ring sequence, in which the wushu champion must unlearn his body to satisfy foreign rules and then wins anyway, is the seed of a dozen later films' favorite argument. For this Galaxy it is the hinge between the Shaolin Temple pictures of his teens and the stardom of 1991: the champion, briefly, as auteur.

03 / NARRATIVE SUMMARY

Story Synopsis

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL FULL STORY SUMMARY

Jet comes home from the war to a Qingdao that has been re-tenanted. The Japanese are gone; the Americans are everywhere, taking the credit for the victory and the liberties of conquerors in a liberated city. His old platoon leader Zhang — who saved Jet's life in battle and took shrapnel for it — pulls a rickshaw now, and gives Jet a room. Asked about his daughter, Zhang says she died. She did not: she works as a bar girl under the name Rui, and her father will not acknowledge her. When Captain Hans, an American Navy officer, drives recklessly through the streets and nearly kills a wounded Chinese soldier, the crowd surrounds the car; Jet fights Hans briefly — each impressing the other — and the mob burns the car, and the riot scatters Jet's party to the foreigners' bar, with its boxing ring and its prostitutes and its insults. Bailey, a Navy boxer, challenges Jet formally. In the ring Jet is forbidden everything he knows: fists only. He breaks the rules from ignorance, tries to leave, is kicked back in by Bailey — and then, mid-beating, adapts: boxing's grammar absorbed into kung fu's, until he is superior at both and Bailey is beaten. The Chinese crowd roars; Jet gives the prize money to the poor and takes shifts on Zhang's rickshaw.

Bailey's answer is a curriculum of humiliations. Zhang's rickshaw is destroyed; Zhang himself is beaten and seriously injured after knocking Bailey's car over for flirting with Rui in it. Jet is forced into work at the bar — where the scheme is revealed: he is to be the sailors' living punching target, beaten bloody in practice bouts designed to break him. Hans, learning that Bailey paid the bar owner for the arrangement, attacks Bailey for shaming the Navy, and challenges Jet himself instead — a formal match, honestly meant. The match, fought long in driving rain, infects its audience: Chinese and American soldiers brawl en masse, Bailey tries to kill Jet in the chaos, and Rui is saved by the man she will learn is her father's brother-in-arms. Jet discovers Rui's identity and reunites father and daughter at last. Bailey kidnaps her in answer. Zhang is shot dead by Bailey; Rui falls to her death; and the Americans frame Jet for the killings. He escapes from prison, leads Hans and Bailey to a factory, and finishes the account: Bailey is sent into the furnace. Hans — the honorable enemy — Jet leaves a way out, and takes it.

10 / THEMATIC ANALYSIS

Themes & Analysis

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

The veteran's peace is a second war, unlisted

The film's governing theme is the homecoming's betrayal: the soldiers who won the war return to a city where their sacrifice is unlisted and their labor is priced at a rickshaw. The theme is worked out from the burned car to the furnace until the audience holds its doctrine of the unlisted war: a society that demobilizes its defenders into humiliation has not ended its conflict but privatized it — the fighting continues at the level of vehicles, daughters and bar tabs, with the veterans outnumbered by their own allies. Its proposition reaches every returning soldier of any nation's peace in the audience. The reach is the story's question to every citizen of a victorious country: what is your peace paying the men who bought it — in wages, in credit, in the right to their own daughters' names — and which of your city's bars is currently franchising their beatings?

Adapt to the foreign rules, then beat them

The film's second theme is the boxing match: the champion forbidden his art, learning the imposed grammar mid-beating. The theme is worked out round by round until the audience holds its doctrine of the absorbed rulebook: the visitor's choices are refusal (and irrelevance), submission (and erasure), or absorption — taking the foreign form into the native body until the form is no longer foreign — and the film files the third as the era's whole strategy in one ring. Its proposition reaches every practitioner entering a system whose rules were written against them in the audience. The reach is the story's question to every newcomer to a rigged game: are you currently refusing, submitting, or absorbing — and what would your art look like, three rounds in, with the handicap converted into curriculum?

Distinguish the adversary from the criminal

The film's third theme is the factory allocation: the furnace for Bailey, the open road for Hans. The theme is worked out through the Navy's two faces until the audience holds its doctrine of the discriminated account: the peace's hardest discipline is not courage but taxonomy — the honorable opponent, who fights you under forms, must be answered under forms and dismissed alive, while the criminal, who franchises your beating and murders your family, is answered without them — and confusing the categories, in either direction, is the era's characteristic failure. Its proposition reaches every person with enemies of both kinds in the audience. The reach is the story's question to every holder of a grievance list: which names on yours are Hans and which are Bailey — and have you, in your exhaustion, begun invoicing them at the same rate?

Shame is the war's last shrapnel

The film's fourth theme is Zhang's disowned daughter: the veteran who survived the metal and is killed by the disgrace. The theme is worked out through the reported death until the audience holds its doctrine of the unacknowledged family: the father's lie — she died — is told to protect the family's honor, and it functions as the family's slow executioner, costing the daughter her name and the father his last years, until a comrade's interference performs the recognition the shame forbade. Its proposition reaches every family with an unacknowledged member in the audience. The reach is the story's question to every keeper of a respectable household: who is your Rui — alive, nearby, and officially dead — and what is the going price, in years, of the recognition you are postponing?

11 / FULL SPOILER CONCLUSION

Ending Explained

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL THE ENDING

Bailey kidnaps Rui. Zhang is shot dead by Bailey, and Rui falls to her death. The Americans frame Jet for the killings and he is jailed — but he escapes from prison and leads Hans and Bailey to a factory. There the account is settled: Bailey is sent into the factory furnace, and Jet leaves Captain Hans a way out, sparing the honorable enemy.

What the ending establishes

  • Bailey's furnace files the grudge's career as concluded in the film's industrial grammar: the franchiser of beatings is consumed by the port's machinery, at the veteran's chosen ground.
  • Hans's open road files the film's ethics as discriminating to the last frame: the duel's code survives the war's end, extended even to the enemy captain's exit.
  • The frame-up's collapse files the peace's justice as corrected extra-legally — the film's bleakest filing: the veteran's innocence is established by his escape and his furnace, not by any court.
  • Zhang's and Rui's deaths file the victory as unrecoverable: the reunion Jet engineered lasts one act, and the archive files the timing as the film's refusal of consolation.
  • Jet's survival files the veteran as the peace's remainder: everyone he came home to is buried, and the film closes its account with the defender alive among the machines.

What remains uncertain

The documented accounts close with the factory settlement; Jet's future in the new China — the civil war already gathering beyond the frame — lies outside the film, and the archive files the uncertainty as the story's own last word on its theme: the veteran of 1945 has won his bar, his ring and his factory, and the century is not finished with him yet. It never is. That is the film's final, quiet filing.

12 / PURPOSES OF THE STORY

Moral of The Story

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Born to Defence uses a rickshaw, a rigged ring and a factory furnace to examine the veteran's peace, the absorbed rulebook, and the discrimination of enemies: a soldier written out of his own victory; a champion who learns the foreign grammar mid-beating; a captain honored and a criminal burned, by the same hand, in the same hour. Through the bar, the rain and the factory, the film argues that a peace which humiliates its defenders has merely privatized its war; that the answer to rules written against you is absorption, not refusal; and that justice, when the courts are framed, is the discrimination of the account — furnace and open road, correctly addressed.

Why tell this story?

Because the champion of China, at twenty-two, wanted to direct his generation's fathers: the soldiers of 1945, whose victory was garrisoned by their allies and whose wages were rickshaws. The archive files the result as the rawest film in this run — uneven, furious, and unlike anything its director-star would permit himself again — and therefore among the most valuable: the one place the anger is not yet choreographed into grace.

1. Pay the veterans in the peace's first budget

The rickshaw rank is the film's first counsel, for every citizen of a victorious country: the defenders' wages are the peace's founding document. The counsel is a civic doctrine with Zhang's shafts as its standard: a society that prices its lifesavers at subsistence and its garrison at luxury has announced its real history of the war, whatever its ceremonies say — and the announcement compounds, at bar-room rates, into riots and furnaces. Audit your own peace's first budget: whose names are in it, whose are missing, and which of your city's veterans is currently pulling the equivalent of a rickshaw past your window?

2. Absorb the rulebook; don't refuse it

The boxing match is the film's second counsel, for every entrant into a system whose rules were written against them: absorption is the third option. The counsel is a strategy doctrine with the ring as its standard: refusal keeps your purity and loses the purse; submission keeps the purse and loses the art; absorption — three rounds of being beaten by the grammar while you learn it — ends with the grammar yours and the purse following. The discipline is in the middle rounds, where the learning looks exactly like losing. Budget for them. Announce nothing. Let the final round do the talking.

3. Keep two lists: adversaries and criminals

The factory allocation is the film's third counsel, for every holder of grievances: taxonomy is justice's first act. The counsel is a discrimination doctrine with the furnace and the road as its standard: the adversary who fights you under forms — however hard, however foreign — is owed forms in return, and an exit; the criminal who franchises your humiliation and murders your family is owed the furnace; and the exhaustion that merges the lists is the precise moment your justice becomes the thing you were fighting. Maintain the lists in writing, in peacetime, with the criteria stated. Your future tired self will thank you at the factory door.

4. Acknowledge the daughter now

Zhang's lie is the film's fourth counsel, for every keeper of a respectable house: the unacknowledged member is the family's open wound. The counsel is a kinship doctrine with the reported death as its standard: the shame that files a living daughter as dead does not protect the family's honor; it mortgages the family's remaining years to a fiction that any bar girl's face can foreclose. The recognition, when Jet finally engineers it, is the film's happiest scene — and it lasts one act. Whatever your house's equivalent acknowledgment is, perform it in the season when it can still be enjoyed, not merely survived.

5. Give the prize money away in public

Jet's purse is the film's fifth counsel, for every winner in a rigged venue: the winnings' disposition is the victory's meaning. The counsel is a reputation doctrine with the poor of Qingdao as its standard: Jet beats the sailor at the sailor's game and immediately converts the purse into the crowd's relief, filing the match, in the public ledger, as a defense rather than a transaction. When you win inside a system you despise, the prize is evidence; spend it where it testifies correctly. The crowd that watched you give it away is the same crowd that will burn the right car, later, when it matters.

6. Leave the honorable enemy a road

Hans's exit is the film's sixth counsel, its benediction, for every victor at a factory door: the spared adversary is your code's signature. The counsel is a mercy doctrine with the open road as its standard: anyone can burn the criminal; the discrimination is proven by the road — the deliberate, visible, unrequired exit left for the man who fought you fairly. It costs nothing but certainty, and it purchases the only thing the furnace cannot: the knowledge, carried by the survivor into every retelling, that your justice had a taxonomy. Leave the road. Make sure he sees you leave it.

The moral of the story is…

Pay the veterans in the peace's first budget; absorb the rulebook rather than refuse it; keep two lists, adversaries and criminals — and leave the honorable enemy a road.

Born to Defence teaches that a peace which humiliates its defenders has merely privatized its war; that the answer to rules written against you is absorption, not refusal; and that justice is the discrimination of the account — furnace and open road, correctly addressed.

The film's final question is therefore not who won the rain match — the film files that under "ask the crowd." It is what your peace is paying the people who bought it; which foreign grammar you are currently being beaten by, and whether you are absorbing it; and whose name in your household is officially dead while its owner serves drinks two streets away.
