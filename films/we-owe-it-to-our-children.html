01 / FILM IDENTITY

Overview

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

We Owe It to Our Children (兒女債 — "the debt of sons and daughters"; also known in English as The More the Merrier) is a 1955 Hong Kong Cantonese family melodrama written and directed by Chun Kim for the Union Film Enterprises, released in 1955 (theatrical release January 1, 1955, per the catalogue listings). Its surviving English synopsis — IMDb's, the fullest this archive could verify — sets out the Union studio's characteristic moral machinery with unusual economy: "accountant Ming (played by Cheung Wood Yau) and his wife (Tsi Lo Lin) suffers a miscarriage in a car accident. The couple decides to adopt a baby girl, not knowing that she's the daughter given up by Ming's poor co-worker who struggles to feed a bunch of kids. Bruce Lee plays the slacker son of the landlord (Ko Lo Chuen)." The design is a closed circuit of the era's two scarcities: children, and the means to feed them. In one household, a child is lost to an accident and a salary can raise another; in the other, children arrive faster than rice, and a daughter is given up to the arithmetic; between them stands an adoption that is, unknowingly, an internal transfer within one office — the accountant unknowingly raising his own clerk's daughter, while the clerk watches, presumably, from the next desk. The title states the film's thesis in the language of accountancy its hero practices: children are a debt — 兒女債, the debt of sons and daughters — owed by the generation that makes them to the generation that must be fed, and the melodrama is the audit of who pays. Lee, fourteen, plays Ah Cai (HKMDB's credit; the French-derived catalogue listings render his part as "le fils fainéant," the idle son) — the landlord's slacker son, the comedy-relief pole of the film's class diagram: the child who has everything, idling, against the children who are given away for want of rice. The cast joins the Union repertory at full strength: Cheung Wood-yau (the adult Frank of An Orphan's Tragedy, in this Galaxy, the same season) as Ming, Tsi Law-lin as his wife, Ko Lo-chuen as the landlord, with Yung Siu-yee and the child Yuen Siu-fai — a future Cantonese opera star — among the ensemble. This archive documents the film from its catalogue record and marks the record's limits: no box office, no contemporary reviews and no ending account survive in the English sources this file could reach, and none is reconstructed.

Moral of The Story

6. Pay the debt forward, in the title's currency

The title itself is the film's sixth counsel, its benediction, for every adult who was once a posted entry in somebody's strained ledger — the surrendered, the adopted, the financed, the fed-at-cost: the film's thesis is that you began as a debt on loving books, and the debt's honorable discharge is not repayment to the original creditors (the clerk cannot be repaid; the desk is empty) but assumption of the next generation's entries. The counsel is a succession doctrine with the nursery as its standard: when you audit what you owe your children — the film's exact title — enter it as the clerk and Ming both did, from opposite sides of the desk: as a sum to be paid forward, in rice, in presence, in surrender if the arithmetic demands it, and in the knowledge that the books of the nursery are never closed, only handed on.

The moral of the story is…

Enter both sides of the transfer; cost the surrender as provision; watch your titles' exchange rate — and pay the debt forward.

We Owe It to Our Children teaches that a child is an entry on every ledger the child touches; that provision's hardest form is sometimes relinquishment, witnessed daily; and that a family's books are honest only when the grief and the arithmetic are entered in the same hand.

The film's final question is therefore not whose daughter she was — the film files that under "ask the next desk." It is whether both sides of your family's transfers are entered; whether your surrenders are costed as provision where they belong; and what sum, exactly, your own title page says you owe your children.
