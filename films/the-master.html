01 / FILM IDENTITY

Overview

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

The Master (龍行天下; released in some markets under the branding 黃飛鴻'92之龍行天下, "Wong Fei-hung '92: The Dragon Walks the World") is a Hong Kong martial arts action film written, produced and directed by Tsui Hark, starring Jet Li, Yuen Wah, Crystal Kwok and Jerry Trimble. Shot in 1989 — one source dates the shoot to 1990 — it was shelved and released only on May 28, 1992, after Once Upon a Time in China made Li a major star. In Los Angeles, Chan Hou-tak (Yuen Wah), a Chinese traditional medicine practitioner and martial artist, is attacked by thugs led by Johnny (Jerry Trimble), a local dojo owner and his former student, who destroy Tak's store; Anna (Anne Rickets), a janitor and banned gymnastics student, saves Tak's life, and he recovers in her camper van. Jet (Jet Li) arrives from Hong Kong to resume his studies under Tak, finds the store shuttered, and has his bag stolen by three thieves — whom he chases down on foot, catches, and is promptly begged by to become their master. Johnny's gang is closing kung fu schools across the city to make his name; Jet meets May (Crystal Kwok), the bank worker responsible for the loan on Tak's store; fights beside Johnny during a carpark robbery and is recognized from Tak's photographs, drawing Johnny's vow to kill both master and student. When Jet's friends are beaten, he trains them to defend themselves; reunited with Tak, he finds the old man has renounced both teaching and medicine. After a store confrontation broken up by police and a quarrel, Jet decides to return to Hong Kong — and is attacked on the airport bus by two shotgun-armed men, defeating them and driving the bus himself after the driver is killed. Meanwhile Tak and the three friends assault the rooftop where Johnny holds Anna hostage; Tak disables fifteen or twenty men but tires with age, and Jet, arriving with May after finding Tak's note and will at the store, takes over, fights Johnny, and kicks him off a hanging wire to his death.

Place in the culture

The film lives in the culture as the missing link of the Li–Tsui partnership: made before the Wong Fei-hung films, released because of them, and watching it is watching the partnership's grammar — the master who renounces, the student who persists, the school as a family — rehearsed in modern dress on foreign streets. Its rooftop finale and bus sequence are filed among the era's most inventive location action. For this Galaxy it stands as the archive's document of Li's pre-fame apprenticeship surfacing, belatedly, into his stardom — the 1992 shelf's most instructive double exposure.

03 / NARRATIVE SUMMARY

Story Synopsis

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL FULL STORY SUMMARY

Los Angeles runs its own kung fu war, in English and at street level. Johnny, a dojo owner building a name, leads his gang against the city's Chinese schools — and against his own former teacher, Chan Hou-tak, whose herbal medicine store is destroyed in an attack that nearly kills him. Tak is saved by Anna, a janitor and gymnastics student banned from her school for attacking a classmate and fired by her exasperated coach; the old master recovers, homeless, in her camper van. From Hong Kong comes Jet, Tak's student, arriving by airport bus to resume his training. He finds the store shuttered — and three thieves steal his bag. Jet chases their car on foot through the streets and catches it; the thieves, awestruck, beg him to be their master, and install him in their squat in an abandoned municipal building, which a larger gang attacks and burns. Jet, who speaks no English and owns nothing, is now a master with three disciples and no school.

The war closes in. Johnny's gang is shuttering schools across the city. Jet meets May, the bank worker who manages the loan on Tak's store; neither can find Tak. Caught in a carpark robbery, Jet fights beside Johnny — who recognizes him from Tak's photographs and announces he will kill both student and master. Jet's friends are beaten; Jet trains them. Reunited at last with Tak, Jet finds the old man finished with it all: no more teaching, no more medicine. At Tak's store, the friends use their new skills against Johnny's men while Jet and Johnny fight outside until the police intervene. Jet and Tak quarrel; Jet decides to go home. On the airport bus, two dreadlocked gunmen from the carpark attack with a shotgun; the driver is killed, and Jet defeats both men and takes the wheel himself. May, following in her car, arrives with him at the store — where they find Tak's note, and his will. Tak and the three friends have gone to the rooftop where Johnny's gang holds Anna hostage. The old master disables fifteen or twenty men and begins, with age, to tire. Jet takes over. After an intense fight, he kicks Johnny off a hanging wire, and Johnny falls to his death. Anna, rescued, departs with her gymnastics class for the competition — her ban overtaken, at last, by events.

Themes & Analysis

The lineage survives in conduct, not premises

The film's governing theme is the school's minimum viable form: a renounced master, a shuttered store, a squat classroom — and a student who keeps the succession alive by behavior alone. The theme is worked out from the airport bus to the rooftop until the audience holds its doctrine of the carried tradition: institutions believe they live in their buildings, charters and founders' faith, but the film's experiment removes all three and the tradition persists, because one person continues to act as its heir — teaching thieves, defending friends, relieving the old man mid-fight. Its proposition reaches every member of a diminished institution in the audience. The reach is the story's question to every heir of anything shrinking: if your school lost its building, its founder's belief and its language tomorrow, what exactly would you do on Monday — and is that, precisely, the tradition?

Reputation by subtraction is a debt

The film's second theme is Johnny's campaign: a name assembled from other schools' closures. The theme is worked out across the city's shuttered storefronts until the audience holds its doctrine of the negative résumé: fame built by subtraction carries no equity — every closed school is a creditor, every wrecked master a witness — and the whole structure is callable by a single refusal, one school that will not close, one student who shows up. Its proposition reaches every builder of a name in a competitive field in the audience. The reach is the story's question to every climber by demolition: if your reputation were audited tonight, how much of it is work you did, and how much is wreckage you caused — and who is currently on an airport bus, coming to collect?

The master's unbelief is the student's examination

The film's third theme is Tak's renunciation: the teacher who no longer believes, and the student who must carry the faith for two. The theme is worked out through the quarrel and the will until the audience holds its doctrine of the relay's hardest leg: every tradition, firm and family eventually passes through a season where its founder is exhausted past belief, and its survival depends on whether the next generation can hold the conviction in trust — not performing faith, but discharging the duties — until the old person's strength returns for one last, essential lap. Its proposition reaches every successor serving under a tired founder in the audience. The reach is the story's question to every deputy of an exhausted institution: can you carry the belief, undiluted and unembarrassed, for exactly as long as the renunciation lasts — and will you hand it back, on the rooftop, without keeping score?

Exile recruits whom the homeland would not

The film's fourth theme is the three thieves: the school's new class, delivered by crime. The theme is worked out from the bag-snatch to the rooftop until the audience holds its doctrine of the open gate: the tradition in its homeland recruits by pedigree and patience; in exile it recruits by encounter — and the film files the exiles as the better students, because their enrollment was a conversion and their tuition was survival. Displacement, the film argues, is the great democratizer of every guarded art. Its proposition reaches every practitioner of a gated craft in the audience. 12 / PURPOSES OF THE STORY

Moral of The Story

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

The Master uses a wrecked store, a renounced teacher and three converted thieves to examine succession, negative fame, and the carrying of another's faith: a student who keeps a lineage alive by conduct while its founder disbelieves; a renegade whose name is a debt of wreckage; an old man who prices his last stand in a will. Through the camper van, the squat and the rooftop, the film argues that a tradition survives in behavior, not premises; that reputation built by subtraction is callable by a single refusal; and that the successor's hardest duty is holding the belief in trust through the founder's exhaustion.

Why tell this story?

Because the master-student film needed its diaspora chapter: the succession removed from the temple, the clan and the mother tongue, and tested against banks, buses and bag-snatchers — and because Tsui Hark, shelving the result until its star was famous, accidentally made the film's own release a demonstration of its thesis: value carried patiently, in obscurity, until the market caught up. The archive files the result as the partnership's most prophetic production.

1. Keep behaving as the heir

Jet's conduct is the film's first counsel, for every member of a diminished institution: the tradition is what its heir does on Monday. The counsel is a succession doctrine with the squat classroom as its standard: when the building is shuttered, the founder renounced and the language foreign, the lineage's entire continuity reduces to one person's daily behavior — teach who arrives, defend who is beaten, relieve who tires. File the paperwork later; the conduct is the charter. If you are the last believer in anything, this is the whole job description, and the film notes it is sufficient.

2. Never build a name by subtraction

Johnny's campaign is the film's second counsel, for every competitor: wreckage is a liability dressed as a résumé. The counsel is a reputation doctrine with the shuttered schools as its standard: every closure you cause creates a creditor with a long memory and, eventually, a student on an airplane. Build the name the slow way — the open school, the taught class, the healed patient — because equity in a reputation is the only kind that cannot be called in a single evening, on a single rooftop, by a single kick.

3. Carry the founder's faith in trust

Tak's renunciation is the film's third counsel, for every successor serving under exhaustion: hold the belief without performing it. The counsel is a deputy's doctrine with the quarrel as its standard: do not argue the tired founder back into faith, do not announce his unbelief to the house, and do not let your own conviction become a reproach — discharge the duties, keep the store's lights conceptually on, and be standing, trained and unembittered, at the exact moment the old person writes the will and climbs the stairs. The relay's hardest leg is run at walking pace, in someone else's despair. Run it quietly.

4. Enroll the bag-snatchers

The three thieves are the film's fourth counsel, for every keeper of a gated craft: chase the car. The counsel is an admissions doctrine with the foot chase as its standard: the most motivated students your school will ever have are currently outside its criteria — audacious, unqualified, learning your address by robbing your student — and exile's wisdom is to convert the encounter instead of prosecuting it. Once in your career, when the audacious unqualified present themselves at a dead run, run after them. The rooftop will grade the decision.

5. Write the will before the rooftop

Tak's paperwork is the film's fifth counsel, for every elder contemplating a last stand: price it, in writing, in advance. The counsel is a bequest doctrine with the note at the store as its standard: the old man's stand is noble rather than desperate because it is documented — the succession arranged, the intentions filed where the heir will find them — so that if the roof goes wrong, the lineage loses a master, not its instructions. Whatever your rooftop is, do the paperwork in the quiet before it: the will, the note, the named successor. It is the difference between a bequest and a disappearance.

6. Take the wheel when the driver falls

The airport bus is the film's sixth counsel, its benediction, for every passenger in a failing vehicle: the departure can be cancelled by competence. The counsel is a crisis doctrine with the shotgun aisle as its standard: Jet is leaving — decided, ticketed, aboard — and the attack converts him from passenger to driver in one motion, and the film files the conversion as the succession in miniature: the heir is whoever takes the wheel when the vehicle is driverless, whatever their travel plans were. In your own derailed departures, note the empty seat. It is usually the job.

The moral of the story is…

Keep behaving as the heir; never build a name by subtraction; carry the founder's faith in trust — and take the wheel when the driver falls.

The Master teaches that a tradition survives in behavior, not premises; that reputation built by subtraction is callable by a single refusal; and that the successor's hardest duty is holding the belief in trust through the founder's exhaustion.

The film's final question is therefore not who is the master — the film files that under "ask the rooftop, mid-relay." It is what your own lineage looks like at minimum viable size; how much of your name is wreckage, callable tonight; and whether you are currently a passenger on a driverless bus, telling yourself the departure was the plan.
