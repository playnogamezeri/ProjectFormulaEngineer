01 / FILM IDENTITY

Overview

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Catch Me If You Can is a 2002 American biographical crime comedy-drama film directed by Steven Spielberg, from Jeff Nathanson's screenplay, based on the semi-autobiographical book by Frank W. Abagnale Jr. and Stan Redding. Released December 25, 2002, it grossed $352.1 million worldwide on a $52 million budget and earned Academy Award nominations for Christopher Walken (Best Supporting Actor) and John Williams (Best Original Score). In 1960s New Rochelle, Frank Abagnale Jr. (Leonardo DiCaprio) worships his father Frank Sr. (Christopher Walken) — a charming, doomed striver being dismantled by the IRS — and watches, helpless, as the family's money, house and marriage collapse: his mother Paula (Nathalie Baye) leaves for his father's friend, and a judge asks a sixteen-year-old to choose which parent to live with. Frank runs instead — and discovers that a uniform, a confident walk and a forged check are a complete technology of belonging. Posing as a Pan Am co-pilot ("deadheading" across the country, cashing doctored payroll checks), then as a Harvard-trained doctor supervising a Georgia hospital's night shift (a tenure sustained by television medical dramas and one terrifying night he survives by delegation), then as a Louisiana prosecutor — a bar exam he actually passes, in the film's driest joke, by studying — Frank steals millions and outruns the FBI's bank-fraud unit, embodied in Carl Hanratty (Tom Hanks), a dogged, divorced, humorless agent whose entire emotional life becomes the chase. Their relationship is the film's engine and its heart: hotel-room near-captures, a Christmas Eve phone ritual in which the pursuer realizes the fugitive calls because he has no one else to call, and a final pursuit through Montrichard, France — Paula's hometown, where Frank has industrialized his forgery in a print shop — ending in a French prison, extradition, a mid-flight escape, and a last run to the house where his mother now lives with another family, where Frank finally stops running and surrenders on her lawn. Sentenced to twelve years, he is visited by Hanratty with a proposal that converts the film's chase into its resolution: Frank works off his sentence in the FBI's check-fraud unit, under Hanratty's custody — the forger authenticating checks for the government, bored on Friday afternoons, once slipping away to a airport in pilot's dress before choosing, at the terminal, to come back. The epilogue's text completes the arc the film has actually been telling — not a crime spree but a custody story: the boy who ran from choosing between his parents ends by choosing, every Monday morning, the man who chased him — the pursuer become the father the con could never forge.

Place in the culture

The film is Spielberg's lightest major work and among his most beloved — a jazz-scored, Saul Bass-titled caper whose melancholy underneath (a son impersonating adults because the adult world dissolved his family) has deepened its reputation steadily. It became a Broadway musical in 2011, and its portrait of Abagnale — whose real-life claims the film adapts from his own book, and whose accuracy has been debated ever since — remains the definitive popular account of America's most famous check forger, framed by the film itself with a gameshow's wink ("To Tell the Truth," 1977, opens the picture).

Spielberg's quickest, jazziest production

The rights to Abagnale's book circulated in Hollywood for two decades (with several directors attached) before Spielberg — drawn, he said, to a story about a broken family told at a run — shot it in early 2002 in under two months across some 140 locations, at a pace and lightness unusual for his productions. Janusz Kamiński's cinematography gives the 1960s a storybook saturation that darkens as Frank's world narrows; John Williams's score — a slinky, saxophone-led jazz idiom unlike his symphonic norm — earned an Oscar nomination, as did Walken's turn as Frank Sr. (Walken won the BAFTA and SAG awards for the role). DiCaprio, between Gangs of New York and his Scorsese era, plays Frank's ages 16-to-21 with a performer's glee that keeps the caper buoyant over its undertow. Released Christmas Day 2002, the film was a major critical and commercial success and remains a perennial favorite — its "based on a true story" claim carried, by design, with the same charm and elasticity as its hero's résumé.

03 / NARRATIVE SUMMARY

Story Synopsis

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL FULL STORY SUMMARY

The film frames the chase in 1969 Marseilles — a sick, shackled Frank being extradited by Hanratty — then rewinds to New Rochelle, where Frank Sr.'s decline is staged as a series of closed doors: the bank loan refused, the IRS audit eternal, the Rotary Club award (his son accepts it in his name, in the film's first impersonation: the boy playing his father, proudly, before an audience) already a relic. Paula's affair and the divorce's ultimatum — a form asking the son to choose a parent — is the film's true inciting incident: every uniform Frank subsequently wears is an attempt to re-enter a world of adults who seemed to know who they were. The pilot years are pure caper mechanics (uniforms ordered by telephone, checks washed and reprinted, a "pilot's" confidence that opens every door the 1960s kept unlocked for a man in epaulettes), and the film's comedy is procedural: the era's trust is the con's real accomplice. Hanratty's pursuit is the mirror discipline — a man with no life assembling one out of a case file, learning his quarry through laundry marks, school records and a yearbook, until the chase's central ritual forms: the Christmas Eve calls, in which Frank, alone in a motel room, phones his pursuer to talk — and Hanratty, alone in an office, realizes the boy is not taunting him. He is visiting.

The doctor and lawyer chapters deepen the pattern: Frank nearly builds a real life as Dr. Conners — engaged to Brenda (Amy Adams), a nurse exiled by her Lutheran family for a past abortion, whose father Roger Strong (Martin Sheen) receives Frank with a grace-and-interrogation dinner that is the film's set piece of belonging — and abandons it the night Hanratty closes in, leaving Brenda at an airport rendezvous he knows is watched, a cruelty the film does not forgive him even as it understands it: the con's first law is that no one may be kept. France industrializes the endgame (a print shop in Montrichard, checks by the million), and the capture there — Hanratty talking him out, on Christmas Eve, with the news that his father has died in a fall at a train station, broke and alone — lands as the film's thesis in reverse: the chase ends when the pursuer becomes the bearer of the family's news, the only mourner Frank has. The final run — escape from the extradition flight, the dash to Paula's new house, the surrender on the lawn as police surround a boy looking through a window at the family that replaced his — completes the geometry: he runs, at the last, not away but toward the original scene, and stops there. The FBI coda is the film's verdict on both men: the state converts its catch into an asset, the asset into an employee, and the employee, one restless Friday, walks an airport in his old uniform — and turns back, choosing the desk, the custody, the man waiting with the car. The mice in Frank Sr.'s parable — two mice fell in a bucket of cream; the first gave up and drowned; the second churned so hard he churned the cream into butter and walked out — get their final reading: Frank churned for years. Hanratty is the one who finally showed him the bucket's edge was a door held by somebody who wanted him alive on the other side of it.

The full narrative summary is an editorial paraphrase, not dialogue or a scene-by-scene transcript.

04 / STORY STRUCTURE

Story & Chronology

The film unfolds between 1963 and 1969, framed by the extradition from France. The sequence below follows the film's narrative order.

Act I — The divorce & the uniform

A family dissolves, a judge offers a boy a choice of parents — and a runaway discovers that a pilot's uniform is a complete technology of belonging.

Act II — The doctor, the lawyer & the calls

A hospital, a fiancée, a bar exam honestly passed — and a Christmas Eve ritual in which the fugitive phones his pursuer because there is no one else.

Act III — Montrichard & the desk

A print shop, a father's death delivered as a capture — and a forger, sentenced, who ends by authenticating checks beside the man who caught him.

THE CON ARTIST

10 / THEMATIC ANALYSIS

Themes & Analysis

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

The impostor as refugee — belonging by forgery

The film's governing theme is its reading of the con as a housing crisis: Frank impersonates adults not for wealth but for admission — each uniform a room where the world's rules are clear and the occupant is wanted. The theme is worked out in the plot's refusals (he keeps almost none of the money's pleasures; he keeps the belonging, nightly, and loses it at every dawn) until the audience holds the film's doctrine of imposture: the fraud is a boy trying to live in the adult world that dissolved under him, using the only keys it left in reach. Its proposition reaches every impostor in the audience — the credentialed-by-confidence, the promoted-beyond-their-depth: audit what your performance is sheltering. The reach is the story's question to every wearer of a borrowed uniform: which dissolved room are you re-entering — and who, told the truth, might still hold the door?

The pursuer as parent — custody by chase

The film's second theme is its transformation of the manhunt into a guardianship: Hanratty's pursuit is the one constant adult attention in Frank's life — he learns the boy's schools, habits, griefs — and the capture, when it comes, is administered as care: the father's death told gently, the extradition ridden beside him, the sentence argued down into a mentorship. The theme is worked out in the Christmas calls (the relationship confessing itself annually) until the audience holds the film's doctrine of pursuit: to chase someone for years, attentively, is a form of knowing them that can end in keeping them. Its proposition reaches every adversary in the audience — the regulator, the rival, the estranged parent: your attention is already a relationship; decide what it is for. The reach is the story's question to every Hanratty with a file: when you catch him, what will you be to him then?

The trust economy — an era audited by a teenager

The film's third theme is its period itself: a civilization running on unverified courtesy — the uniform believed, the check honored, the stranger sponsored — whose every ritual Frank performs back to it, slightly forged. The theme is worked out as comedy with an undertow of elegy (the world that made Frank possible is the world his career helps end: verification, databases and suspicion are the spree's true legacy) until the audience holds the film's doctrine of trust: every courtesy is an attack surface, and every attack surface was once a kindness. Its proposition reaches every designer of a modern verification regime in the audience: you are building on the grave of a handshake. The reach is the story's question to every institution pricing its courtesies: which of your rituals could a charming sixteen-year-old perform back to you?

The parable's second reading — churning is not arriving

The film's fourth theme is its father's story, re-read by its ending: the two mice preach endurance, and Frank endures magnificently — but the film's resolution is not the butter; it is the hand on the bucket. Frank is not saved by churning harder but by finally letting himself be caught by the one man whose catching is a keeping. The theme is worked out in the airport coda (the uniform donned once more, the turn back freely chosen) until the audience holds the film's amendment to the striver's gospel: survival is a solo virtue with a ceiling; being kept is a relational one without it. Its proposition reaches every churner in the audience in their eleventh hour in the bucket: the exit may not be upward. The reach is the story's question to every drowning mouse with strong legs: whose hand, exactly, are you refusing?

11 / FULL SPOILER CONCLUSION

Ending Explained

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL THE ENDING

Captured in France after learning of his father's death, Frank is extradited, escapes once to his mother's new home, and surrenders. Sentenced to twelve years, he is recruited by Hanratty to work for the FBI's check-fraud unit under his supervision. After one last flight impulse at an airport, Frank chooses to return — and, per the closing text, goes on to a long career designing secure checks, while he and Hanratty remain friends.

What remains uncertain

How much of Abagnale's account is itself a performance (a debate the film knowingly inherits from its source book, and winks at in its gameshow frame); whether Brenda ever learned the whole truth; and whether Frank Sr.'s parable was wisdom or the disease itself — all stand open at the film's close. Within this film, the archive files the uncertainty as the story's own last word on its theme: the greatest con in the picture may be the memoir, and the film loves its hero too honestly to mind.

Moral of The Story

Catch Me If You Can uses a divorce, a uniform and a manhunt to examine belonging, custody and trust: a boy who forges identities because the adult world dissolved his address; a pursuer whose attention matures into guardianship; and an era whose courtesies are audited, lovingly, by the teenager they admit. Through the checks, the Christmas calls and the final desk, the film argues that imposture is usually a housing problem; that being caught by the right person is a form of being kept; and that churning, however heroic, is not the same as arriving.

Why tell this story?

Because Spielberg — cinema's great chronicler of broken families and the boys who run from them — found in Abagnale's caper his purest fable: a runaway whose crimes are all applications for admission, chased by the one man whose attention turns out to be the admission itself. He told it at a jazz tempo so the melancholy arrives as undertow rather than lecture. The result is his most beloved late caper, the archive files: a manhunt that was a custody hearing all along.
