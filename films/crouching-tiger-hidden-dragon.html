01 / FILM IDENTITY

Overview

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Crouching Tiger, Hidden Dragon is a 2000 wuxia film directed by Ang Lee, written by Wang Hui-ling, James Schamus and Tsai Kuo-jung, from Wang Dulu's novel — the fourth of his Crane-Iron pentalogy. Premiered at Cannes on May 18, 2000, it grossed $213.5 million worldwide on a $17 million budget, became the highest-grossing foreign-language film in American history ($128 million in the U.S. alone), and was nominated for ten Academy Awards — then a record for a non-English-language film — winning four: Foreign Language Film, Cinematography, Art Direction and Original Score. In Qing-dynasty Beijing, the great swordsman Li Mu Bai (Chow Yun-fat) is retiring from the jianghu, the martial underworld, having failed in meditation to find peace — haunted instead, he confesses, by a grief he cannot name: a lifetime beside Yu Shu Lien (Michelle Yeoh), who runs a security company and was once betrothed to his sworn brother, killed years ago; the two have loved each other in silence for decades, bound by a code of honor neither will be the first to break. Mu Bai gives his sword, the Green Destiny — four hundred years old, a legend in steel — to Sir Te (Sihung Lung) as a gift, asking Shu Lien to deliver it; and that night the sword is stolen from Sir Te's house by a masked thief of astonishing skill, who fights Shu Lien across the rooftops of Beijing in the film's first flight — Yuen Woo-ping's choreography lifting the wuxia tradition into pure cinema, warriors running up walls and standing on bamboo-thin branches, gravity treated as a courtesy. The thief is Jen Yu (Zhang Ziyi): the governor's daughter, eighteen, betrothed against her will to a bureaucrat's son, and secretly trained for ten years by Jade Fox (Cheng Pei-pei) — the outlaw who murdered Mu Bai's master and has hidden for a decade as Jen's governess, teaching her from a stolen Wudang manual she could only half-read, so that the pupil has secretly surpassed the teacher. Around the stolen sword the film builds its double architecture of desire: the old generation, Mu Bai and Shu Lien, who have every right to love and will not speak; and the young, Jen and Lo (Chang Chen) — "Dark Cloud," the desert bandit who stole her comb in the Gobi and then her heart, in the film's great central flashback — who speak of nothing else and have no right at all. Jen wants what the romances promised: freedom, the jianghu, a life unwritten by her family; and she pursues it with a genius for swordsmanship and a child's grasp of its costs, refusing every hand offered her — Lo's, Shu Lien's, Mu Bai's offer to teach her — until the film's accounts come due: Jade Fox's revenge, a poisoned dart meant for Jen that Mu Bai takes, and the century's most quietly devastating deathbed scene, in which the swordsman spends his last breath at last on the unsaid thing: "I would rather be a ghost, drifting by your side — than enter heaven without you." Jen, holding an antidote that arrives too late, makes the film's final accounting at Wudang Mountain, in the legend Lo told her: the faithful heart's leap from the bridge — she jumps, and the film, like the legend, declines to say she fell. Lee — who called it his childhood dream of a wuxia film, made with a Sense and Sensibility heart — built the rare action film whose fights are its dialogue: every duel in it is an argument about love, freedom or grief, conducted at the speed of steel.

Place in the culture

The film carried wuxia — and Mandarin-language cinema — into the Western mainstream in a single leap: it remains the benchmark for the genre's artistry, made wire-fu a global grammar (via Yuen Woo-ping, fresh from The Matrix), launched Zhang Ziyi internationally, and proved a subtitled film could be a blockbuster. Its bamboo-forest duel and rooftop chase are now canonical sequences, and its emotional architecture — repression versus appetite, two generations mirrored — is the template for the prestige martial-arts films that followed, from Hero to House of Flying Daggers.

The dream, made for $17 million

Lee shot for eight months across China — Beijing studios, the Gobi Desert, the bamboo forests of Zhejiang — in punishing conditions, for a budget that would not cover a Hollywood tentpole's catering, with a cast for whom Mandarin was, in most cases, a learned language: Chow, a Cantonese speaker, and Yeoh, who had spoken her lines phonetically in earlier films, carried the film's most demanding dialogue with a discipline the production remembered with awe; Yeoh also shot much of the film around a serious knee injury. Zhang Ziyi, nineteen and a dancer by training, was cast for the quality Lee needed most — a face that could hold innocence and cruelty in the same shot. The film's multinational financing (Taiwan, Hong Kong, China, the U.S.) made it a pan-Chinese production of a kind rarely assembled, and its Cannes premiere began one of the great word-of-mouth runs in modern box office: released in America on a handful of screens in December, it expanded for months on audience evangelism alone.

03 / NARRATIVE SUMMARY

Story Synopsis

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL FULL STORY SUMMARY

The theft is the film's detonator, and Lee is careful to make it a liberation before it is a crime: Jen, suffocating inside an arranged marriage and a governor's decorum, steals the Green Destiny the way another heroine might steal a horse — to feel, for one night, what the stories feel like — and her rooftop duel with Shu Lien, the woman she most admires, is the film's thesis in motion: two generations of women warriors, one bound by the code and one allergic to it, fighting to a draw neither can afford to win. The investigation draws the old enemies back onto the board: Jade Fox surfaces after a decade, killing the policeman Tsai who hunted her; Mu Bai, who has waited ten years to avenge his master, finds his revenge complicated by the discovery that the Fox's pupil is the greater talent — and that he wants, against every rule of his school, to teach her. The desert flashback is the film's beating heart, set down in its middle like a confession: Lo's raid on the Yu caravan, the stolen comb, the pursuit across the Gobi that turns, day by day, from combat into courtship — two young people entirely outside the world's permissions, inventing, in caves and under desert stars, the free life Jen will spend the rest of the film trying to re-enter. Its end is the film's economics of freedom in miniature: Lo lets her go home, because the desert cannot keep her, and she returns to Beijing carrying the legend he told her — the boy who leapt from Wudang's bridge and floated, because his heart was faithful — as her private scripture.

The last movement collects every debt. Jen, refusing the marriage to the end, flees to the jianghu she romanticized and finds its true curriculum: a tavern full of fighters she humiliates with the Green Destiny — the film's most exhilarating and most damning sequence, a girl defeating the entire martial world and finding it tastes of nothing; Shu Lien's duel with her, fought with every weapon in an arsenal and a sister's fury, in which the older woman wins the fight and loses the girl; Lo's return, at the wedding itself, begging her to come away — refused, because Jen cannot yet tell freedom from flight. And then the accounts: Jade Fox's ambush, the poisoned darts, Mu Bai's intervention and his wound; the antidote race; the cave, where the film's twenty years of silence are finally spent in a single speech — Mu Bai, dying, using his last breath not on the code, not on revenge, but on Shu Lien's name and the admission that his meditation failed because his heart was here all along. Jen, arriving with the antidote moments too late, inherits the whole wreckage of her refusals; her final scene at Wudang, with Lo, is the film's closing of its double architecture: he wishes, at her request, for them to be back in the desert — and she steps off the bridge into the legend, faithful-hearted, and the film ends, as it must, in the air.

The full narrative summary is an editorial paraphrase, not dialogue or a scene-by-scene transcript.

THE SWORDSMAN

Li Mu Bai

Chow Yun-fat. The jianghu's greatest living swordsman — Wudang's heir, avenger-in-waiting of his murdered master, and a man retiring from the sword with his life's one sentence still unspoken to the woman beside him. Chow plays him with a gravity and gentleness that make his lethality feel like a form of courtesy, and Mu Bai's function is the story's portrait of mastery as a life postponed: he has perfected everything except the permission to want. The archive files him as the decade's most consequential swordsman in film: a last breath, spent, at last, on the right words.

THE PROPRIETOR

Yu Shu Lien

Michelle Yeoh. Head of a security company — a warrior of the first rank who has built a life inside the code: her dead fiancé was Mu Bai's sworn brother, and the code has kept her and Mu Bai in a twenty-year silence of mutual, honorable misery. Yeoh plays her with a contained, luminous dignity that makes every restraint legible as a cost, and Shu Lien's function is the story's portrait of the code's noblest prisoner: she wins every fight in the film except the one against her own permissions. The archive files her as the decade's most consequential warrior in film: an arsenal, a company, and a sentence she never got to hear in time.

THE HIDDEN DRAGON

Jen Yu

Zhang Ziyi. The governor's daughter — betrothed, decorous, and secretly the most gifted young fighter in the film, trained in hiding by Jade Fox and drunk on the romances of the jianghu she has never actually lived in. Zhang plays her with a volatility that swings between innocence and cruelty within single scenes, and Jen's function is the story's portrait of appetite as a form of genius: she wants everything the code forbids, immediately, and the film honors the wanting even as it invoices it. The archive files her as the decade's most consequential daughter in film: a leap, taken, on a faithful heart.

THE BANDIT

Lo (Dark Cloud)

Chang Chen. A desert raider of the Gobi — who steals a comb from a governor's caravan and finds himself, across a pursuit that becomes a courtship, the keeper of the film's only free love and its governing legend. Chang plays him with a rough, tender directness that makes him the film's one unguarded heart, and Lo's function is the story's portrait of freedom as a place rather than a condition: he has it, in the desert, and cannot export it to Beijing. The archive files him as the decade's most consequential bandit in film: a wish, made, on a bridge — take us back to the desert.

THE FOX

Jade Fox

Cheng Pei-pei. A legendary outlaw — murderer of Mu Bai's master, who stole the Wudang manual, could not read its deepest teachings, and hid for ten years as a governess, raising Jen as her instrument of revenge against the school that excluded her. Cheng — herself the queen of 1960s wuxia — plays her with a bitter, gimlet-eyed intelligence, and Jade Fox's function is the story's portrait of exclusion as the jianghu's original sin: Wudang would not teach a woman, so a woman stole the book, and the film is the interest on that refusal. The archive files her as the decade's most consequential outlaw in film: a stolen manual, half-read, and a decade of patience.

THE HOST

Sir Te

Sihung Lung. A senior official and the two warriors' oldest friend — recipient of the Green Destiny, host of its theft, and the film's portrait of the civil world that the jianghu's passions keep invading. Lung, Ang Lee's recurring father-figure, plays him with a warm, worried gravitas, and Sir Te's function is the story's portrait of the establishment as a kind man in an armed world: his house is where the sword was safest and where it was stolen, which is the film's whole sociology in one address. The archive files him as the decade's most consequential host in film: a gift received, guarded, and gone by morning.

THE GOVERNOR

Governor Yu

Li Fazeng. Jen's father — a high Qing official whose household is the film's engine of decorum: the arranged marriage, the governess in the nursery, the daughter filed as an alliance. His function is the story's portrait of the patriarchal order as an administrator rather than a tyrant: he loves his daughter in the only grammar he has, which is arrangement, and the film files the tragedy accordingly — no villain, just a filing system. The archive files him as the decade's most consequential governor in film: a household run like a ministry, with a dragon in the nursery.

THE INSPECTOR

Inspector Tsai

Wang Deming. A police inspector who has hunted Jade Fox for years — arriving in Beijing with his daughter to close the case, and killed, with her, by the Fox's ambush on the riverbank. His function is the story's portrait of the law as the jianghu's outclassed neighbor: diligent, brave and entirely out of his depth among people who can stand on bamboo. The archive files him as the decade's most consequential inspector in film: a warrant, served, on a legend — and answered.

THE AIDE

Bo

Xian Gao. Shu Lien's loyal assistant at the security company — the firm's runner, witness and extra pair of eyes, present at the theft's discovery and the investigation's errands. His function is the story's portrait of the retainer as the jianghu's connective tissue: companies like Shu Lien's run on Bos, and the film honors the type with its usual economy. The archive files him as the decade's most efficient aide in film: a message, delivered, at a run.

THE MASTER

Master Jiang Nan

Present only in memory and motive: Mu Bai's teacher, murdered by Jade Fox — the death that has organized Mu Bai's entire adult life into a waiting room for revenge, and whose school's exclusion of women set the whole tragedy in motion a generation earlier. His function is the story's portrait of the dead master as the jianghu's true legislator: every principal in the film is executing, resisting or avenging his curriculum. The archive files him as the decade's most consequential master in film: a closed door, inherited, by everyone.

THE SWORD

The Green Destiny

A four-hundred-year-old blade, flexible as silk and sharp enough to cut steel — the jianghu's most famous weapon, given away by its master, stolen by a girl, and fought over by everyone in the film. The Sword's function is the story's portrait of the object as the plot's true protagonist: it is a retirement, a temptation, a dowry and a diploma in one length of steel, and each character who holds it is briefly revealed by what they do with it. The archive files the Green Destiny as the decade's most consequential fictional sword in film: a legend that fits in a girl's sash.

THE MOUNTAIN

Wudang

Not a person but the film's presiding institution: the sacred mountain and its school, source of the manual, the code and the master — and of the legend of the leap with which the film ends. Wudang's function is the story's portrait of tradition as both treasury and gatekeeper: everything beautiful in the film descends from it, and so does everything that excluded Jade Fox and silenced Shu Lien. The archive files Wudang as the decade's most consequential fictional school in film: a mountain that teaches flight and forbids it, in the same manual.

Two generations, one code

The film's factional design files every institution by its door: Wudang's locked, the Household's guarded, the jianghu's revolving — and the archive files the design as the story's durable sociology: the old are imprisoned by the code they keep, the young by the freedom they imagine, and the doors are the same doors.

Qing Beijing — the rooftops

A capital of courtyards, ministries and tiled roofs — the roofs being the film's true map: the jianghu travels above the civil world, at night, on the tiles, while the ministries sleep below. Beijing's function is the story's portrait of the ordered city as a surface: everything decorous happens at ground level; everything true happens one storey up. 08 / THE GREEN DESTINY, QINGGONG & THE CODE OF THE XIA

The Green Destiny, Qinggong & the Code of the Xia

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

The Green Destiny — the given-away legend

A 400-year-old sword whose retirement gift starts the plot: Mu Bai gives it away to end his life in the jianghu, and the jianghu, in the person of a governor's daughter, declines to let it rest. The Sword's function is the story's portrait of the masterpiece as a responsibility: it cannot be owned innocently; it can only be wielded, surrendered or stolen. The archive files the Destiny as the decade's most consequential fictional weapon in film: a retirement, stolen, on its first night.

Qinggong — the lightness skill

The art of weightlessness: running up walls, standing on bamboo tips, crossing rooftops like a rumour — the film's signature physics, in which mastery is measured by how little of the world you need to touch. Qinggong's function is the story's portrait of freedom as a technique: Jen's flight is real, trained and earned, which is what makes her tragedy a matter of judgment rather than ability. The archive files qinggong as the decade's most consequential fictional skill in film: flight, with a curriculum and a dress code.

The code of the xia — honor's operating system

The martial world's unwritten law: loyalty to the dead, silence about the heart, revenge as a duty and desire as a dereliction — the system that keeps Mu Bai and Shu Lien apart for twenty years and sends Jade Fox into exile. The code's function is the story's portrait of honor as both spine and cage: every noble act in the film is the code's, and so is every wasted decade. The archive files the code as the decade's most consequential fictional constitution in film: a law whose finest products are its prisoners.

The manual — the stolen curriculum

Wudang's secret book, stolen by Jade Fox, half-legible to her, mastered in secret by her pupil: the film's engine of irony, in which the excluded teacher's ignorance becomes the student's surpassing gift. The manual's function is the story's portrait of knowledge as the true contraband: the school's monopoly, not the sword, is what everyone is really fighting over. The archive files the manual as the decade's most consequential fictional book in film: a syllabus, misread, into a masterpiece.

10 / THEMATIC ANALYSIS

Themes & Analysis

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

The unsaid — repression as a life sentence

The film's governing theme is the twenty-year silence between its two most honorable people: Mu Bai and Shu Lien have done everything together except speak, and the film's ruling is that their restraint — noble, mutual, code-sanctioned — is not a virtue with a cost but a cost with a virtue's costume. The theme is worked out in the deathbed arithmetic (a whole life exchanged for one sentence, delivered at the last possible moment) until the audience holds its doctrine of the unsaid: what honor forbids you to say does not evaporate; it accrues, and it is collected, with interest, at the end. Its proposition reaches every honorable person in the audience maintaining a dignified silence of long standing: your code has a billing department. The reach is the story's question to every Mu Bai in meditation: if your last breath is already budgeted for the sentence, what exactly is the discount for spending it now?

Freedom misread — Jen's curriculum

The film's second theme is the young generation's error, drawn with love: Jen wants freedom and pursues it as the romances describe it — theft, flight, duels, refusal — discovering at each station that the jianghu of the stories is a place where other people pay for your liberty in blood. The theme is worked out in her refusals (Lo, Shu Lien, Mu Bai's teaching — every offered form of the life she wants, declined because it was offered) until the audience holds its doctrine of seized versus given: a freedom that cannot survive being handed to you is not freedom but appetite in costume. Its proposition reaches every talented young refuser in the audience. The reach is the story's question to every hidden dragon: which of your refusals is a principle, and which is the fear that an accepted gift would make you a student again?

Exclusion's interest — Jade Fox and the locked door

The film's third theme is the tragedy's first cause, placed a generation before the plot: Wudang would not teach a woman, so a woman stole the book — and everything in the film, the murdered master, the hidden pupil, the poisoned dart, is compound interest on that one locked door. The theme is worked out in the Fox's dying question (did her pupil ever care for her) until the audience holds its doctrine of exclusion: an institution's refusal to teach does not prevent the learning; it prevents the loyalty, and the learning proceeds anyway, weaponized and unsupervised. Its proposition reaches every gatekeeper in the audience — the school, the guild, the firm — currently congratulating itself on its standards. The reach is the story's question to every Wudang with a manual: who is outside your door tonight, teaching themselves from a stolen copy, and what will their pupil do to your master?

The faithful heart — the legend as a technology

The film's fourth theme is its closing wager: Lo's legend of the leap — the boy who jumped from Wudang and floated because his heart was faithful — is treated by the film not as folklore but as an instrument, the story by which the young navigate: Jen believes it the way sailors believe in stars, and her final act is to steer by it. The theme is worked out until the audience holds the film's doctrine of the useful legend: a story need not be true to be load-bearing; it needs to be believed at the moment of the jump. Its proposition reaches every teller and hearer of formative stories in the audience. The reach is the story's question to every keeper of a family legend: which of your stories is a bridge someone will one day step off — and have you told it faithfully enough to hold them?

11 / FULL SPOILER CONCLUSION

Ending Explained

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL THE ENDING

Jade Fox ambushes the party; Mu Bai kills her but takes a poisoned dart meant for Jen. Jen races for the antidote but returns too late: Mu Bai dies in Shu Lien's arms, finally declaring his love. Later, at Wudang, Lo asks Jen to make a wish; she asks him to wish for them to be back in the desert — then leaps from the mountain bridge, as in his legend of the faithful heart, and the film ends without showing her fall.

What the ending establishes

  • Mu Bai's deathbed files the unsaid as the film's true antagonist: the dart kills him, but the silence is what the deathbed is about.
  • Jade Fox's death files exclusion's account as closed in the red: she dies asking her pupil for one word of love, unanswered in kind.
  • Jen's lateness files appetite's invoice as delivered in full: the antidote in her hand, the master dead — her education completed at the highest tuition in the film.
  • Shu Lien's survival files the code's cost as a life sentence of the other kind: she remains, with the sentence she waited twenty years to hear, as her widowhood's only dowry.
  • The leap files the legend as the film's final jurisdiction: over Jen's fate, the court is a story Lo told in a cave, and it does not publish its verdict.

What remains uncertain

Whether Jen falls or floats; what Shu Lien does with the rest of her life; and whether the Green Destiny's retirement ever takes — all stand open at the film's close, by its design. Within this film, the archive files the uncertainty as the story's own last word on its theme: the film ends in the air because its subject was never the landing — it was the faithful heart at the moment of the jump.

12 / PURPOSES OF THE STORY

Moral of The Story

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Crouching Tiger, Hidden Dragon uses a stolen sword to examine silence, freedom and exclusion: two masters who spent twenty years not saying the one sentence; a girl who seized every freedom except the offered ones; and a school whose locked door armed its own tragedy. Through the rooftops, the desert and the bridge, the film argues that honor's silences are billed at the deathbed; that a freedom which cannot be accepted as a gift is appetite in costume; and that institutions which refuse to teach will be answered by students they cannot govern.

Why tell this story?

Because Ang Lee wanted his childhood's wuxia dream told with a novelist's heart — the fights as feelings, the women at the center, the old genre made to carry the weight of a Sense and Sensibility — and because Wang Dulu's buried pentalogy, with its warrior women and its repressed masters, had been waiting sixty years for exactly that reader. The result carried a whole cinema across the Pacific, the archive files: a sword story that conquered the world by whispering.

1. Spend the sentence now

Mu Bai's deathbed is the film's first counsel, for every honorable person maintaining a long, dignified silence — toward a colleague, a parent, a Shu Lien of one's own: the film's arithmetic is merciless; the sentence costs the same breath whenever it is spent, and its value to the hearer declines to a dowry of grief if it is saved for the cave. The counsel is a speech doctrine with the last breath as its standard: identify the one sentence your code has been forbidding — the love, the apology, the gratitude, the claim — and audit the code's authority to forbid it. Most such codes, examined, turn out to be habits with titles. Say the sentence this week, in peacetime, at full price. It is the cheapest it will ever be.

2. Accept one offered door

Jen's refusals are the film's second counsel, for every talented young person who can only enter a life by stealing it: her tragedy is not rebellion but a definition — freedom as whatever is not given — which guarantees she must refuse the teaching, the love and the door each time they are honestly offered, until the offers stop. The counsel is a freedom doctrine with the bamboo forest as its standard: once a year, accept one thing you want in the form in which it is offered — the mentorship, the introduction, the legitimate route — and notice what the acceptance costs your self-story. If it costs you the story that you seized everything, pay it. That story is the most expensive possession you own, and it bills in other people's blood.

3. Teach the person at your door

Wudang's gate is the film's third counsel, for every institution and master guarding a curriculum — the firm, the guild, the family business, the art: the Fox is the standing refutation of gatekeeping as quality control, since the excluded student learned anyway and returned as the catastrophe. The counsel is an exclusion doctrine with the stolen manual as its standard: audit your door for its Jade Foxes — the categories of person your standards conveniently exclude — and price the exclusion honestly, as a loan taken against your own future, at compound interest, repayable in a currency of unsupervised brilliance. Then teach one of them, properly, inside the walls, where the loyalty can form. It is cheaper. It is also the whole point of having walls.

4. Keep your legends in good repair

Lo's story is the film's fourth counsel, for every parent, elder and keeper of a family's or a team's formative tales: Jen steers her life's final act by a legend told once, in a cave, by a bandit — which means the stories we tell the young in unguarded hours are navigational instruments with decades of range. The counsel is a legend doctrine with the bridge as its standard: know which of your stories are load-bearing — the grandfather's leap, the founder's gamble, the family's faithful heart — and tell them accurately, in full, with their costs attached, because someone who loves you will one day step off something while believing them. A legend is a bridge built of words. Maintain it like one.

5. Let your Shu Lien win one argument

The arsenal duel is the film's fifth counsel, for every elder watching a gifted young person mistake appetite for freedom: Shu Lien wins the fight and loses the girl, because a duel — however masterful — is the wrong instrument for the transfer she is attempting, which is not defeat but recognition. The counsel is a mentorship doctrine with the broken arsenal as its standard: when your Jen is mid-rampage, resist the demonstration of superiority she is begging you for; it will only certify her story that the old are gatekeepers. Offer instead the one thing Mu Bai offered and she refused — teaching — and offer it without the duel first. She may refuse it anyway. But she will refuse an offer, not a defeat, and offers, unlike defeats, can be accepted later.

6. Retire your sword on purpose

Mu Bai's gift is the film's sixth counsel, its benediction, for every master approaching the end of a career in the jianghu — the partner, the founder, the champion: he gives the Green Destiny away deliberately, as an act of self-authorship, and the film's tragedy is not the gift but the errand's timing — he retired the sword a decade after he should have retired the silence. The counsel is a retirement doctrine with the gift as its standard: when you lay down your instrument, lay down the whole apparatus with it — the feuds, the codes, the unspoken files — in a single ceremony, while the people it concerns are alive to receive it. A retirement conducted in installments bills each installment separately. Ask the cave.

The moral of the story is…

Spend the sentence now; accept one offered door; teach the person at your gate — and keep your legends in good repair.

Crouching Tiger, Hidden Dragon teaches that honor's silences are collected at the deathbed with interest; that a freedom which cannot be given, only seized, is appetite in costume; and that a locked door does not prevent the learning — it prevents the loyalty.

The film's final question is therefore not whether you could win the duel — the film files that under "the arsenal is breakable." It is which sentence you are saving; which offered door you are refusing on principle; and whose leap, one day, will be steered by a story you told in a cave.
