01 / FILM IDENTITY

Overview

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

V for Vendetta is a 2005 dystopian political thriller directed by James McTeigue in his directorial debut, from a screenplay by the Wachowskis, adapting the DC/Vertigo graphic novel by Alan Moore (who, disillusioned by earlier adaptations, had his name removed) and David Lloyd. It premiered December 11, 2005 and opened widely March 17, 2006, grossing $134.7 million worldwide on a budget of about $54 million. In a near-future Britain ruled by the fascist Norsefire party — a regime built on a manufactured plague panic, governed by High Chancellor Adam Sutler (John Hurt) through surveillance, curfews, black bags and a state broadcaster — a masked man in a Guy Fawkes mask (Hugo Weaving) opens the film by rescuing a young television assistant, Evey Hammond (Natalie Portman), from the secret police, and then, as a first date, conducting the demolition of the Old Bailey to Tchaikovsky's 1812 Overture, broadcast over the city's emergency system. V is the sole survivor of Larkhill, a detention camp where the regime experimented on the people it had disappeared — homosexuals, Muslims, dissidents, the ill — with a viral weapon; the experiments left him disfigured, preternaturally strong, and devoted, across twenty years, to a double project: the methodical murder of every official who staffed Larkhill (the camp's doctor, Delia Surridge (Sinéad Cusack), its chaplain, its commander — now the regime's propaganda chief, security head and bishop) and the conversion of his revenge into a revolution: a promise, made on state television's hijacked airwaves, that in exactly one year, on the fifth of November, the people will join him outside Parliament to watch it fall. The film is built as three interlocking investigations: Inspector Finch (Stephen Rea), the regime's chief detective, hunting V and uncovering, clue by clue, the regime's founding crime — the St. Mary's virus, released by Norsefire itself on its own people to seize power and a pharmaceutical fortune; Evey, sheltering with V, then with a persecuted television host (Stephen Fry), then in a staged imprisonment — V's cruelest gift, a fake state cell in which, reading the smuggled autobiography of Valerie Page, a Larkhill prisoner who died loving a woman and refusing to surrender the last inch of herself, Evey is tortured by her own mentor until she would rather die than inform, and emerges, shaved and rain-washed, unafraid; and V himself, discovering that his design has one flaw — he has fallen in love with his successor, and an idea, as he tells her, cannot kiss her, and cannot die for her either. The climax fuses the three: Creedy (Tim Pigott-Smith), the security chief, trades V for Sutler and murders his own chancellor; V, mortally wounded, kills Creedy's squad and Creedy, and dies in Evey's arms in a train loaded with explosives beneath Parliament; and Finch, who has found both the truth and Evey at the lever, chooses — the regime's own detective — to let her pull it. The final image is the film's thesis and its bequest: the fireworks over a falling Parliament, watched by a crowd of thousands wearing V's mask, who remove them, unmasked and unafraid, as the dominoes of the old order finish falling in the shape of a V.

Place in the culture

The film's afterlife exceeds its box office: the Guy Fawkes mask, designed by Lloyd for the comic, became — through the film — the global uniform of protest movements from Anonymous onward, one of the most recognized political images of the century, and the film itself is now a fixture of every fifth of November. Portman's performance (including the real on-camera head-shaving, done in one take) anchored her transition to adult leading roles, and the film's Valerie letter is widely cited as one of the era's most affecting sequences in a blockbuster. Its argument — that a frightened public can be governed through a manufactured plague and a television — has only gained citations since.

A debut, a mask, and a delayed fifth of November

The Wachowskis, admirers of the comic since before The Matrix, wrote the adaptation in the 1990s and produced it after that trilogy, handing the direction to their first assistant director McTeigue. The production shot in Berlin and London (the finale's march required closing Whitehall and Parliament Square — a permission the production obtained for midnight hours, with hundreds of extras in masks) and built V's Shadow Gallery as the film's warm interior counter-world. Weaving replaced James Purefoy, who departed weeks into filming; Weaving performed V entirely by voice and physicality, his face never seen — a casting the production converted into doctrine (V is a voice, a mask and a set of manners, which is the film's point about him). Portman shaved her head on camera in a single take for the imprisonment sequence. The film was scheduled for November 4, 2005 — the eve of the Gunpowder Plot's 400th anniversary — but was delayed (officially for post-production; the July 2005 London bombings made a Parliament-demolition climax a delicate release), premiering instead at Austin's Butt-Numb-A-Thon on December 11, 2005, screening at Berlin in February 2006, and opening March 17, 2006 to strong reviews and $134.7 million worldwide. Cinematographer Adrian Biddle died days before the premiere; the film is dedicated to him.

03 / NARRATIVE SUMMARY

Story Synopsis

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL FULL STORY SUMMARY

The film opens with its date and its doctrine: Guy Fawkes, 1605, remembered in voice-over — a man, and the idea a man can fail to kill — and then the future London of curfews and Fingermen, where Evey Hammond, out after hours to meet a predatory television executive, is rescued from the secret police by a masked swordsman of impossible courtesy, who introduces himself in a cascade of V-alliteration and invites her to a concert: the Old Bailey, demolished in time to the 1812 Overture, with the city's emergency broadcast as his orchestra. The regime's response assembles the film's machinery: Sutler's fury by video wall, Creedy's security state, Prothero's propaganda voice (the "Voice of London," raging nightly on the state network), and Finch's investigation — the one honest instrument in the government, a tired detective whose method is to follow facts into rooms his masters would prefer unopened. Evey, sheltering in V's Shadow Gallery — a museum of banned books, films and music beneath the city, where breakfast is cooked by a man in a mask and an apron — learns the vendetta's shape in stages: the murders begin (Prothero, Larkhill's commander, found dead with a black rose; the Bishop Lilliman, Larkhill's chaplain, killed at his altar of appetites with Evey, planted as bait, escaping through the confessional's chaos), and Finch's parallel inquiry assembles Larkhill from the victims' common file: a detention camp, a Dr. Surridge, a virus, a survivor who walked out of the fire the night the camp burned.

The middle act is the film's moral engine, and it is Valerie's. Evey, having fled V and sheltered with her friend Gordon Deitrich — a closeted talk-show host whose satirical abduction-sketch of Sutler brings the Fingermen through his door in the night — is seized, bagged, and imprisoned: shaved, starved, interrogated, offered release at the price of V's location, and sustained in her cell by a single document passed through a hole in the wall — the autobiography of Valerie Page, written on toilet paper in the next cell at Larkhill, years before: an actress who loved a woman, was disappeared for it, and died refusing to surrender the last inch of herself, which is the only thing, she writes, that cannot be taken. Evey, offered the door at last, chooses the inch — and the cell opens: the prison was V's construction, the torturer his performance, the whole atrocity a designed ordeal to burn the fear out of her, because (his doctrine) a person integrated and unafraid is the only weapon the regime cannot confiscate. The film does not acquit him of it — Evey's fury is given its full weight, and his apology is the confession of a man who knows his methods are his wound's — and the rain scene that follows, Evey on the roof with her arms raised, reborn in the storm as V was in Larkhill's fire, is the film's hinge: the successor is made. Finch, meanwhile, completes his own education at Larkhill's ruins and in Surridge's diary — the regime's founding crime assembled in a detective's notebook: the St. Mary's virus was Norsefire's own, tested at Larkhill, released on a school, a station and a water plant, and monetized as a cure by the party's pharmaceutical patrons, whose stock made the leadership rich in the panic that made them rulers. The fifth of November arrives with the state at maximum alert and its two heads devouring each other: Creedy, trading Sutler's life for V's surrender, murders the Chancellor in his bunker; V, keeping the appointment in the tunnel with a chest already full of bullets he does not mention, kills Creedy and his squad in the film's balletic last fight, and dies in Evey's arms on the platform — refusing, in his last doctrine, to let the train be his monument: the choice of the lever, he tells her, is hers, because the revolution must belong to the living. Finch, arriving with the truth and a gun, holsters both and watches her pull it — and the crowd that pours into Whitehall, thousands deep in V's masks, stands unmasked at last as the fireworks write the date over a falling Parliament: the idea, having survived its man, takes its bow in the faces of the people who inherited it.

The full narrative summary is an editorial paraphrase, not dialogue or a scene-by-scene transcript.

04 / STORY STRUCTURE

Story & Chronology

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

The film unfolds across exactly one year, fifth of November to fifth of November. The sequence below follows the film's narrative order.

Act I — The concert

A rescue, a demolition set to Tchaikovsky, and a promise broadcast to a frightened city: one year from tonight.

Act II — The vendetta & the cell

Larkhill's staff murdered one by one, a detective assembling the regime's founding crime — and a young woman, in a staged cell, choosing the last inch of herself.

Act III — The fifth of November

A chancellor murdered by his own security chief, a dying man keeping an appointment — and a lever, pulled by the successor, under a sky of fireworks.

05 / CHARACTER DATABASE

Character Database

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Search the principal characters documented in this film. Roles and motivations below summarize their function in the 2005 movie.

THE MASK

V

Hugo Weaving. The sole survivor of Larkhill — a prisoner designated by a Roman numeral, experimented on, burned out of the camp's fire and remade by twenty years of patience into a one-man revolution: swordsman, chemist, conductor, jailer, executioner. Weaving, never unmasked, builds him entirely from voice and gesture — a theatrical courtesy over an absolute will — and V's function is the story's portrait of the idea wearing a man: he insists, correctly, that he is not a person but a function (the mask, the date, the vendetta), and the film's tragedy is the man leaking through the function — in the apron, in the dominoes, in the love he cannot afford. The archive files him as the decade's definitive masked figure in film: a revolutionary whose most radical act is arranging to be succeeded.

THE SUCCESSOR

Evey Hammond

Natalie Portman. A television assistant from a dissident family — her parents taken by the regime, her brother dead at St. Mary's school — who has survived by keeping her head down, and whose education out of fear is the film's plot. Portman plays her transformation in the body (the hair, the posture, the voice that stops apologizing) and Evey's function is the story's portrait of the citizen as the revolution's true unit: V can demolish buildings and murder officials, but only Evey can do the thing the film is actually about — stand in a cell, offered the door, and refuse to inform. The archive files her as the decade's most complete heroine in a political film: a successor manufactured by her mentor's worst method and vindicated by her own choice at the lever.

THE DETECTIVE

Inspector Finch

Stephen Rea. Norsefire's chief inspector — a tired, decent policeman serving an indecent state, whose investigation of V becomes, file by file, an investigation of his own government: Larkhill, the virus, the fortunes made. Rea plays him with a weary integrity that never announces itself (Finch does not defect; he accumulates), and his function is the story's portrait of the honest instrument inside the machine: the regime is not undone by its enemy's bombs but by its own detective's notebook, and Finch's final holstering — the state's own hand, refusing to fire — is the film's quietest and largest political act. The archive files him as the decade's finest screen detective in a political film: a man whose revolution is conducted entirely in evidence.

THE PARTNER

Dominic Stone

Rupert Graves. Finch's young sergeant — the investigation's legs and its audience surrogate, asking the questions the audience asks and filing the answers with a junior officer's dawning comprehension. Graves plays him with an earnest, unshowy competence, and Dominic's function is the story's portrait of the next generation inside the machine: he is what the regime's institutions produce when they accidentally employ the honest — a detective being educated, at working pace, by his own case. The archive files him as the decade's most essential minor detective in film: a sergeant whose case file is a civics course.

THE HIGH CHANCELLOR

Adam Sutler

John Hurt. Norsefire's leader — a face on a video wall, a voice in a bunker, a ruler who governs a nation he never physically enters, through fear administered as public health. Hurt — who played the victim of a totalitarian state in 1984 — plays the tyrant as a study in mediated power: Sutler is almost never in a room with anyone, and his function is the story's portrait of the modern dictator as a broadcast: a man whose body is a screen and whose authority evaporates the moment his own security chief decides the broadcast is over. The archive files him as the decade's most complete screen tyrant in film: a chancellor murdered, appropriately, off-camera, by his own apparatus.

THE SECURITY CHIEF

Peter Creedy

Tim Pigott-Smith. Head of the secret police — the regime's black-bag hand, a cold, ambitious functionary whose loyalty is to the apparatus rather than the man on the wall, and who therefore commits the film's most regime-typical act: he murders his own chancellor as a business transaction with the terrorist. Pigott-Smith plays him with a silken, bureaucratic menace, and Creedy's function is the story's portrait of the security state eating its head: Norsefire's instruments were built to be aimed, and the film files the bunker scene — the apparatus decapitating the ideology to save itself — as the tyranny's true death, before any fireworks. The archive files him as the decade's most consequential security chief in film: a policeman whose coup is a contract killing, invoiced to the enemy.

THE VOICE

Lewis Prothero

Roger Allam. The "Voice of London" — Norsefire's broadcast propagandist, a ranting television demagogue who was, at Larkhill, the camp's commander, and who keeps his old uniform's authority in a bathroom shrine of dolls and medals. Allam plays him with a bombast that curdles, in his death scene's shower-room terror, into the film's most pathetic portrait of power remembered, and Prothero's function is the story's portrait of propaganda as a war crime with a time slot: his voice built the public's fear for a decade, and V's vendetta files him not as a journalist but as a staff officer. The archive files him as the decade's definitive propagandist in film: a voice, silenced in the room where he kept his past.

THE HOST

Gordon Deitrich

Stephen Fry. A beloved television talk-show host — closeted, witty, keeper of a cellar of banned art and a Quran he treasures as poetry — whose one act of broadcast satire (a Benny Hill-style sketch abducting the Chancellor) brings the Fingermen through his door in the night. Fry plays him with a warmth that makes his disappearance the film's most domestic horror, and Deitrich's function is the story's portrait of the entertainer in the police state: his show is the regime's licensed laughter, and the film files the exact width of the license — one sketch — as the measure of the freedom. The archive files him as the decade's most consequential minor host in film: a comedian whose cellar is a museum and whose punchline is a raid.

THE DOCTOR

Delia Surridge

Sinéad Cusack. Larkhill's physician — the experimenter who kept the camp's diary, profited from the virus's patent fortune, and has lived since in a quiet, sleepless guilt that makes her the vendetta's only penitent target: she asks V, at the end, whether he will kill her, hears that he already has (the poison, painless, administered before the conversation), and dies apologizing — the only one of his victims he permits to speak. Cusack plays her in a few scenes of exhausted honesty, and Surridge's function is the story's portrait of the collaborator as the tragedy's conscience: her diary is Finch's Rosetta stone and the film's evidence that the regime's servants know exactly what they did. The archive files her as the decade's most complete minor collaborator in film: a doctor whose deathbed is an interrogation she welcomes.

THE LETTER

Valerie Page

Natasha Wightman. A Larkhill prisoner, seen in flashback — an actress disappeared for loving a woman, who wrote her autobiography on toilet paper in the cell next to V's and died with her integrity, in her own phrase, intact to the last inch. Valerie never meets a living character; her letter is read, in full, twice, and functions as the film's scripture — the document that makes V a revolutionary rather than merely an avenger, and that saves Evey in her cell. Her function is the story's portrait of the witness as the revolution's true author: the masked man is her publisher; the fireworks are her royalties. The archive files her as the decade's most consequential absent character in film: a prisoner whose toilet-paper memoir outlives the state that killed her.

THE BROADCASTER

Roger Dascomb

Ben Miles. Head of the state television network — the regime's programming chief, who manages the news of demolitions and terrorists with a producer's instincts, and whose hijacked broadcast (V's address to the nation, aired on his own emergency channel) is his career's defining failure. Miles plays him with a sleek, managerial anxiety, and Dascomb's function is the story's portrait of the media apparatus as the regime's nervous system: Norsefire rules through his schedules, and the film files his control room — watching its own signal carry the revolution's invitation — as the state's most honest portrait of itself. The archive files him as the decade's most complete minor broadcaster in film: a network chief, out-programmed by his own transmitter.

THE BISHOP

Bishop Lilliman

John Standing. Larkhill's chaplain, now a bishop of the regime's church — a glutton and predator in vestments, whose appetites (young girls, delivered by arrangement) make him the vendetta's most squalid target and Evey's most dangerous assignment. Standing plays him with a jovial corruption that curdles instantly into terror, and Lilliman's function is the story's portrait of the church as the regime's franchise: his cassock is a uniform of the same state, his prayers a department of it, and the film files his altar as Larkhill's branch office. The archive files him as the decade's most consequential minor cleric in film: a chaplain whose confessional is an ambush, in both directions.

06 / NORSEFIRE, THE FINGERMEN & THE SHADOW GALLERY

Norsefire, the Fingermen & the Shadow Gallery

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
THE REGIME

Norsefire

The ruling party: a fascist government organized as a body — the Eye (surveillance), the Ear (listening), the Nose (police), the Mouth (propaganda), the Finger (secret police) — under a Head who appears only on screens. Norsefire's function is the story's portrait of the state as an anatomy of senses: it watches, hears, smells and speaks, and its one unmonitored organ is its conscience, which it outsourced to a detective. Norsefire ends the film decapitated by its own Finger and dissolved in fireworks, the archive files it as the decade's most completely diagrammed fictional regime: a body, beheaded by its own hand, in a bunker.

THE POLICE

The Fingermen

The secret police: plainclothes enforcers who black-bag citizens from the street — the regime's fingertips, and the film's first image of its justice (Evey's rescue is from their attempted rape under color of curfew law). The Fingermen's function is the story's portrait of terror as a street-level service: the state does not need its citizens to believe; it needs them to hurry home. The Fingermen end the film as Creedy's private army, spent in a tunnel, the archive files them as the decade's most consequential fictional police: a hand, amputated by its own chief's ambition.

THE UNDERGROUND

The Shadow Gallery

V's home: a disused station gallery beneath London, filled with everything Norsefire banned — books, films, music, paintings, a jukebox, a suit of armor, a domino room — a one-man civilization in exile. The Gallery's function is the story's portrait of culture as the revolution's true headquarters: V's bombs are logistics; his library is the cause — the film files the banned archive, lovingly kept by a murderer, as the country's memory, held in trust for its citizens. The Gallery ends the film as a tomb and a bequest, the archive files it as the decade's most complete fictional refuge: a museum that raised a successor.

THE PUBLIC

The People of London

The film's fourth faction and its final arbiter: a frightened, curfewed population — addressed by V on their own emergency channel, offered masks by the thousand in the post, and summoned, a year in advance, to a demolition. The People's function is the story's portrait of the public as a sleeping organ of the state: Norsefire's anatomy has no organ for them, which is the regime's diagramming error — the crowd that assembles in Whitehall is the body discovering it has a will. The People end the film unmasking themselves in the fireworks' light, the archive files them as the decade's most consequential fictional crowd: an audience, converted, at last, into a citizenry.

The state is a body; the crowd is its missing organ

The film's factional design is an anatomy lesson: Norsefire organizes itself as senses and a head, and is undone by the two things its diagram omits — a conscience (Finch's) and a public (the crowd's). The archive files the design as the story's durable political science: regimes fall to the organs they forgot to include in themselves.

07 / WORLD & SETTING

World & Setting

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

London under Norsefire — the curated city

The film's capital: a London of curfews, concrete and broadcasts — the landmarks intact (the Old Bailey, Parliament, the Underground's disused stations) and the life administrated out of them, down to the butter, the eggs and the jokes permitted on television. London's function is the story's portrait of the familiar as the dystopia's method: nothing in the city is futuristic except its permissions — the horror is that it is recognizably home, with a curfew. The archive files this London as the decade's most consequential fictional capital: a home, under new management, with the same postcode.

Larkhill — the founding ruin

The film's buried location: the detention camp where the regime rehearsed its plague on the disappeared — burned, erased from the maps, and preserved in a doctor's diary and a detective's pilgrimage. Larkhill's function is the story's portrait of the atrocity as a foundation: Norsefire's Britain is quite literally built on the camp (its power, its fortunes, its virus), and the film stages Finch's visit to the ruin — standing in the cell corridor, feeling the place's weather enter him — as the state's own detective reading his government's birth certificate. The archive files Larkhill as the decade's most eloquent fictional ruin: a camp whose ashes are a country's constitution.

08 / THE VENDETTA, THE FIFTH OF NOVEMBER & THE IDEA

The Vendetta, the Fifth of November & the Idea

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

The vendetta — revenge as a schedule

V's private project: the Larkhill staff list, executed one by one across twenty years of preparation and one year of performance — each killing a signature (a black rose, a domino pattern, a date) rather than a mere death. The vendetta's function is the story's portrait of revenge as an art form with a flaw: it is finite — the list ends — and V's whole arc is the discovery that his schedule needs a successor project larger than itself, or it dies with its last name. The archive files the vendetta as the decade's most completely scheduled fictional revenge: a list, completed, and converted at the last moment into a bequest.

The fifth of November — the appointment

The revolution's calendar: a date announced a year in advance, on the state's own channel, in the teeth of the state's total surveillance — a promise whose keeping is the film's suspense engine. The appointment's function is the story's portrait of the deadline as a political instrument: by naming the day, V converts a frightened population's private discontents into a synchronized public — an audience with a date is a crowd in rehearsal. The archive files the date as the decade's most consequential fictional appointment: a demolition, diarized by a nation.

The mask — the reproducible face

Guy Fawkes's grin, mass-mailed to every household: a face anyone can wear, behind which anyone can stand — the film's central technology, cheaper than any bomb. The mask's function is the story's portrait of anonymity as the idea's distribution system: V cannot be killed out of a symbol the post has delivered to a million doors, and the final crowd — masked, then unmasking by choice — is the technology completing itself. The archive files the mask as the decade's most consequential fictional object: a face that made a crowd unidentifiable, and therefore, for one night, unafraid.

The idea — the immortal asset

The film's thesis as a system: men can be captured, beaten and killed; ideas cannot — they are, in V's formulation, bulletproof — and therefore the revolution's capital must be transferred, before the man's death, from his body to his successor and his crowd. The idea's function is the story's portrait of succession as the revolutionary's final discipline: V's dominoes fall in the shape of his initial, but the lever is pulled by another hand — the film files that handover, not the explosion, as the revolution. The archive files the idea as the decade's most completely transferred fictional asset: a vendetta, converted, at par, into a public inheritance.

The lever is the verdict

The archive files the lever as the decade's most consequential prop in film: a revolution, transferred, at the moment of pulling, from a man to a public.

10 / THEMATIC ANALYSIS

Themes & Analysis

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Ideas are bulletproof — the succession problem

The film's governing theme is its hero's mortality management: that a revolution embodied in one man is a hostage to his body, and V's whole design — the mask anyone can wear, the date anyone can keep, the successor anyone can become — is an engineering project to transfer the idea out of the man before the man's account closes. The theme is worked out in the film's doublings (V dies; the crowd unmasks; the dominoes fall in his initial and the fireworks write nothing) until the audience holds the film's doctrine entire: the test of an idea is not whether its author survives but whether its inheritance is complete — a lever, a letter and a million masks, distributed in advance. Its proposition reaches every founder in the audience whose cause is currently their own pulse: your succession plan is your revolution's real constitution. The reach is the story's question to every V with a list and a date: who pulls your lever — and have you given them the letter, or only the fireworks?

The last inch — integrity as the final property

The film's second theme is Valerie's letter: that a person stripped of everything — freedom, name, hair, future — retains one unalienable asset, the last inch of integrity, whose surrender is the only transaction the regime truly needs and whose refusal is therefore the revolution's smallest and largest unit. The theme is worked out in the letter's double reading (V's conversion, off-screen, twenty years before; Evey's, in the cell, in the film's center) until the audience holds the film's anthropology: governments fall not when their opponents grow strong but when their subjects, one cell at a time, stop selling the inch. Its proposition reaches every pressured person in the audience with a signature, a statement or a silence being purchased: price the inch correctly — it is the whole estate. The reach is the story's question to every Evey in an interrogation with a door visible: what, exactly, is your last inch — and have you read the letter of the woman who kept hers?

Fear is the regime's only product — the plague as a business model

The film's third theme is its founding crime: that Norsefire did not merely exploit a plague but manufactured one — tested it on the disappeared, released it on a school, and sold the cure — converting its citizens' terror into power and its patrons' shares into fortunes in a single transaction. The theme is worked out in Finch's investigation, which assembles the crime as an audit (the camp, the diary, the stock) rather than a revelation, until the audience holds the film's political economy: the regime's ideology is a prospectus; its god is a share price; and its fear is not a by-product but the product. Its proposition reaches every citizen in the audience of a frightened era: when a government sells you the cure, ask who held the patent on the disease. The reach is the story's question to every Finch with a notebook: whose fortune, precisely, is your emergency indexed to?

The mentor's atrocity — Evey's cell

The film's fourth theme is its most contested: that V's staged imprisonment of Evey — a fake state torture, conducted by her protector, to burn out her fear — replicates the regime's own methods in the revolution's service, and the film refuses to launder it (her fury is honored; his defense is a confession, not an acquittal; the rain that follows is her triumph, not his vindication). The theme is worked out in the film's insistence that the audience hold both facts at once — it worked, and it was a violation — until the audience holds the film's hardest doctrine: a cause that adopts its enemy's instruments will produce its enemy's graduates, and the only thing that saves V's project from his methods is that his successor, at the lever, is free to refuse him. Its proposition reaches every mentor, movement and parent in the audience tempted by a productive cruelty: the result does not retroactively sign the consent form. The reach is the story's question to every V with a frightened protégé and a design: what are you building in them that they would not thank you for — and is the thanks, or the building, the point?

11 / FULL SPOILER CONCLUSION

Ending Explained

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL THE ENDING

Creedy murders Sutler in a trade, then V — already mortally wounded — kills Creedy and his men and dies in Evey's arms. Finch, who has uncovered the regime's founding crime, lets Evey pull the lever: the explosives train destroys Parliament before a crowd of masked Londoners, who unmask as the fireworks rise.

What the ending establishes

  • Creedy's murder of Sutler files the regime as self-consumed before it is overthrown: the apparatus kills the ideology to save itself, and is killed in its turn within the hour.
  • V's death in the tunnel files the succession as the design's completion: the man dies on schedule, in private, having transferred everything — the lever, the letter, the masks — that the idea needs to survive him.
  • Finch's holstered gun files the state's conscience as the revolution's final instrument: the regime's own detective, holding the evidence, chooses the evidence.
  • The unmasking crowd files the idea's inheritance as received: the masks come off because they are no longer needed — anonymity was the scaffolding; the faces are the building.
  • The falling dominoes file the vendetta's conversion as complete: the private pattern, executed in a gallery for one dying man, resolves into the public's initial — revenge, fully amortized, into a date a nation now keeps.

What remains uncertain

What the crowd builds on the cleared ground, whether the successor's freedom survives the morning after the fireworks, and whether an idea that required a V can govern without one — all stand open at the film's close (no sequel followed; the question is the point). Within this film, the archive files the uncertainty as the story's own last word on its theme: the building is down, the masks are off, and the film ends exactly where citizenship begins — which is to say, without instructions.

12 / PURPOSES OF THE STORY

Moral of The Story

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

V for Vendetta uses a vendetta, a staged cell and a million mailed masks to examine fear, succession and the last inch of the self: a survivor who converts revenge into an inheritance; a young woman who refuses, at the price of her life, to sell her integrity; and a detective whose notebook does what bombs cannot. Through Larkhill, the letter and the lever, the film argues that a frightened public is a business model before it is a politics; that an idea survives only by being transferred, in full, to successors who are free to refuse it; and that the smallest unit of resistance is a single person declining, in a small room, to surrender the one inch that is theirs.

Why tell this story?

Because McTeigue and the Wachowskis understood that Moore and Lloyd's fable had become, in the age of the manufactured emergency, a documentary premise — and that its hardest material (the hero's own torture of his successor) was the part that could not be cut without lying about how fear is actually unlearned. They told it as a thriller because the argument needed a pulse, and ended it with fireworks because the argument's conclusion is a crowd. The result is the decade's defining political film, the archive files: a blockbuster whose most quoted line is a doctrine of inheritance.

1. Transfer the idea before the man expires

V's design is the film's first counsel, for every founder, artist and movement-builder whose cause currently has their own pulse as its life-support: build the succession as deliberately as the launch (the masks distributed, the date public, the successor educated, the lever unguarded) because the idea's immortality is not a property of its truth but of its transfer paperwork. The counsel is an inheritance doctrine with the tunnel as its solicitor's office: V dies owning nothing but a train, and it is enough, because everything else was already given away. If your revolution cannot survive your diary's next empty page, it is a vendetta with better marketing. Convert it, at par, while you can still sign.

2. Keep the last inch

Valerie's letter is the film's second counsel, for every person in a small room being offered a door at the price of a signature, a name or a silence: the inch they are asking for is not a formality — it is the entire estate, and its market price (your comfort, your release, your career) is set precisely because it is the one thing the buyer cannot manufacture. The counsel is an integrity doctrine with the cell as its examination: you do not need a mask, a train or a twenty-year plan to resist; you need a correct valuation of the inch. The woman who wrote the letter had nothing else left, and the film's whole revolution is her refusal's compound interest. Read her terms before you sign yours.

3. Audit the emergency

Finch's notebook is the film's third counsel, for every citizen of a frightened era and every professional inside its machinery — the detective, the journalist, the civil servant: follow the emergency's money (the camp, the patent, the share price) with a notebook rather than an opinion, because manufactured fear always has a ledger, and the ledger is always legible to whoever is willing to read it in order. The counsel is an investigation doctrine with the diary as its Rosetta stone: Finch does not become a revolutionary; he becomes a reader — and a state whose own detective has read its founding documents is a state in its last chapter. Keep the notebook. Read it in the ruins. Let the facts hold the door.

4. Refuse the productive cruelty

Evey's cell is the film's fourth counsel, for every mentor, movement and institution tempted to burn the fear out of its people by force — the hazing that builds character, the ordeal that manufactures loyalty, the training that breaks to rebuild: the film's verdict is double and must be kept double — the method can work, and it is a violation, and the second fact is not canceled by the first. The counsel is an ethics doctrine with the rain as its limit: if your protégé's growth requires their non-consent, what you are building is not their freedom but your monument, staffed by a graduate. Find the slower road. The inch you would take from them is the inch the whole cause exists to defend.

5. Make the date and keep it

The fifth of November is the film's fifth counsel, for every dispersed company of the discontented — the team, the movement, the family with a grievance and no calendar: name a day, a year out, in public, and let the appointment do the organizing that meetings cannot — a crowd with a date is a public in rehearsal, and a regime (or a board, or a habit) that knows the day is coming must govern, all year, in its shadow. The counsel is a scheduling doctrine with the hijacked broadcast as its invitation: V's genius was not the explosives but the diary entry. Announce your fifth of November. Then spend the year becoming the people who would keep it.

6. Unmask when the fireworks start

The crowd's last gesture is the film's sixth counsel, its benediction, for every heir of a successful resistance — the movement that won, the whistleblower vindicated, the family that survived its Sutler: the mask was scaffolding, and the maturity of the cause is measured by the willingness to stand, at the moment of victory, in your own face. The counsel is a graduation doctrine with Whitehall as its ceremony: anonymity protects the becoming; it must not replace the being — an idea that still needs its author's mask, after the building falls, has inherited the fireworks and missed the lever. Take it off. Let the record show who stood there.

The moral of the story is…

Transfer the idea while you can still sign; keep the last inch at any price they name; audit the emergency's money — and let your successor pull the lever, free to refuse you.

V for Vendetta teaches that fear is a business model with a police force; that integrity's last inch is the whole estate of the self; and that a revolution is complete not when the building falls but when its authorship has been given away.

The film's final question is therefore not whether you would wear the mask — the film files that under "postage." It is what your last inch is and who is currently bidding for it; whose letter you are carrying unread in your own cell wall; and whether, given the lever and the detective's holstered gun, you would know the date by heart.

Editorial note: This is an interpretive reading of the film's themes, not a claim about every private intention of its creators or a prediction of future franchise events.
