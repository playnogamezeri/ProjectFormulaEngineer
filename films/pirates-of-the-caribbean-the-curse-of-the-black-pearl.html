01 / FILM IDENTITY

Overview

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Pirates of the Caribbean: The Curse of the Black Pearl is a 2003 American fantasy swashbuckler film directed by Gore Verbinski, produced by Jerry Bruckheimer for Walt Disney Pictures and based on the Disney theme-park attraction. Released July 9, 2003, it grossed $654.3 million worldwide on a $140 million budget — the fourth-highest-grossing film of 2003 — revived the pirate genre from a generation's dormancy, and earned five Academy Award nominations, including Best Actor for Johnny Depp. Eight years after a young Elizabeth Swann (Keira Knightley) plucks a gold medallion from a shipwrecked boy, the medallion's call draws the Black Pearl — a cursed ship with black sails — to Port Royal, Jamaica. Its crew, led by Captain Hector Barbossa (Geoffrey Rush), are the thieves of Hernán Cortés's Aztec gold: 882 cursed pieces whose theft left them undead — neither living nor dead, unable to taste, drink or feel, revealed by moonlight as skeletons — and who have spent a decade returning every piece to its stone chest on the Isla de Muerta, awaiting the final coin and the blood of its owner's line to lift the curse. The coin is Elizabeth's; the blood, they believe, is hers — she has given the name Turner, borrowed from the boy she rescued, Will (Orlando Bloom), now a blacksmith's apprentice hopelessly in love with her, and in fact the son of Bootstrap Bill Turner, the one crewman who repented, mailed a coin to his son, and was strapped to a cannon and sunk for it. Into this machinery wanders Captain Jack Sparrow (Depp) — the Pearl's mutinied former captain, arrived in Port Royal to steal a ship, possessed of a compass that points at what its holder wants most, an unsettled ten-year account with Barbossa, and a single pistol shot he has saved, for a decade, for the man who marooned him. The film's plot is a triple-cross conducted in tall ships: Jack trades Will's identity to Barbossa for the Pearl; Will, learning the price, trades himself; Elizabeth, marooned with Jack on the mutiny's island, burns the rum as a signal fire — the film's comic theology of sacrifice — and Commodore Norrington (Jack Davenport), Elizabeth's fiancé and the Navy's most correct officer, conducts the rescue that becomes the final battle's frame. In the Isla's cave, with the last coin, Will's blood and a returned Jack — now cursed himself by pocketing a coin, a skeleton at the duel's moonlit climax — the accounts converge: Jack spends his ten-year bullet on Barbossa at the exact moment Will's blood restores the crew's mortality, and Barbossa dies mid-sensation, an apple in his hand, feeling, at last, everything — "I feel… cold." The coda is a gallows and a rescue: Jack, condemned at Port Royal, is freed by Will's thrown sword and Elizabeth's declared love, escapes over the parapet to the Pearl — restored to him by a crew that kept the Code loosely, as is traditional — and sails out as the film's final verdict on its own title: a pirate, and a good man, in the same captain.

Place in the culture

The film single-handedly revived the pirate picture, launched a five-film franchise, and installed Depp's Sparrow — a performance built, famously, on Keith Richards and Pepé Le Pew, which studio executives initially feared would ruin the film — among the century's defining screen characters. Its ride-source became a film that then re-edited the ride (Sparrow and Barbossa now appear in the attraction), and its dialogue ("Savvy?", "Why is the rum gone?", the Code's "guidelines") entered the permanent vernacular of adventure cinema.

The ride becomes a fleet

Disney had tried to film its 1967 attraction for a decade; the Elliott–Rossio script's masterstroke was grafting a supernatural curse — inspired by the ride's ghostly tableaux — onto a classical swashbuckler, and Bruckheimer's production built it with practical ships (three full vessels, including a Pearl constructed on a barge) and location shooting in the Caribbean and Los Angeles. Depp invented Sparrow's whole register on set — the gold teeth his own addition, the swaying sea-legs walk, the slurred courtesy — to the initial alarm of executives who asked whether the character was drunk or gay, and to the eventual verdict of an Academy Award nomination, the first for a performance in a theme-park adaptation. The premiere was held at Disneyland, with the film screened beside its source. Released July 9, 2003 to low expectations for a "dead" genre, it became the summer's word-of-mouth phenomenon and the year's fourth-biggest film; four sequels followed, and the attraction was refurbished to include its own creations.

03 / NARRATIVE SUMMARY

Story Synopsis

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL FULL STORY SUMMARY

The film opens in fog and childhood: a girl singing a pirate song on a warship's bow, a boy pulled from a wreck with a gold skull medallion, a father's warning that the coin marks him as a pirate — and Elizabeth's eight-year keeping of both the coin and the secret, the film's whole plot filed as a childhood act of mercy. Port Royal's adult order is then introduced as a machine of ranks: Governor Swann's mansion, Norrington's promotion ceremony (Elizabeth fainting off the parapet, corset first, into the sea — the medallion's pulse calling the Pearl like a bell), Will the apprentice presenting swords he made and his master will claim, and Jack Sparrow arriving atop a sinking ship with the cinema's most efficient character introduction: a pirate surveying a gallows of his colleagues, removing his hat to them, and stepping ashore from a mast as his vessel submerges beneath him. The Pearl's night raid — cannon fire, skeletal boarders revealed in moonlight's strobing, Elizabeth invoking parley and the Pirate's Code with a scholar's nerve — converts the adventure into a negotiation story: every subsequent set piece is a bargain struck, betrayed or honored, and the film's real action is contractual. Barbossa's dinner with his captive (the apple he cannot taste, offered as the curse's whole biography: "you'd best start believing in ghost stories, Miss Turner — you're in one") is the film's gothic centerpiece, and Jack and Will's counter-expedition — stealing the Interceptor by the immortal ruse of walking under a rowboat on the seabed, recruiting Gibbs and a Tortuga crew — is its comic one.

The Isla de Muerta convergence is a market of crossed purposes: Jack selling Will's bloodline to buy back his ship; Barbossa selling Jack's life for the ritual's security; Elizabeth, her blood already spilled and found wanting (the film's cruelest clerical error: the curse's accountants have the wrong Turner), discarded onto the marooning island with Jack, where the rum-runner's cache becomes her signal fire and his despair — "welcome to the Caribbean, love" delivered to an empty horizon. Norrington's rescue converts the romance plot into the battle's frame: Elizabeth trades her hand in marriage for the Pearl's pursuit, and the final engagement is fought on three decks at once — the Dauntless's crew against skeletons who cannot die, Will and Elizabeth freeing Jack's crew from the Pearl's brig, and in the cave, the duel of the two captains, in which Jack's pocketed coin — revealed in a pass through moonlight, the film's great visual punchline: a skeleton grinning inside Sparrow's coat — buys him the duel's duration until Will's knife, Will's blood and the last coin close the account. Barbossa's death is the film's moral receipt: the curse lifted a half-second after Jack's decade-saved bullet arrives, so that the great sensation-starved pirate dies feeling the bullet, the cold, and the apple he drops — mortality restored as a final, fatal gift. The gallows coda then reverses the opening's ranks: the Navy's rope, the blacksmith's thrown sword, the governor's daughter's declared love, the commodore's grace in defeat ("this is a beautiful sword — I would expect the man who made it to show the same care and devotion in every aspect of his life"), and a crew's vote, taken over the rail, to go back for the captain who once defined their Code's flexibility. The Pearl's wheel, at the last, is in the hands the film has spent 143 minutes auditing for it.

The full narrative summary is an editorial paraphrase, not dialogue or a scene-by-scene transcript.

05 / CHARACTER DATABASE

Character Database

Search the principal characters documented in this film. Roles and motivations below summarize their function in the 2003 movie.

THE CAPTAIN

Jack Sparrow

Johnny Depp. The Black Pearl's mutinied captain — a pirate of reputation in ruins and genius in improvisation, whose compass points at his heart's desire, whose pistol holds one ten-year-old bullet with a name on it, and whose whole method is the negotiation conducted at a dead run. Depp plays him with a swaying, gold-toothed, half-soused elegance that reinvented the screen pirate outright, and Jack's function is the story's portrait of freedom as a discipline: he is the only character who never lies about what he is, which makes him, paradoxically, the film's most reliable counterparty. The archive files him as the decade's definitive screen pirate: a captain whose ship is a philosophy with sails.

THE MUTINEER

Hector Barbossa

Geoffrey Rush. The Pearl's first mate turned captain by mutiny — the curse's chief sufferer and its most articulate: a decade of untasted apples and unquenched wine has made him piracy's great tragedian, a sensualist sentenced to a world without sensation. Rush plays him with a theatrical, apple-crunching relish that makes him the film's co-lead in everything but billing, and Barbossa's function is the story's portrait of the thief punished in his appetite: he stole gold and lost taste — the curse as an accountant's joke. The archive files him as the decade's finest screen pirate captain after Sparrow: a mutineer whose last word is a weather report from inside his own body.

THE BLACKSMITH

Will Turner

Orlando Bloom. A foundry apprentice of masterful skill and invisible credit — his swords are sold under his drunken master's name — whose blood, unknown to him, is the curse's final ingredient, and whose love for Elizabeth is the plot's engine and its conscience. Bloom plays him with an earnest, athletic sincerity that anchors the film's romance in the classical mode, and Will's function is the story's portrait of the heir as a tradesman: the pirate's son who chose the forge, discovering that the blood will have its say regardless. The archive files him as the decade's most complete screen blacksmith: a maker whose finest work is a sword thrown into a gallows rope.

THE GOVERNOR'S DAUGHTER

Elizabeth Swann

Keira Knightley. Port Royal's governor's daughter — a pirate-romance scholar in a corset, whose childhood mercy (a medallion kept, a boy protected) authors the entire plot, and whose arc is the film's true education: from invoking parley as a dinner guest to burning a rum cache as a signal officer to choosing her husband at a gallows. Knightley plays her with a fierce, quick-witted intelligence that makes her the film's strategist in silk, and Elizabeth's function is the story's portrait of the lady as the adventure's author: every man in the film is reacting to her decisions. The archive files her as the decade's finest screen governor's daughter: a hostage who conducted her own rescue as a series of negotiations.

THE COMMODORE

James Norrington

Jack Davenport. The Royal Navy's rising officer — Elizabeth's fiancé, Jack's pursuer, the film's most correct man, whose defeat in love is administered with a grace that makes him the story's quiet standard of honor. Davenport plays him with a clipped, wry dignity that keeps the rival sympathetic at every reverse, and Norrington's function is the story's portrait of the institution's best product: the law, in him, is neither stupid nor cruel — merely outsailed. The archive files him as the decade's most complete screen commodore: an officer whose finest maneuver is a concession, handsomely made.

THE FIRST MATE

Joshamee Gibbs

Kevin McNally. Jack's loyal first mate — a former Royal Navy sailor turned pirate, the saga's chorus of superstition and seamanship, recruited in a Tortuga pig-pen and thereafter the Pearl's institutional memory. McNally plays him with a rumbling, salty warmth, and Gibbs's function is the story's portrait of the crew as a continuity: captains are mutinied and restored; Gibbs endures, keeping the ship's lore and its bottles. The archive files him as the decade's most consequential screen first mate: a memory with a cutlass, fluent in omens.

THE GOVERNOR

Weatherby Swann

Jonathan Pryce. Port Royal's governor and Elizabeth's father — a decent, bewildered administrator whose authority is real everywhere except over the events of this film, and whose love for his daughter is the civil power's one reliable compass. Pryce plays him with a fluttering, humane dignity, and Swann's function is the story's portrait of the state as a parent: his cannons defend the town; his heart, at the gallows, quietly defects to the pirates. The archive files him as the decade's most complete screen governor in an adventure film: an empire's local office, outvoted by fatherhood.

THE COMIC RELIEF, PORT

Pintel

Lee Arenberg. A Pearl crewman — with Ragetti, the cursed crew's double act: violent, ignorant, oddly theological, forever debating the curse's terms and the Code's fine print in the middle of atrocities. Arenberg plays him with a bellowing, exasperated energy, and Pintel's function is the story's portrait of the rank and file as the curse's true constituency: the officers have philosophies; the crew has grievances and a glass eye's custody dispute. The archive files him as the decade's most consequential minor pirate: a skeleton with a union's instincts.

THE COMIC RELIEF, STARBOARD

Ragetti

Mackenzie Crook. Pintel's partner — the wooden-eyed, scripture-quoting half of the double act, whose reading of the Bible (for its pictures, and in hopes of credit) is the crew's whole theological department. Crook plays him with a gangling, plaintive absurdity, and Ragetti's function is the story's portrait of the pirate as a parishioner: even the undead, it turns out, keep a chaplain's hours. The archive files him as the decade's most memorable minor pirate: an eye, wooden, on the lookout for grace.

THE CAPTAIN OF THE REVENGE

Anamaria

Zoe Saldaña. A pirate captain recruited in Tortuga — whose boat Jack once stole (and, she notes, sunk), whose price for joining the expedition is a slap-administered negotiation and the Interceptor itself, promised as compensation. Saldaña plays her with a compact, furious authority, and Anamaria's function is the story's portrait of Jack's past as a creditors' queue: half the Caribbean holds his paper, and the film files her claim as the most legitimate. The archive files her as the decade's most consequential minor captain in film: a creditor, sailing, with the fleet.

THE SILENT CREWMAN

Cotton

David Bailie. A Pearl crewman whose tongue was cut out — and who has trained his parrot to speak for him, imperfectly, so that the crew interprets the bird like an oracle with a vocabulary problem. Bailie plays him in eloquent silence, and Cotton's function is the story's portrait of the crew's diversity as the saga's texture: the Pearl's company is a parliament of the maimed, the cursed and the mute, and it out-sails the Navy's uniformity. The archive files him as the decade's most efficient silent performance in an adventure film: a sailor whose lines are delivered by proxy, in feathers.

THE SMALLEST CREWMAN

Marty

Martin Klebba. A dwarf crewman of the Pearl — recruited in Tortuga, handy in a fight and in a brig escape, whose stature the film never treats as his summary. Klebba plays him with a compact, cheerful ferocity, and Marty's function is the story's portrait of the crew as a true cross-section: the Pearl takes all the Navy discards and makes a navy of them. The archive files him as the decade's most consequential minor crewman in film: a sailor whose size is the least interesting entry in his file.

Norrington's command: the Dauntless and the Interceptor, the gallows and the broadside — the empire's Caribbean enforcement, correct, brave and chronically out-negotiated. THE FREE PORT

Tortuga

The pirates' ungoverned harbor: recruitment office, brawl, market and choir — where Jack assembles a crew from the Caribbean's discharged and disreputable in a single evening. The Code is more what you'd call guidelines

The archive files the design as the story's durable sociology: in this sea, the flexible code outlives the correct one.

08 / THE AZTEC GOLD, THE BLOOD DEBT & THE PIRATE'S CODE

The Aztec Gold, the Blood Debt & the Pirate's Code

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

The Aztec gold — the curse's instrument

882 pieces of Cortés's treasure, cursed by the heathen gods against its thieves: whoever takes a piece is undead until all are returned with the thieves' blood. The gold's function is the story's portrait of plunder as a loan: the treasure was never owned, only borrowed at an interest rate of sensation. The archive files the gold as the decade's most consequential fictional treasure: a chest whose contents bill their holders.

The blood debt — the ritual's fine print

The curse's settlement terms: every coin returned, and the blood of every thief — or his offspring — repaid to the chest. Bootstrap's coin, mailed to his son in repentance, makes Will the account's final settlement. The debt's function is the story's portrait of inheritance as a liability: the son's blood pays the father's theft, and the film files the doctrine in both directions — Will's blood also buys his father's absolution. The archive files the blood debt as the decade's most consequential fictional inheritance: a mortgage, denominated in a son.

The Pirate's Code — guidelines

The pirates' ancient articles — parley, marooning, the captain's authority — kept, as Barbossa concedes, more as guidelines than rules, by the very men who wrote them. The Code's function is the story's portrait of law as a living negotiation: it binds exactly as much as its keepers' honor requires, which turns out, at the gallows and the rail, to be just enough. The archive files the Code as the decade's most consequential fictional legal system: a constitution whose flexibility is its ratification.

The compass — desire as navigation

Jack's bartered compass, which points not north but at what its holder most wants: the saga's perfect instrument for a man whose wants are a fleet, a freedom and a reputation at once. The compass's function is the story's portrait of navigation by appetite: it is useless to the dutiful and infallible for the honest. The archive files the compass as the decade's most consequential fictional instrument: a needle that reads its reader.

10 / THEMATIC ANALYSIS

Themes & Analysis

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Freedom is a ship — the vessel as the self

The film's governing theme is its captain's definition: the Pearl is freedom — not transport, not property, but the condition of a life self-steered — and every character's arc is priced in that currency (Will's forge, Elizabeth's corset, Norrington's rank, Barbossa's sensations are each a hull of some kind, kept or escaped). The theme is worked out in the final image (the wheel, the horizon, the song returning) until the audience holds the film's doctrine: a person is the vessel they answer for, and its loss is the biography's true mutiny. Its proposition reaches every professional in the audience whose Pearl is a practice, a firm, a craft: your freedom has a hull, and its maintenance is not a hobby. The reach is the story's question to every captain currently in a rowboat: what is your ship — and who is keeping it for you?

Sensation as mortality — the curse's fine print

The film's second theme is its curse's design: that the thieves' punishment is not death but numbness — a decade of untasted food, unfelt wind, unquenched everything — and that mortality, with its pains included, is reclassified by the starved as a gift. The theme is worked out in Barbossa's arc (his evil is a famine; his death, feeling cold, is a feast of one sensation) until the audience holds the film's doctrine of appetite: to feel anything, at the end, is the whole treasure. Its proposition reaches every numb high-achiever in the audience sitting on an Isla of unspendable gold: the chest you cannot taste is a sentence, whatever the market says. The reach is the story's question to every holder of a hoard: when did you last feel the wind — and what would your blood buy back?

Guidelines, not rules — the flexible code

The film's third theme is its jurisprudence: that the pirates' Code — kept loosely, invoked opportunistically, honored at the rope — outperforms the Navy's statutes at every crisis, because a code held as guidelines can bend to the case while a rulebook can only break or bind. The theme is worked out in the plot's bargains (parley honored by its inventors' enemies, the crew's vote at the rail) until the audience holds the film's legal philosophy: a constitution's survival is measured by its amendments' honesty, not its text's rigidity. Its proposition reaches every institution in the audience governed by a founding document: the question is never whether your people bend the code, but whether the code was built to be bent toward its purpose. The reach is the story's question to every keeper of a rulebook: which of your rules are guidelines wearing a statute's uniform — and does the crew know?

The blood of the father — inheritance as an account

The film's fourth theme is its Turners: that a son inherits his father's debts in his very blood — Bootstrap's theft is Will's mortgage; Bootstrap's repentance is also Will's inheritance, the coin mailed home as the father's one honest asset — and the film files both entries in the same account. The theme is worked out until the audience holds the film's doctrine of lineage: you do not choose the account; you choose its settlement. Its proposition reaches every heir in the audience — of a name, a firm, a reputation, a debt: the audit begins with an inventory of both columns. The reach is the story's question to every Bootstrap's child: which of your father's coins are you carrying — and have you decided, yet, what your blood is for?

11 / FULL SPOILER CONCLUSION

Ending Explained

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL THE ENDING

Jack, secretly cursed by a pocketed coin, duels Barbossa and shoots him with his ten-year-old bullet at the moment Will returns the last coin with his blood, lifting the curse — Barbossa dies mortal, dropping his apple. Jack is later rescued from the gallows by Will and Elizabeth, escapes to the Pearl, and sails away captain once more.

What the ending establishes

  • The synchronized shot and blood file the film's two economies as one ledger: the feud's bullet and the curse's debt close in the same second, or neither closes.
  • Barbossa's dropped apple files sensation as the treasure's true denomination: he dies rich by the only measure his decade taught him.
  • The gallows rescue files the town's verdict as a defection: Port Royal's best citizens — its heir, its daughter, its commodore's grace — vote against its rope.
  • The crew's return files the Code's flexibility as vindicated: they kept it loosely, as is traditional, and the looseness saved its author's life.
  • Jack at the wheel files freedom as the film's closing balance: the Pearl is not won; it is resumed, like a self.

What remains uncertain

Bootstrap Bill's fate at the ocean's bottom, the crew's captured company's trials, and what a compass that points at desire will do to a captain at peace — all stand open at the film's close (the sequels take up the first of these directly). Within this film, the archive files the uncertainty as the story's own last word on its theme: the curse is lifted, the accounts are closed, and the horizon — the only creditor Jack Sparrow acknowledges — remains open for business.

12 / PURPOSES OF THE STORY

Moral of The Story

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Pirates of the Caribbean: The Curse of the Black Pearl uses a cursed chest, a saved bullet and a flexible code to examine freedom, sensation and inheritance: a captain whose ship is his self; a crew whose treasure is a famine; and a blacksmith whose blood settles his father's account in both its columns. Through the medallion, the island and the gallows, the film argues that freedom is a vessel you answer for; that to feel anything is the whole treasure; and that a code kept as guidelines, honestly, outlives a rulebook kept as a cage.

Why tell this story?

Because Elliott and Rossio found, inside a theme-park ride's tableaux, the pirate genre's missing engine: a curse that makes its thieves accountants — every coin to be returned, every drop of blood repaid — so that the adventure could be about settlement rather than plunder. Verbinski and Depp then gave the settlement a captain worth watching it for. The result is the decade's defining swashbuckler, the archive files: a ghost story whose treasure is a sensation and whose hero is a negotiation with sails.

1. Know your Pearl

Jack's definition is the film's first counsel, for every professional whose freedom has a hull — the practice, the studio, the craft, the route: name the vessel your self-steering depends on, and audit its custody as you would a child's. The counsel is a freedom doctrine with the rowboat as its warning: a captain separated from his ship does not become a landsman; he becomes a plotter of recoveries, and every year of it is billed. If your Pearl is currently in another's hands, the film's whole plot is your business plan: patience, a compass that reads your desire honestly, and one bullet, saved, for the exact account.

2. Taste the apple

Barbossa's famine is the film's second counsel, for every holder of an unspendable hoard — the savings never lived on, the reputation never risked, the gold in an Isla of a portfolio: a treasure that cannot be tasted is a curse with good marketing, and its interest compounds in numbness. The counsel is an appetite doctrine with the dropped apple as its receipt: he dies grateful for cold. Inventory your sensations as rigorously as your assets. The wind counts. Schedule it like revenue.

3. Write your code as guidelines, honestly

The Code's flexibility is the film's third counsel, for every founder, captain and keeper of a rulebook: a constitution that cannot bend toward its purpose will be broken against it, usually at the worst moment and by your best people. The counsel is a governance doctrine with the rail vote as its ratification: the crew's loose keeping of the Code is what saves its author. Draft for the bending you actually want — parley's honor, the captain's recall — and say, in the document itself, that these are guidelines for pirates of honor. The honesty is the binding agent.

4. Settle the father's account in both columns

Will's blood is the film's fourth counsel, for every heir auditing an inheritance — a name, a firm, a family's story: the account has two columns, the theft and the repentance, the debt and the mailed coin, and a settlement that reads only one will misprice your blood. The counsel is a lineage doctrine with the chest as its ledger: Will's blood closes the curse because it carries both the pirate and the penitent. Read your whole inheritance before you spend yourself on it. Some of what you carry is ballast; some is ballast's opposite, and the difference is the biography.

5. Save one bullet for the true account

Jack's pistol is the film's fifth counsel, for every negotiator with a long feud and a short magazine: identify the single expenditure that settles the foundational account — not the daily skirmishes, not the intermediaries — and refuse, for a decade if necessary, to spend it on anything else. The counsel is a strategy doctrine with the marooning as its terms: he was given one shot with his exile's kit, and the discipline of its keeping is his whole revenge's finance. Most campaigns die of a thousand small expenditures. Keep yours chambered, named, and patient.

6. Let the crew vote at the rail

The rescue's vote is the film's sixth counsel, its benediction, for every leader whose people hold their recall in their hands: build, across your command, the kind of credit that makes a crew turn a ship around for a captain under a rope — credit deposited in shared storms, honest shares and a code kept flexibly — because no contract clause covers the gallows. The counsel is a leadership doctrine with the returning sail as its dividend: they came back because of who he had been at sea, not what he could pay ashore. Audit your rail credit in peacetime. The rope, when it comes, allows no time for fundraising.

The moral of the story is…

Know your Pearl and keep its custody; taste the apple while you can; write your code as guidelines, honestly — and save one bullet for the true account.

Pirates of the Caribbean: The Curse of the Black Pearl teaches that freedom is a vessel a person answers for; that sensation, not gold, is the treasure's denomination; and that a father's account, read in both its columns, can be settled by a son's blood and a blacksmith's thrown sword.

The film's final question is therefore not whether you would sail with Jack Sparrow — the film files that under "compass readings." It is what your Pearl is and who holds it; which of your hoard you can still taste; and whether your crew, at the rail, would vote to come back for you.
