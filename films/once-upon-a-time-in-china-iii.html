01 / FILM IDENTITY

Overview

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Once Upon a Time in China III (黃飛鴻之三獅王爭霸, "Wong Fei-hung 3: The Lion King Competition") is a Hong Kong martial arts film directed, produced and co-written by Tsui Hark, the third installment of the Once Upon a Time in China series, starring Jet Li as Wong Fei-hung with Rosamund Kwan, Max Mok and Hung Yan-yan. First released in Taiwan in December 1992, it opened in Hong Kong on February 11, 1993. With Chinese sovereignty eroding under foreign imperialism, the Empress Dowager Cixi and Li Hongzhang stage a lion dance competition to showcase Chinese martial arts and restore national pride, inviting troupes from across China to compete for the title of Lion Dance King. Wong Fei-hung travels by train from Foshan to Beijing with 13th Aunt (Rosamund Kwan) and his apprentice Leung Foon (Max Mok) to visit his father, Wong Kei-ying, at the Cantonese Ten Tigers Association. At the station, 13th Aunt's old Britain classmate, the Russian diplomat Tomanovsky, begins vying for her attention — to Wong's disgust at the Western custom of kissing a woman's hand. Wong Kei-ying, attacked by the wealthy rival Chiu Tin-bak and his henchman Clubfoot, has escaped with minor injuries, and blesses his son's romance. Before the official contest, the gathered troupes are already brawling in an unofficial one; Leung Foon's mischief accidentally unleashes a stampede that crushes Clubfoot's legs, Chiu abandons the crippled man, and Wong — taking pity — shelters and heals his enemy's henchman, who becomes his apprentice. With a camera Tomanovsky gave her, 13th Aunt uncovers a plot to assassinate Li Hongzhang during the competition, with Tomanovsky among the conspirators; Wong enters the contest to stop it, defeats Chiu and his deadly lion mask atop the scaffold, and wins — while Tomanovsky is shot dead by his fellow Russians, exposed as a spy for the Japanese embassy. Wong then refuses the gold medal: his victory, he tells Li Hongzhang, is a Pyrrhic one, for the competition has only deepened conflict among the Chinese themselves. He throws the medal back, turns his back, and walks away.

THE FAREWELL

Li's Last Chapter

The third film was Jet Li's final Once Upon a Time in China as Wong Fei-hung in the original run — Vincent Zhao took the role for parts IV and V, and Li returned only for part VI, filed elsewhere in this archive.

THE SET-PIECE

The Scaffold Final

The closing lion-dance battle atop the scaffold — dozens of rival lions, one deadly mask, three of Wong's party fighting upward — stands among the most celebrated set-pieces in the genre's history.

Place in the culture

The film lives in the culture as the series' most ambivalent chapter: a national pageant staged by a dying dynasty, in which the hero's greatest victory is the refusal of its prize. Its politics are the trilogy's thesis distilled — China's enemy, Wong concludes on the scaffold, is not only the foreigner at the gate but the Chinese set against Chinese for a medal — and its final gesture, the medal thrown back at the empire's chief minister, remains one of the defining images of 1990s Hong Kong cinema. For this Galaxy it completes Li's original Wong Fei-hung trilogy, filed here alongside parts I and II (built separately in this run) and his farewell return in part VI.

03 / NARRATIVE SUMMARY

Story Synopsis

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL FULL STORY SUMMARY

The dynasty, losing its country to foreign pressure, decides to answer with choreography: the Empress Dowager Cixi and Li Hongzhang proclaim a Lion Dance King competition, summoning China's troupes to Beijing to demonstrate national strength. Wong Fei-hung arrives by train with 13th Aunt and Leung Foon to visit his father at the Cantonese Ten Tigers Association — and at the station meets the competition's other front: Tomanovsky, a Russian diplomat and 13th Aunt's classmate from her studies in Britain, who begins courting her with Western gallantries, kissing her hand while Wong seethes at the custom. Wong Kei-ying has been attacked by Chiu Tin-bak, a wealthy rival martial artist, and his henchman Clubfoot, but escaped with minor injuries; seeing his son and 13th Aunt together, the elder Wong gives the couple his blessing. The gathered troupes cannot wait for the official contest and are already fighting an unofficial one in the streets. Wong spectates — but Leung Foon and his friends have secretly joined the mischief. Chasing the annoying Leung, Clubfoot is caught in a stampede when Leung accidentally releases a stable of horses, and his legs are crushed under the hooves. Chiu abandons him as useless. Wong Fei-hung takes the crippled henchman in and heals his legs; Clubfoot, first hostile, is won by the mercy and becomes Wong's new apprentice.

The pageant's second plot is photographed into the open. Tomanovsky has given 13th Aunt a camera; with it, she inadvertently uncovers a conspiracy to assassinate Li Hongzhang during the competition — and Tomanovsky is one of the conspirators. She warns Wong, who joins the contest he had disdained, to stop the assassins from inside it. In the final round, Wong, Leung Foon and Clubfoot fight dozens of rival lion dancers up the scaffold; Chiu joins carrying a large and deadly lion mask, and Wong defeats him and wins the competition. Meanwhile the assassination fails, and Tomanovsky is shot dead by his fellow Russians, who confirm he was a spy working for the Japanese embassy. Then comes the film's famous refusal: presented with the gold medal, Wong declines it. His victory is a Pyrrhic victory, he tells Li Hongzhang — the competition has only led to greater conflict among the Chinese people themselves. He throws the medal back to the minister, turns his back on him, and walks away.

The full narrative summary is an editorial paraphrase of the documented accounts (Wikipedia's plot account), not dialogue or a scene-by-scene transcript.

07 / WORLD & SETTING

World & Setting

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Beijing — the capital as a stage

The film's world is the imperial capital in the dynasty's late century: train stations receiving Russian diplomats, associations housing Cantonese elders, streets full of brawling lions, and a court answering erosion with choreography. It is a city where the era's three forces — tradition, modernity and empire — meet in a single love triangle at a railway platform, and where a folk festival must carry the weight of national survival. The Setting's function in the record is the archive's portrait of the trilogy's widest canvas: Foshan's streets have become the capital's stage, and every private story in it — a courtship, a crippling, a blessing — is simultaneously a dispatch from a civilization negotiating its decline. The archive files this Beijing as the decade's most consequential fictional capital in film: a sovereignty, performed, while it lasted.

The Train — the modern arrival

The railway that brings Wong's party from Foshan, and the station where the old classmate waits with his hand-kissing. The Train's function is the story's portrait of modernity as the plot's delivery system: it imports the hero, the diplomat and the era's new speeds in one arrival, and the film's first conflict is a courtesy. The archive files this train as the decade's most consequential fictional train in film: a century, arriving, on schedule.

10 / THEMATIC ANALYSIS

Themes & Analysis

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

A Pyrrhic victory is a diagnosis, not a trophy

The film's governing theme is the refused medal: the winner's audit of the contest itself. The theme is worked out from the street brawls to the scaffold until the audience holds its doctrine of the fratricidal pageant: a competition that sets a nation's members against one another for a symbol of unity does not merely fail to unite — it manufactures the division it displays, paying out grudges in public and calling the receipts pride. Wong's refusal is the only move that converts the spectacle back into information. Its proposition reaches every participant in a unifying contest in the audience. The reach is the story's question to every winner of an internal competition: what did your victory cost the whole you were meant to strengthen — and do you have the winner's one privilege, the refusal, and the minister to throw it back to?

Mercy is the only appreciating payroll

The film's second theme is Clubfoot's conversion: the discarded henchman revalued by his enemy's household. The theme is worked out through the two employers until the audience holds its doctrine of the two payrolls: wages terminate at depreciation, because a purchased loyalty is a contract with the work, not the worker — while mercy, which pays nothing, appreciates, because it was extended to the person at his lowest valuation and is therefore owed at every higher one. Its proposition reaches every employer and employee in the audience. The reach is the story's question to every person who manages anyone: when your Clubfoot is crippled — by the market, by injury, by age — which employer are you on the record as being: the one who abandons in the street, or the one whose household is famous for the opposite?

The gift testifies against the giver

The film's third theme is the camera: the diplomat's courtship gift exposing the diplomat. The theme is worked out through 13th Aunt's photographs until the audience holds its doctrine of the double-agent instrument: every technology imported as seduction carries its maker's world inside it, and in the hands of the person it was meant to impress, it may document the very operation it was meant to decorate — the era's tools do not stay loyal to the era's powers. Its proposition reaches every recipient of a powerful patron's gifts in the audience. The reach is the story's question to every person courted with instruments: what is your camera currently photographing — and if you developed all of it, honestly, whose conspiracy would it expose?

Spectacle is the declining state's last language

The film's fourth theme is the pageant itself: a dynasty fluent only in ceremony. The theme is worked out through the court's production until the audience holds its doctrine of the staged sovereignty: when a state can no longer perform its functions — defend, tax, negotiate — it performs its symbols instead, at rising cost and rising fidelity, until the performance becomes the most accurate available report on the state itself. The lions were supposed to lie about China's unity; staged honestly, they told the truth. Its proposition reaches every citizen of a spectacle-producing order in the audience. The reach is the story's question to every audience member at a national pageant: if this ceremony is the report, what is it reporting — and who in it is playing Wong, climbing the scaffold only to hand the medal back?

11 / FULL SPOILER CONCLUSION

Ending Explained

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL THE ENDING

In the final round, Wong Fei-hung, Leung Foon and Clubfoot fight dozens of rival lion dancers up the scaffold; Wong defeats Chiu Tin-bak and his deadly lion mask and wins the Lion Dance King competition. The assassination plot fails, and Tomanovsky is shot dead by his fellow Russians, exposed as a spy for the Japanese embassy. Presented with the gold medal, Wong refuses it: his victory is a Pyrrhic victory, because the competition only led to greater conflict among the Chinese people. He throws the medal back to Li Hongzhang, turns his back on him, and walks away.

What the ending establishes

  • The scaffold victory files the pageant as won on its own terms so it can be judged on the hero's: only the crowned man can return the crown's medal with authority.
  • Clubfoot's presence in the final files the mercy as compounded: the crippled henchman, healed, fights in the winning ascent — the film's payroll thesis, demonstrated at altitude.
  • Tomanovsky's death files the foreign contest as self-consuming: the powers shoot their own spy on China's stage, and the archive files the era's diplomacy as thereby summarized.
  • The refused medal files the trilogy's thesis as delivered: China's wound is internal division, and no choreography commissioned by the dividers can medal it closed.
  • The turned back files the hero's constitutional role as exercised: the folk master lectures the empire's minister in the empire's own ceremony, and walks out unpunished — the film's portrait of where legitimacy actually sat.

What remains uncertain

The documented accounts close with the walk-away; the dynasty's decline, the powers' contest and China's division all continue beyond the frame, and the archive files the uncertainty as the story's own last word on its theme: the hero can refuse the medal, but he cannot refuse the era — he can only file his dissent, in public, at the summit of its pageant, and leave the record standing.

12 / PURPOSES OF THE STORY

Moral of The Story

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Once Upon a Time in China III uses a national pageant, a crippled henchman and a diplomat's camera to examine fratricidal competition, the two payrolls, and the winner's privilege of refusal: a dynasty staging unity and manufacturing division; an employer discarding his injured man and an enemy appreciating him; a gift testifying against its giver; a champion climbing a scaffold to hand the prize back. Through the station, the streets and the scaffold, the film argues that a contest which sets a people against itself is a diagnosis, not a cure; that mercy is the only payroll that appreciates; and that the winner's highest use of a victory is sometimes to return it, with the reason stated.

Why tell this story?

Because the trilogy needed its hero to graduate from fighting China's enemies to auditing China's remedies — and because Tsui Hark understood that the folk master's final opponent was not a governor or a foreigner but a medal: the symbol that would convert his dissent into the dynasty's decoration. The archive files the result as the original trilogy's thesis, spoken from the top of a scaffold, in the decade's most quoted refusal.

1. Audit the unifying contest for its divisions

The pageant is the film's first counsel, for every participant in an internal competition: count what the contest manufactures. The counsel is a systems doctrine with the brawling troupes as its standard: before entering — or staging — the tournament meant to unite your house, price its fratricide: the grudges it will pay out, the rankings it will publish, the Clubfoots it will cripple in the preliminaries. Some contests are worth their divisions. The dynasty's was not, and no one in it ran the audit until the winner ran it from the podium. Run yours from the entry form.

2. Be the employer whose payroll appreciates

Clubfoot's conversion is the film's second counsel, for every manager of people: your treatment of the depreciated is your reputation's whole content. The counsel is a payroll doctrine with the two households as its standard: Chiu's wage bought service until the injury; Wong's mercy bought the final round's third fighter. The practical form is simple and expensive: when a member of your house is crippled — by the market, by health, by a stampede you partly caused — shelter first and audit later, in public enough that the house learns its own constitution. The archive notes the return on the investment is filed under "scaffold, final round."

3. Develop the patron's gift honestly

The camera is the film's third counsel, for every recipient of a powerful courtship: use the instrument; read all the film. The counsel is an intelligence doctrine with 13th Aunt's photographs as its standard: the gifts of the interested — the tools, the access, the introductions — are given to decorate an operation, and the recipient's one defense is to operate the gift at full honesty, documenting what it actually shows, including about the giver. Accept the camera. Take the pictures. Develop everything. The conspiracy you expose may be the one the gift was meant to hide, which is the gift's final, unintended generosity.

4. Reserve the winner's refusal

The thrown medal is the film's fourth counsel, for every victor in a compromised contest: the refusal is a privilege that must be earned and then spent. The counsel is a rhetoric doctrine with the podium as its standard: Wong could return the medal because he had won it, outright, in front of everyone — the refusal of the second-place is a complaint; the refusal of the champion is a verdict. So win first, cleanly, at full effort; and if the institution's prize would convert your victory into its decoration, spend the privilege: return it, state the reason in one sentence, and walk. The sentence will outlive the medal. It has.

5. Bless the couple between the attack and the pageant

Wong Kei-ying's blessing is the film's fifth counsel, for every elder of a house: ratify the young people's facts while you are still the authority they want ratifying them. The counsel is a succession doctrine with the Association as its standard: the father does not litigate the romance's propriety, audit the aunt's foreignness, or defer the blessing to a calmer season — he issues it between an assassination attempt and a national crisis, because elders' blessings are a currency that spends only in their lifetime. Whatever your house's equivalent approval is — the partnership, the marriage, the venture — issue it at the station, while the train is still in.

6. Spectate until the contest needs a conscience

Wong's late entry is the film's sixth counsel, its benediction, for every principled abstainer: non-participation is a position, not a purity. The counsel is an engagement doctrine with the scaffold as its standard: Wong spectates while the troupes brawl — correctly — and enters the moment the contest becomes the venue of an assassination, because the refusal to play is only honorable while the game is merely foolish. Audit your abstentions for their expiry: the pageant you disdain may become the one arena where the plot must be stopped, and the crown you refuse in advance cannot be thrown back. Enter, win, refuse — in that order, when the order is forced on you.

The moral of the story is…

Audit the unifying contest for its divisions; be the employer whose payroll appreciates; develop the patron's gift honestly — and reserve the winner's refusal.

Once Upon a Time in China III teaches that a contest which sets a people against itself is a diagnosis, not a cure; that mercy is the only payroll that appreciates; and that the winner's highest use of a victory is sometimes to return it, with the reason stated.

The film's final question is therefore not who is the Lion Dance King — the film files that under "ask the medal, mid-flight." It is what your own house's pageant is manufacturing besides pride; whose legs your institution would leave in the street; and whether, crowned at last atop your scaffold, you would have the sentence ready — and the minister to throw it to.
