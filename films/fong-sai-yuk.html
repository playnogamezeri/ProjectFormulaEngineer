01 / FILM IDENTITY

Overview

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Fong Sai-yuk (方世玉; also known as The Legend of Fong Sai-yuk and The Legend) is a 1993 Hong Kong action-comedy directed by Corey Yuen, written by Chan Kin-chung, Kevin Tsai and Jeffrey Lau, and produced by Jet Li, who stars as the Cantonese folk hero Fong Sai-yuk. The film won the Hong Kong Film Award and the Golden Horse Award for Best Action Choreography. Fong, a brash young champion, falls for Lei Ting-ting (Michelle Reis) at a track-and-field meeting; her father, the hot-headed tycoon Tiger Lui, stages a contest — defeat his wife Siu-wan (Sibelle Hu) atop a scaffold, without touching the ground, and win Ting-ting's hand. Fong fights Siu-wan to a standstill, glimpses a homely servant he mistakes for the bride, and deliberately throws the match. His mother Miu Tsui-fa (Josephine Siao), outraged at the family name's loss of face, enters disguised as Fong's elder brother, defeats Siu-wan and catches her as she falls — whereupon Siu-wan falls in love with the "brother," Tiger Lui insists on the marriage, and Fong is married to Ting-ting in his mother's place, discovering his bride's true beauty only after sparring with her in the dark. Then his father Fong Tak (Chu Kong) returns from a trip and is revealed as a member of the Red Flower Society, the anti-Qing underground; the Governor of Nine Gates (Vincent Zhao, in his first major screen role) ambushes him, demanding the society's list of members' names. The Fangs hide in the Lei residence, are recognized, and in the ensuing fight Siu-wan is killed by a gunshot and Fong Tak captured. The Governor stages a public execution to lure the son; Fong storms the ground alone, and at the critical moment his mother arrives with Chan Ka-lok (Adam Cheng), the society's leader, and its members — the Governor is defeated, Fong Tak freed, and Chan, impressed by the young man's bravery, takes him as his godson.

RELEASE

March 4, 1993

Ninety-one minutes; a smash hit across Asia, and the first of Li's two Fong Sai-yuk films in a single year — the sequel followed in July.

THE AWARDS

THE CROSSOVER

The antagonist is played by Vincent Zhao (Chiu Man-cheuk) — the Governor of Nine Gates was his first major screen role — so this archive page also serves his filmography in this Galaxy's records.

Place in the culture

The film lives in the culture as the reinvention that scandalized the purists and delighted everyone else: a folk hero rebuilt as a smiling, mischievous mama's boy, in a film whose true co-star is the mother — Josephine Siao's turn was singled out by reviewers as scene-stealing, and the mother-son double act became the diptych's engine. Contemporary critics filed it as a classic of kung fu cinema: dextrous, farcical, and anchored by fight invention of the highest order. For this Galaxy it opens the Fong Sai-yuk diptych (its sequel is filed separately in this run) and marks Li's great tonal pivot of 1993 — from the stern Wong Fei-hung to the grinning Canton champion, in the same spring.

The pivot production

The production record marks the film as Li's reinvention vehicle: fresh from the Once Upon a Time in China films' stern folk master, he produced for himself a folk hero of the opposite register — prankish, filial, romantic — under the direction of Corey Yuen, with action raised, per contemporary reviewers, to a level of surreal invention (assassin surfers, audience-head choreography) beyond even the series they had left. The archive records the gamble's result: a hit, two choreography prizes, and a sequel in cinemas within five months.

03 / NARRATIVE SUMMARY

Story Synopsis

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL FULL STORY SUMMARY

Fong Sai-yuk is Canton's beloved tearaway: a linen-seller's son with a champion's gifts and his mother's temper, to his father Fong Tak's embarrassment. At a track-and-field competition he falls for Lei Ting-ting, daughter of the bandit-turned-tycoon Tiger Lui. Tiger stages a contest for her hand: any man who can defeat his wife Siu-wan — herself a formidable martial artist — atop a scaffold, in a fight where neither may touch the ground, wins the bride. Fong fights Siu-wan across the scaffold and onto the heads of the crowd, has the match in hand — and then glimpses a homely servant girl in the audience, mistakes her for Ting-ting, and throws the fight rather than win such a prize. Word of the "defeat" reaches his mother. Miu Tsui-fa, a master in her own right, disguises herself as Fong's elder brother, enters the contest, defeats Siu-wan and catches her as she falls from the scaffold; the rescued Siu-wan, held in the stranger's arms, falls in love on the spot. Tiger Lui insists the "brother" marry his daughter — and since no brother exists, Fong is married to Ting-ting in the disguise's place, confined in the Lei residence, avoiding a bride he believes homely until a sparring match in the dark reveals her true face.

The comedy then changes key. Fong Tak returns from a trip, and the feeble-seeming father is revealed as a member of the Red Flower Society, the underground sworn to overthrow the Qing. The Governor of Nine Gates ambushes the society's meeting and demands the list of members' names; Fong and his mother arrive in time for Fong to hold the Governor off while his parents escape. The family hides, disguised, in the Lei residence — but the Governor visits, recognizes them, and in the ensuing fight Siu-wan is killed by a gunshot and Fong Tak is captured. The Governor stages a public execution of the father to bait the son. Fong storms the execution ground alone to save him; at the critical moment, Miu Tsui-fa arrives with Chan Ka-lok, leader of the Red Flower Society, and the society's members. The Governor and his men are defeated, Fong Tak is freed, and Chan Ka-lok — impressed by Fong Sai-yuk's bravery — takes the young man as his godson, inducting Canton's champion into the revolution his sequel will inherit.

The full narrative summary is an editorial paraphrase of the documented accounts (Wikipedia's synopsis and contemporary reviews), not dialogue or a scene-by-scene transcript.

  • The rescue files the trap as reversed by its own publicity: the execution advertised to bait the son also summoned the society — the state's theater, hijacked by its intended audience.
  • Siu-wan's death, earlier, files the triumph's price as already paid: the finale's joy is conducted in a film that has buried its funniest casualty, and the archive files the shadow as the sequel's inheritance.
  • Fong Tak's freedom files the refusal as vindicated: the man who would not spend the names is freed by the names' owners — the list, in effect, rescues its keeper.
  • The Governor's defeat files the folk claim as proven for this round: family plus society out-fights the administration, in public, on the state's own stage.
  • The adoption files the comedy as a recruiting film all along: Chan takes a godson, and the archive files the christening as the diptych's true final scene — the revolution acquiring its heir.

What remains uncertain

The documented accounts close with the adoption; the Governor's masters in the capital, and the society's long war, lie beyond the film — and its sequel, filed separately in this archive, takes up the godson's story inside the society. The archive files the uncertainty as the story's own last word on its theme: a folk hero's enlistment is a beginning disguised as an ending, and the family that won the afternoon has merely been promoted into a larger danger.

12 / PURPOSES OF THE STORY

Moral of The Story

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Fong Sai-yuk uses a thrown contest, a nonexistent brother and a defended roster to examine verification, concealed strength, and the ethics of other people's names: a champion who forfeits on a glimpse; a mother who is her family's secret army; a father who will die before spending a list; a state that stages executions as advertising. Through the scaffold, the Lei residence and the execution ground, the film argues that decisive action on unverified impressions is the root of most catastrophes; that every household's true strength is the member its filing system discounts; and that other people's names, once entrusted, are not yours to spend at any price.

Why tell this story?

Because the folk canon needed its hero young, funny and mothered — a legend whose engine is not solitude but family — and because Corey Yuen and Josephine Siao between them proved the genre's warmest thesis: that the deadliest person in Canton is the one the whole town has agreed to underestimate. The archive files the result as the action-comedy's high-water mark of 1993, prize-winning choreography in service of a household.
