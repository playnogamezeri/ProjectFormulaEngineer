01 / FILM IDENTITY

Overview

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

My Father Is a Hero (給爸爸的信, "Letter to Daddy"; released in the United States and United Kingdom as The Enforcer and Jet Li's The Enforcer) is a 1995 Hong Kong action film directed by Corey Yuen, written by Sandy Shaw and Wong Jing, and starring Jet Li, Anita Mui and the child actor Xie Miao. Kung Wei (Jet Li) is a police officer of the People's Republic of China assigned to infiltrate a Hong Kong criminal gang run by Po Kwong (Yu Rongguang). The cover is built in the cruelest available way: Wei is arrested in front of his home, in sight of his wife — who suffers from severe asthma — and his eight-year-old son Ku (Xie Miao), a young martial-arts student who watches his father taken away as a criminal. Before he goes, Wei gives the boy a beeper number: the one thread back. Imprisoned alongside Darkie (Blackie Ko), a former member of Po's gang, Wei escapes with him and is inducted into the gang, proving his loyalty in robberies and volunteering, in an arms deal with foreign criminals, to wear a bomb-laden vest as part of a ruse to steal the merchandise and keep the money in Po's hands. In Hong Kong, Inspector Anne Fong (Anita Mui) is investigating the gang's trail — and crosses paths with Ku, who, as his mother's health fails, has come looking for the father he believes is a criminal. Fong, befriending the boy, comes to suspect the truth her superior and former lover Inspector Cheng (Damian Lau) will not credit: that the gang's rising star is a policeman. The film ends where its premise always pointed — on a boat, with father and son fighting side by side, and the boy holding his breath against a killer's hands while a helicopter decides the rest.

RELEASE

March 2, 1995

Released in Hong Kong in the spring of Li's biggest decade; the American re-edit as The Enforcer carried it to the audience that would meet him in Hollywood three years later.

THE PAIRING

Father & Son, Again

Li and Xie Miao had played father and son the year before in The New Legend of Shaolin (1994) — filed later in this run — and their partnership is the era's defining screen family.

THE TITLE

Letter to Daddy

The Chinese title names the film's true subject: not the infiltration but the message — everything the boy cannot say to a father who vanished as a criminal, and the beeper number that stands in for it.

Place in the culture

The film lives in the culture as the most emotionally direct of Li's 1990s vehicles: an undercover-cop picture whose real suspense is not whether the cover holds but what it costs a child to believe it. Anita Mui, Cantopop's greatest star, plays the inspector as the family's surrogate investigator — the adult who reads the evidence correctly because she reads the boy correctly — and Xie Miao's Ku, a genuine child martial artist, fights the finale as his father's equal. For this Galaxy it is the dramatic panel of the 1995 pair, beside High Risk's farce: in both, a man's real work is done under a false account of him; here, the false account is filed in his son's heart.

03 / NARRATIVE SUMMARY

Story Synopsis

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL FULL STORY SUMMARY

Kung Wei's arrest is a performance with one audience that matters: his son. The mainland officer is taken from his home in disgrace — his asthmatic wife watching, eight-year-old Ku watching — because the Hong Kong gang he must infiltrate verifies a man by his police record, and the record must be witnessed to be believed. What the boy cannot be told is the only thing that would make it bearable. Wei leaves him a beeper number, and vanishes into prison, where his cellmate is Darkie, a small-time member of Po Kwong's gang. The escape is engineered; the gratitude is real; and Darkie delivers his rescuer, as designed, into Po's organization. Wei rises the undercover way — a staged robbery to prove loyalty, errands, violence survived — until he stands close enough to the center to volunteer for the operation's most expendable role: in an arms deal with foreign criminals, he wears a bomb-laden vest as the gang's guarantee, part of a ruse by which Po means to steal the bombs and keep the money too.

Across the border of the plot, the cost comes due. Ku's mother's health is failing, and the boy — who believes, on the evidence of his own eyes, that his father is a criminal who abandoned them — travels to Hong Kong to find him. There his path crosses Inspector Anne Fong's: she is investigating the gang, and the child searching for a gangster father is at first a lead, then a responsibility, then the key to the case. Watching the boy, Fong arrives at the conclusion her evidence has been circling: the gang's new star is no criminal but a policeman deeper undercover than her own service knows. Her superior, Inspector Cheng — her former lover — will not credit it. So the case's two truths run in parallel toward the same boat: the father's, which only the gang can see, and the son's, which only Fong believes.

The finale is fought on the water, where Po's operation comes apart and the cover with it. Father and son end up, at last, on the same side of the same fight — the boy's training suddenly legible as his inheritance — against Po and his men. Po takes Ku hostage and begins to choke him; and the child does the one thing his father's world taught him: he uses his breathing exercises, holding his air, buying seconds against the hands at his throat, until Fong's shot from the helicopter ends the grip. Po, beaten, arms the timer of the last bomb and pins Wei down with a chain aboard the boat as Fong lifts the unconscious boy away. Wei tears free, knocks Po out, and escapes the explosion by the width of the fuse — surfacing into the film's whole argument: a father restored to his son not by the cover's explanation but by the evidence of the fight, which the boy could read in the only language the family was ever fluent in.

11 / FULL SPOILER CONCLUSION

Ending Explained

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL THE ENDING

Aboard Po's boat, father and son fight Po's men together. Po seizes Ku and chokes him; Ku holds his breath using his breathing exercises until Inspector Fong, from a helicopter, shoots Po. As Fong evacuates the unconscious boy, Po arms the last bomb's timer and pins Wei with a chain; Wei breaks free, knocks Po out, and escapes the boat's explosion, reuniting with Fong and Ku.

What the ending establishes

  • The shared fight files the cover's acquittal: the son revises his verdict on the evidence of his father's conduct, the only testimony the operation ever allowed him.
  • The breathing exercise files the inheritance as sufficient: the father's training, not his explanation, is what stands between the boy and death.
  • Fong's shot files the dissenting deduction as the case's decisive act: the officer who believed the child saves the father.
  • The chain and the timer file the undercover man's last loyalty test: pinned to the operation's wreck, he frees himself — the service that sent him is not in the frame.
  • The reunion files the letter as finally delivered: everything the Chinese title promised — a message from son to father and back — arrives in person, over the water, in the clear.

What remains uncertain

The documented accounts agree on the ending's shape; what the family is owed — by the service that spent the boy's belief, and by the years the cover consumed — the film leaves unitemized, and the archive files the uncertainty as the story's own last word on its theme: a reunion settles the heart's account, but the institution's ledger, as in every film in this run, is carried forward unpaid.

12 / PURPOSES OF THE STORY

Moral of The Story

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

My Father Is a Hero uses a witnessed arrest, a beeper number and a bomb vest to examine the cover billed to the family, inheritance as training, and the jurisdiction of belief: an officer whose disgrace must be believed by his own son; an inspector whose correct deduction cannot get a hearing; a child whose breathing exercise is worth more, at the crisis, than every secret in the file. Through the prison, the gang and the boat, the film argues that an operation's morality is measured by what it spends of its dependents' trust; that the durable bequest is a practiced discipline, not an explanation; and that being right is a finding, while being believed is a jurisdiction — and the gap between them is where institutions fail.

Why tell this story?

Because the undercover genre had spent decades billing its costs to wives and children off-screen, and Corey Yuen's film moves the invoice into the title. The archive files the result as the emotional summit of Li's contemporary-set 1990s work: a police picture whose climax is a child holding his breath, and whose heroism is finally audited by the only witness who was never cleared to know.

1. Count the dependents in every cover story

The witnessed arrest is the film's first counsel, for every professional with a necessary fiction: your cover has an uncleared audience, and they are paying for it. The counsel is a dependents doctrine with Ku's verdict as its standard: before adopting any story that must be believed at home — the unexplained absences, the performed disgrace, the classified decade — itemize who will believe it, what the belief will cost them at their most vulnerable age, and what provision you have made that is actually spendable by them in your absence. Wei's service made no provision but a beeper. Make yours in advance, in writing, with a Fong appointed: a person outside the fiction who is cleared to believe the child.

2. Install training where explanations cannot go

The breathing exercise is the film's second counsel, for every parent, mentor and predecessor: bequeath disciplines, not narratives. The counsel is an inheritance doctrine with the boat as its standard: the people who will miss you cannot spend your explanations in a crisis — they can spend a practiced breath, a learned craft, a rehearsed composure — so invest your presence in repetitions rather than in speeches, and measure your legacy by what their hands and lungs can do on the worst day, unaccompanied. Wei's son survived on a technique. Audit your own bequests for their technique-to-narrative ratio, and correct it while there is still time to practice together.

3. File your dissent where the fire will find it

Cheng's refusal is the film's third counsel, for every correct dissenter: being right is a finding; arrange for it to become a jurisdiction. The counsel is a documentation doctrine with Fong's persistence as its standard: when your deduction is declined by the org chart, record it, date it, and continue the investigation through every channel the refusal leaves open — a witness protected, a theory tested — because institutions are eventually audited by events, and the file that survives is the one that was written down by the person who was right. Fong's vindication arrives by helicopter; it was earned at a desk, in the months her superior would not listen.

4. Appoint a believer for your witnesses

Fong's guardianship is the film's fourth counsel, for every institution that classifies: the uncleared deserve an advocate with clearance. The counsel is a welfare doctrine with the boy's journey as its standard: any operation that must be believed by dependents should budget, as a line item, a person whose duty is to those dependents — to hear the child, credit the spouse, and carry their evidence into the rooms where the cover is managed — because a state that can staff a bomb vest can staff a believer. If your organization has no such office, you are not looking at an oversight. You are looking at its accounting of what a childhood is worth.

5. Earn trust knowing you will be audited by its exposure

Po's promotion is the film's fifth counsel, for every performer of a role: excellence inside a fiction creates real debts outside it. The counsel is an exposure doctrine with Darkie as its standard: the people who trust your performance sincerely — the sponsor, the host, the friend made under the cover — are owed a reckoning proportioned to their sincerity, and the time to design that reckoning is before the boat, not on it. Wei's debts to Darkie are the film's unpaid bill, and the archive files them deliberately: every infiltrator's success has this line item. Price it into the mission, or admit the mission is priced in other people's hearts.

6. Answer the letter

The beeper is the film's sixth counsel, its benediction, for every absent father, mother, and classified heart: the message your dependents send into the silence is a letter, and it is addressed to you. The counsel is a correspondence doctrine with the Chinese title as its standard: 給爸爸的信 — a letter to daddy — is what every unreturned signal from a child, a spouse, a friend of the disappeared finally is; and the film's whole plot is the father's journey back to answer it in person. Whatever your classification, find the channel the operation forgot to forbid — a number kept, a ritual kept, a breath taught — and answer through it, at whatever cost in protocol. The letter has been waiting since the arrest. It does not require clearance. It requires a father.

The moral of the story is…

Count the dependents in every cover story; install training where explanations cannot go; file your dissent where the fire will find it — and answer the letter.

My Father Is a Hero teaches that an operation's morality is measured by what it spends of its dependents' trust; that the durable bequest is a practiced discipline, not an explanation; and that being right is a finding, while being believed is a jurisdiction.

The film's final question is therefore not whether the cover held — the film files that under "ask the gang." It is who is keeping your beeper number, and what they currently believe about you; what your people could actually do, trained by you, on the worst day of your absence; and which of your correct findings is waiting, this week, for a superior to refuse it — in writing, where the fire can find it.
