01 / FILM IDENTITY

Overview

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Mean Girls is a 2004 American teen comedy directed by Mark Waters, written by Tina Fey and based in part on Rosalind Wiseman's nonfiction study of adolescent female social hierarchy, Queen Bees and Wannabes. Released April 30, 2004, it grossed $130.1 million worldwide on a budget of about $17 million, and has since become one of the most quoted and culturally durable comedies of its century. Cady Heron (Lindsay Lohan), sixteen, raised in Africa by research-zoologist parents and homeschooled her whole life, enters North Shore High School as a transfer student with excellent math, no social antibodies, and a naturalist's eye — the film's narration frames the school through her field training: the cafeteria's territorial seating map, the jungle's watering-hole rules, and the three girls who run the ecosystem. The Plastics — Regina George (Rachel McAdams), the queen bee, an apex predator of such polish that her cruelty reads as weather; Gretchen Wieners (Lacey Chabert), her anxious lieutenant, heir to the Toaster Strudel fortune and keeper of everyone's secrets, "which is why she has so much hair"; and Karen Smith (Amanda Seyfried, in her film debut), the clique's sweet, vacant weathergirl of a person — recruit Cady as a novelty. Her first friends, the outcasts Janis Ian (Lizzy Caplan) and Damian (Daniel Franzese), recruit her right back: as a spy, who will infiltrate the Plastics and report, in Janis's long-grievanced design, their destruction. The campaign proceeds by sabotage disguised as devotion — Kälteen bars, a weight-gain diet disguised as a health regimen; foot cream passed off as face cream; the ex-boyfriend Aaron Samuels (Jonathan Bennett), Cady's crush and Regina's property, pried loose by gossip — until the infiltration completes itself in the infiltrator: Cady, fluent now in Girl World, begins to enjoy the throne's preliminaries, fails her calculus on purpose to pursue Aaron, throws a party to exclude her handlers, and is discovered by Janis in the film's thesis confrontation — she has become plastic, and worse, she has become Regina with worse hair. Regina, discovering the sabotage, deploys the regime's doomsday weapon: the Burn Book, the Plastics' scrapbook of slander about every girl and teacher in the school, which she frames Cady's circle for writing and distributes, photocopied, through the halls — converting the school's cold war hot in a single afternoon of rioting juniors, an emergency assembly, Ms. Norbury's (Fey) trust-fall curriculum of public apology, and a confession economy in which Cady, taking the blame that is partly hers, begins the film's last movement: penance by mathematics. Regina, exiled and feral, is hit by a school bus in the film's most famous punctuation mark — an accident Cady did not cause and half the school believes she arranged — and the regime ends not with a coronation but with a dispersal: Cady wins the state Mathletes championship on the limit of a function (the limit does not exist; neither, she realizes mid-proof, does the value of calling herself better than Regina), gives away the Spring Fling crown in pieces to every girl it was used to rank, and the Plastics dissolve into healthier ecosystems — Regina into lacrosse's sanctioned violence, Gretchen into a new clique she does not run, Karen into the school weather report. Girl World, the narration closes, is at peace — the film's definition of victory being not a better queen but a waterhole where nobody has to watch their back.

Place in the culture

The film became the defining high school comedy of its generation and one of the most memetically durable movies ever made: its lines ("On Wednesdays we wear pink," "That's so fetch," "You can't sit with us," October 3rd) entered the language as permanent vocabulary, October 3 is observed annually as Mean Girls Day, and the film's afterlife includes a hit Broadway musical (2018) and a film of that musical (2024). Lohan's performance was named by The New Yorker among the century's finest screen performances, McAdams's Regina became the template for a generation of screen queen bees, and Fey's script is the standard citation for how much sociology a comedy can carry.

Principal cast

Lindsay Lohan as Cady Heron; Rachel McAdams as Regina George; Lacey Chabert as Gretchen Wieners; Amanda Seyfried as Karen Smith; Lizzy Caplan as Janis Ian; Daniel Franzese as Damian Leigh; Jonathan Bennett as Aaron Samuels; and Tina Fey as Ms. Norbury.

From a parenting book to a field guide

Fey, then head writer of Saturday Night Live, read Wiseman's book — a work of social research, with no plot and no dialogue — and pitched producer Lorne Michaels on building a comedy inside its findings, drawing the characters' names and several situations from her own high school years in Pennsylvania. Waters, fresh from Freaky Friday with Lohan, directed with a discipline unusual for the genre: the comedy is played straight, the school's sociology shot like a nature documentary (Cady's narration and the animal-attack fantasy cutaways literalize the metaphor), and the adults are staffed from Fey's SNL circle — Meadows, Poehler, Ana Gasteyer, and Fey herself as the teacher whose trust-fall assembly converts the film's war into its peace talks. McAdams, cast against her Canadian ingenue type, built Regina's menace on stillness and smile; Seyfried, in her film debut, was nearly cast as Regina. Shot in Toronto, the film opened at number one and finished at $130.1 million — but its real gross was cultural: within a decade it had become the most quoted American comedy of its era, a status ratified by the Broadway musical and by the rare distinction of its own calendar day.

03 / NARRATIVE SUMMARY

Story Synopsis

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL FULL STORY SUMMARY

The film opens with the field naturalist's arrival: Cady, photographed by her parents on her first day like a released animal, learns the cafeteria's map at the cost of eating lunch in a bathroom stall, and is adopted by Janis and Damian — the school's self-exiled artists — who give her the map's legend (the Plastics: "they're fabulous, but they're evil") hours before the Plastics themselves issue the invitation that makes the map useless: sit with us, all week, starting now. The infiltration is Janis's design (her grievance dates to eighth grade, when Regina outed her as a Lebanese girl misheard as a lesbian, and the film files the feud's origin as a pronunciation) and the campaign's first phase is pure ethnography: the Plastics' constitution, unwritten and total — the dress code's rotating articles, the ex-boyfriend jurisprudence under which Aaron is Regina's permanent territory, the Burn Book produced like a family album — and Cady's reports, delivered to her handlers with a scientist's neutrality that does not survive her first taste of the throne's weather. The sabotage phase is the film's comic engine and its moral trap: the Kälteen bars fed to a weight-obsessed queen as a Swedish health secret; the face cream that is foot cream; the three-way calling attack that turns the clique's communications against itself; Aaron, alienated from Regina by her own infidelity, reported to Cady's heart like a liberated province — and Cady, mid-campaign, beginning to fail calculus deliberately to manufacture tutoring sessions, to speak in the clique's grammar without quotation marks, to throw the party (while her parents are away) at which she drunkenly confesses her sabotage-adjacent love to Aaron, vomits on his shoes, and is found by Janis on the lawn for the film's thesis exchange: "You are the thing you were sent to destroy" is not its wording, but it is its content — Janis's verdict, delivered with a friendship's full armament, is that Cady has become plastic, and Regina with worse hair is the review's headline.

The regime's collapse is Regina's own generalship: discovering the Kälteen fraud in a dress that no longer zips (the film's most economical revenge — she has been eating a thousand-calorie bar as a diet food for months), she retaliates with the Burn Book itself, inserting a page about herself to frame the frame, and delivers copies to Principal Duvall (Tim Meadows) and the halls in one motion — converting Girl World's cold war into a riot the faculty must suppress with an assembly. The assembly is the film's peace process: Ms. Norbury, herself a Burn Book entry ("drug pusher," in the book's economy of slander), runs an apology-and-trust-fall curriculum in the gymnasium whose comedy is real reconciliation conducted at scale, and Cady — summoned to Duvall's office with the book's other suspects — declines to name the authors and takes the punishment that is, by the film's accounting, fairly hers in part. The last movement is penance by calculus: Cady joins the Mathletes she had mocked ("social suicide," in the old regime's currency), apologizes down her list — to Janis, to her teachers, to Regina herself, in a hospital-room visit the film plays without irony — and at the state championship, facing the limit problem, solves her own case in the solving of it: the limit does not exist, and neither does the moral high ground she has been computing from. Regina's bus — stepped in front of, mid-rant, in the street outside school — is the film's most famous full stop: not Cady's doing, instantly believed to be, and the rumor's correction is part of her penance's price. The Spring Fling closes the books: Cady, crowned queen by the electorate's nostalgia, breaks the crown and distributes its pieces to the ranked and the rankers alike, dances with Aaron (the account settled with a kiss and no territory), and the narration files the peace: the Plastics disbanded into sports, weather, and other people's cliques — Girl World at peace, not because a better queen took the throne, but because the throne was melted down for parts.

The full narrative summary is an editorial paraphrase, not dialogue or a scene-by-scene transcript.

05 / CHARACTER DATABASE

Character Database

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Search the principal characters documented in this film. Roles and motivations below summarize their function in the 2004 movie.

THE NATURALIST

Cady Heron

Lindsay Lohan. A homeschooled zoologists' daughter — whose field training makes her the school's perfect ethnographer and, by the same gift, its most corruptible spy: she understands Girl World's rules so completely that mastery curdles into membership. Lohan plays her with a guileless precision that lets the audience watch the corruption happen in increments too small to object to, and Cady's function is the story's portrait of the infiltrator as the system's true product: the school does not defeat her cover; it promotes her out of it. The archive files her as the decade's finest screen transfer student: a naturalist who went native, and did the math to come back.

THE APEX PREDATOR

Regina George

Rachel McAdams. The Plastics' queen — a predator whose weapon is not cruelty but administration: she governs by compliment and exclusion in the same sentence, maintains the Burn Book as the regime's archive, and experiences her own sabotage (the bars, the cream, the boyfriend) as a series of weather events she cannot yet attribute. McAdams plays her with a smiling stillness that makes her rare rages genuinely frightening, and Regina's function is the story's portrait of the tyrant as a public utility: the school's girls do not merely fear her; they organize their self-worth by her exchange rate — which is why her fall must be meteorological (a bus) rather than electoral. The archive files her as the decade's definitive screen queen bee: an apex predator, retired by traffic, into lacrosse.

THE LIEUTENANT

Gretchen Wieners

Lacey Chabert. Regina's right hand — the Toaster Strudel heiress whose father invented the pastry and whose own invention, "fetch," is the film's saddest running campaign: a slang term she is trying to make happen, as she tries to make herself happen, inside a regime that pays her in proximity. Chabert plays her with an anxious, confessional sweetness (her secrets spill because keeping them is the only power she has been issued), and Gretchen's function is the story's portrait of the lieutenant as the regime's true believer: she is not evil; she is invested — and the film files her apology-assembly breakdown as the clique's most honest document. The archive files her as the decade's most complete screen second-in-command: a keeper of secrets, in a big house, with very little power in it.

THE WEATHERGIRL

Karen Smith

Amanda Seyfried. The Plastics' third member — the "dumbest girl you will ever meet," by her clique's own filing, whose gifts are real and meteorological: a fifth sense, by her account, in her breasts, for rain. Seyfried, in her film debut, plays her with a guileless, luminous vacancy that keeps her the film's one uncorrupted Plastic — her cruelty is entirely borrowed, worn like the clique's clothes, and returned without a mark on her. Karen's function is the story's portrait of the courtier as an innocent: she is the control group proving the regime corrupts by participation, not by nature, since she participates fully and is corrupted not at all. The archive files her as the decade's most consequential screen debut in a comedy: a weathergirl, correctly forecasting, in the wrong department.

THE EXILE

Janis Ian

Lizzy Caplan. The school's self-exiled artist — whose eighth-grade destruction by Regina (a rumor built on a mishearing) made her the regime's nemesis and the film's moral prosecutor: she designs the infiltration, and she delivers its verdict when the spy goes native. Caplan plays her with a sardonic, wounded intelligence that keeps the film honest — Janis's revenge is as much an obsession as Regina's rule is a tyranny, and the film files both — and Janis's function is the story's portrait of the resistance as a mirror with its own distortions: her cause is just, her methods are the enemy's, and her finest moment is forgiving the spy she created. The archive files her as the decade's finest screen exile: a prosecutor whose case was sound and whose conduct required its own apology.

THE CARTOGRAPHER

Damian Leigh

Daniel Franzese. Janis's partner and the film's chorus — "too gay to function," in the Burn Book's filing, which he has converted into a heraldic motto — whose cafeteria map, talent-show emceeing and running commentary make him Girl World's chief cartographer and its most reliable narrator after Cady herself. Franzese plays him with a warmth and comic timing that make him the audience's favorite citizen of the school, and Damian's function is the story's portrait of the exile as the community's memory: he knows where every table sits because he has been seated by the map himself. The archive files him as the decade's most essential screen cartographer: a mapmaker, drawn by the territory, with affection.

THE TERRITORY

Aaron Samuels

Jonathan Bennett. Regina's ex-boyfriend and Cady's crush — a decent, handsome senior whose function in the school's jurisprudence is as a disputed province: the ex-boyfriend rule makes him Regina's permanent territory in Plastics law, and the campaign's sabotage is largely conducted through his reassignment. Bennett plays him with an uncomplicated decency that is itself the film's point — he is not a prize but a person, and his refusal, at the party, of the drunken Cady's Plastic performance is the one honest border negotiation in the film. Aaron's function is the story's portrait of the territory as a citizen: the film files his Spring Fling settlement — a kiss, freely given, after the war — as Girl World's first lawful transfer. The archive files him as the decade's most consequential screen ex-boyfriend: a province, granted self-determination.

THE TEACHER

Ms. Sharon Norbury

Tina Fey. North Shore's math teacher and Mathletes moderator — a divorced, second-job-working adult whose authority the Burn Book tries to liquidate ("drug pusher") and whose response is the film's peace process: the assembly's apology curriculum and the maxim that governs it — "you all have got to stop calling each other sluts and whores; it just makes it okay for guys to call you sluts and whores." Fey plays her with a dry, tired decency that makes her the film's adult standard, and Norbury's function is the story's portrait of the teacher as the demilitarized zone's engineer: she cannot end Girl World; she can schedule its ceasefire and referee its apologies. The archive files her as the decade's finest screen teacher in a comedy: a moderator, of mathletes and of peace.

THE PRINCIPAL

Principal Ron Duvall

Tim Meadows. North Shore's principal — a former inner-city teacher whose calm is the product of worse schools than this one, and whose management of the Burn Book crisis (a baseball bat carried through a riot, an office tribunal conducted like a parole hearing) is the faculty's institutional ballast. Meadows plays him with a weary, deadpan authority that steals every scene it files, and Duvall's function is the story's portrait of the administrator as the peace's enforcement arm: Norbury designs the ceasefire; Duvall's bat and office make it stick. The archive files him as the decade's most complete screen principal: an enforcer whose weapon is mostly the suggestion of one.

THE COOL MOM

June George

Amy Poehler. Regina's mother — a tracksuit-era cautionary exhibit who offers her daughter's friends cocktails and asks, in the film's most quoted parental line, "what's the 411?" Poehler plays her with a desperate, hilarious youth-chasing energy, and June's function is the story's portrait of the regime's home front: Regina's administration was apprenticed at this kitchen island, where the mother competes with the daughter's generation instead of governing her own. The archive files her as the decade's most consequential minor mother in a comedy: a cool mom — not a regular mom — filed as an origin story.

THE MATHLETE CAPTAIN

Kevin Gnapoor

Rajiv Surendra. The Mathletes' captain — a rapper-scholar ("Kevin G") whose confidence operates at a frequency the Plastics' exchange cannot price, and whose state-championship team becomes Cady's penance and refuge. Surendra plays him with an unshakeable, joyful assurance, and Kevin's function is the story's portrait of the unranked as the school's free territory: the Mathletes are Girl World's Switzerland, where status is computed in correct answers, and his recruitment of Cady is the film's peace treaty in embryo. The archive files him as the decade's most essential screen mathlete: a captain whose league table cannot be Photoshopped.

THE COACH

Coach Carr

Dwayne Hill. North Shore's gym teacher and health instructor — whose abstinence curriculum ("don't have sex, because you will get pregnant and die") is the faculty's comic nadir and whose own Burn Book exposure (an alleged liaison with a student, in the book's economy) makes him the assembly crisis's most compromised adult. Hill plays him with a blustering, whistle-necklaced authority, and Carr's function is the story's portrait of the adult world as Girl World's senior division: the faculty's secrets are filed in the same book, by the same economy. The archive files him as the decade's most instructive minor coach in film: a health teacher, filed under his own curriculum's warnings.

The regime is a currency

The archive files the design as the story's durable economics: in Girl World, as elsewhere, regimes fall when the population stops accepting the notes.

The Book's function is the story's portrait of the archive as the regime's true seat of power: whoever holds the record holds the school — and its publication is the film's revolution, since a cruelty everyone can read is a cruelty everyone must answer for. Girl World — the rules of the ecology

The film's name for the school's social physics: a jurisdiction with its own jurisprudence (ex-boyfriend law), sumptuary codes (pink on Wednesdays, ponytails rationed, jeans confined to Fridays) and foreign policy (the three-way calling attack). Girl World's function is the story's portrait of the informal constitution as the binding one: no adult wrote these rules, no student can appeal them, and the film files their enforcement — by the governed, upon themselves — as the system's genius and its tragedy. The archive files Girl World as the decade's most completely codified fictional jurisdiction: a constitution, enforced at lunch, by its subjects.

The limit — penance by calculus

The state championship's final problem and the film's moral instrument: Cady, solving for the limit of a function, realizes the limit does not exist — and that her own computation (her worth, ranked against Regina's) is the same error. The limit's function is the story's portrait of mathematics as the one honest court in the school: it cannot be gossiped into a different answer, which is why the Mathletes are the peace's free territory. The archive files the limit as the decade's most consequential fictional equation: a nonexistent value, correctly identified, as a moral.

The apology assembly — the peace process

Ms. Norbury's gymnasium curriculum: public apologies and trust falls, administered to a rioting population — the film's peace conference, in which the governed confess their enforcements to each other and are caught, literally, by the girls behind them. The assembly's function is the story's portrait of reconciliation as a physical exercise: trust, in Girl World, is rebuilt by falling backward in public and being caught by your enemies. The archive files the assembly as the decade's most consequential fictional peace process: a ceasefire, conducted in a gymnasium, with falling.

10 / THEMATIC ANALYSIS

Themes & Analysis

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Going native — the spy becomes the subject

The film's governing theme is its infiltration's boomerang: that a cover identity, worn daily among people whose approval is the local weather, does not stay a cover — Cady's corruption is not a fall but an acclimatization, administered in compliments too small to refuse. The theme is worked out in the film's discipline of increments (she does not decide to become Plastic; she decides, successively, to skip a report, to enjoy a compliment, to fail a test on purpose, to exclude her friends from a party) until the audience holds the film's anthropology: systems do not recruit by argument; they recruit by seating — sit at the table long enough and the table's values are the price of the chair. Its proposition reaches every infiltrator in the audience — the undercover employee, the reformer inside the firm, the friend keeping one foot in a toxic circle to "influence" it: your handlers are watching for the lawn scene. The reach is the story's question to every Cady with a commission: who is reporting on you, and what would their last three reports say?

The regime is a currency — power backed by belief

The film's second theme is its economics: that Regina's power has no army, no law and no property — it is a fiat currency of regard, accepted because everyone accepts it, and therefore destructible by the classic instruments of a bank run: debasement (the sabotage), publication (the Burn Book), and a competing hard currency (the Mathletes' correct answers). The theme is worked out in the fall's speed (a regime of years collapses in an afternoon, because nothing was ever holding it but the population's portfolio decisions) until the audience holds the film's political science: most social tyrannies are monetary phenomena — audit what backs the notes you accept. Its proposition reaches every subject of an informal hierarchy in the audience — the office, the group chat, the scene: the queen's exchange rate is quoted daily, by you. The reach is the story's question to every Gretchen with a full head of secrets: what is your loyalty denominated in, and what is it worth on the open market?

Calling each other names — the governed enforce the regime

The film's third theme is Ms. Norbury's maxim: that Girl World's cruelty is not imposed by its queens but enforced, daily and voluntarily, by its population — girls calling each other the words that license everyone else to use them — and that the peace therefore cannot be a decapitation but must be a disarmament, conducted by the governed upon themselves. The theme is worked out in the assembly's design (no one is punished into the ceasefire; they confess and catch each other into it) until the audience holds the film's civics: a culture's cruelties survive its tyrants unless the enforcement class — everyone — retires. Its proposition reaches every population in the audience that polices itself in a regime's grammar: your Burn Book has your handwriting in it too. The reach is the story's question to every junior at the trust falls: which of the regime's words are you still issuing, and whose fall are you positioned to catch?

The limit does not exist — worth is not a ranking

The film's fourth theme is its calculus: that Cady's whole moral arithmetic — her worth computed as a function of Regina's disgrace, Aaron's preference, the table's seating — is a limit problem with no solution, and that the championship's answer is the film's: the value she is solving for does not exist, because people are not ranked on a single axis by anyone whose table matters. The theme is worked out in the crown's breaking (the ranking's physical symbol, distributed as souvenirs of the ranking's abolition) until the audience holds the film's mathematics of dignity: the peace is not Cady winning the ranking but the school, briefly, declining to compute it. Its proposition reaches every ranked person in the audience — by follower counts, seating charts, league tables of any kind: check whether the limit exists before you spend a school year approaching it. The reach is the story's question to every contestant at the board: what were you about to prove, and to whose cafeteria?

11 / FULL SPOILER CONCLUSION

Ending Explained

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL THE ENDING

Regina publishes the Burn Book and frames Cady's circle; after a school-wide riot and apology assembly, Cady takes her share of the blame, joins the Mathletes and wins the state championship. Regina is hit by a bus (an accident), survives, and channels her aggression into lacrosse. Cady is crowned Spring Fling Queen, breaks the crown and shares it with all the girls, and the Plastics peacefully disband.

What the ending establishes

  • The broken crown files the peace as an abolition rather than a succession: Girl World's throne is not transferred but melted down, at the ceremony, by its elected winner.
  • The Mathletes victory files penance as a practice rather than a performance: Cady's rehabilitation is computed in correct answers, in public, on a team she had priced as social death.
  • Regina's lacrosse files the predator's energy as re-deployable: the film's mercy is to give its tyrant a sanctioned venue for the same drives, rather than a redemption that denies them.
  • The bus files the regime's end as meteorological rather than moral: no one deposes Regina; traffic does — and the film lets the anticlimax stand as its verdict on how such reigns actually end.
  • The disbanded Plastics file the peace as an ecology rather than a treaty: each girl migrates to a habitat that prices her actual gifts — weather, sport, other people's courts.

What remains uncertain

Whether Girl World's peace survives the next school year and the next transfer student, whether "fetch" ever happens, and whether the junior class now rising has read the Burn Book as a warning or a manual — all stand open at the film's close (its stage musical and later film revisit the school with new cohorts). Within this film, the archive files the uncertainty as the story's own last word on its theme: peace at the watering hole is a daily practice, not a permanent settlement — but for one spring, nobody has to watch their back.

12 / PURPOSES OF THE STORY

Moral of The Story

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Mean Girls uses a homeschooled naturalist, a pink-bound archive and a state math championship to examine infiltration, currency and ranking: a spy promoted by her target into herself; a regime whose power is a belief the governed can withdraw; and a queen deposed not by a rival but by a photocopier, a bus, and a broken crown. Through the cafeteria, the Burn Book and the limit problem, the film argues that cover identities become real at the speed of seating; that social tyranny is a currency whose backing is the subjects' own enforcement; and that the worth a ranking computes does not exist — which is the most useful answer a championship ever produced.

Why tell this story?

Because Fey understood that Wiseman's sociology was a war story waiting for its field reporter, and that a comedy — played straight, narrated like a nature film — could smuggle a generation's social physics into the culture's permanent vocabulary. She told it with jokes because the jokes are the delivery system for the doctrine: nobody quotes a lecture for twenty years. The result is the decade's most durable high school film, the archive files: a field guide that its subjects still use to identify their own watering holes.

1. Watch the seating, not the speeches

The cafeteria map is the film's first counsel, for every newcomer to an organization — the school, the firm, the scene: the territory's true constitution is its seating chart, not its handbook, and your first week's intelligence should be gathered at lunch. The counsel is a field doctrine with the map as its legend: who sits where, who may move, whose table is exile and whose is court — this is the civics lesson every institution teaches daily and documents nowhere. Learn it before you are seated by it. And keep Damian's corollary in the margin: the greatest people you will ever meet are rarely at the table with the dress code.

2. Audit your cover identity quarterly

Cady's acclimatization is the film's second counsel, for every infiltrator, reformer and double agent of ordinary life — the employee changing the firm from inside, the friend staying in the circle to moderate it: the cover becomes the wearer at the speed of compliments, and the only known countermeasure is a handler with lawn privileges — someone licensed to tell you, in full sentences, that you have become the thing. The counsel is a cover doctrine with Janis as its standard: appoint your prosecutor before you need her, and schedule the reports. If no one in your life is authorized to say "you've become plastic," you are already seated.

3. Check what backs the currency

Regina's exchange rate is the film's third counsel, for every subject of an informal hierarchy — the office star, the scene's gatekeeper, the group's queen: ask what the authority is backed by (an archive? a skill? a belief?) because fiat regimes fall at photocopier speed the day the population audits the backing. The counsel is a monetary doctrine with the Burn Book as its reserve vault: power that lives in a scrapbook dies by publication. If you serve such a regime, price your loyalty accordingly. If you suffer under one, remember the governed are the backing — and a currency is only ever one afternoon of disbelief from the bus.

4. Keep a hard-currency skill

The Mathletes are the film's fourth counsel, for every person priced by a soft market — the ranked, the reviewed, the seated: maintain one competence whose value is computed rather than rumored — a proof, a craft, a measurable art — because it is the only currency that survives a bank run on your reputation. The counsel is a portfolio doctrine with the limit problem as its mint: correct answers cannot be Photoshopped, and a team that trades in them is a Switzerland in any school. Cady's penance works because it is denominated in this money. Acquire some before you need sanctuary; sanctuaries have admissions standards, and they are the point.

5. Apologize like a trust fall

The assembly is the film's fifth counsel, for every participant in a collective cruelty — the pile-on, the freeze-out, the group chat's cold war: the peace process that works is public, specific and physical in its structure — you say your part aloud, to the population, and then you fall backward and let the people you wronged demonstrate the new physics by catching you. The counsel is a reconciliation doctrine with the gymnasium as its chamber: private regret changes nothing in an ecology; the apology must be filed where the enforcement happened, in front of the enforcers. Schedule the assembly. Bring your own entry from the book.

6. Break the crown; distribute the pieces

The Spring Fling is the film's sixth counsel, its benediction, for every winner of a ranking they have come to see through — the crowned, the promoted, the anointed of any cafeteria: the highest use of a corrupt honor is its public abolition, at the ceremony, by its winner — the one person whose refusal the system cannot file as sour grapes. The counsel is a coronation doctrine with the plastic tiara as its instrument: Cady's pieces, distributed to the ranked and the rankers, are the peace's founding coinage. When the crown comes to you — and the electorate's nostalgia is undefeated — ask what it was minted to rank. Then break it where everyone can see the pieces are plastic.

The moral of the story is…

Learn the seating before the speeches; audit your cover before it sets; keep one skill whose value is computed, not rumored — and when they crown you, break the crown.

Mean Girls teaches that regimes of regard are currencies backed by their subjects' enforcement; that infiltration corrupts at the speed of seating; and that the limit of a person, ranked against another, does not exist.

The film's final question is therefore not whether you would survive Girl World — the film files that under "cafeteria." It is whose table you are seated at and what the chair is costing you; what your loyalty is denominated in; and whether, handed the crown at the ceremony, your hands would know to break it.
