01 / FILM IDENTITY

Overview

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

The Lion King is a 1994 American animated musical drama produced by Walt Disney Feature Animation, directed by Roger Allers and Rob Minkoff, with songs by Elton John and Tim Rice and a score by Hans Zimmer. Released June 24, 1994, it grossed $979 million worldwide including reissues ($763.5 million in its original run) on a $45 million budget — the highest-grossing film of 1994 and, at the time, the highest-grossing animated film ever made — won two Academy Awards (Best Original Score; Best Original Song, "Can You Feel the Love Tonight"), and was selected for the National Film Registry in 2016. It was the Disney Renaissance's B-team production — the studio's senior animators largely chose Pocahontas, expecting it to be the prestige hit — and it became instead the era's defining work, an original story (the studio's first animated feature not adapted from an existing tale) built from Hamlet, Bambi and the biblical lives of Joseph and Moses, and carried by the most confident opening sequence in animation: a sunrise, a chant in Zulu, and every animal in Africa converging on Pride Rock for the presentation of a cub. Simba (Jonathan Taylor Thomas as a cub, Matthew Broderick as an adult) is heir to Mufasa (James Earl Jones), the king whose philosophy — everything the light touches; a king's time as ruler rises and falls like the sun; we are all connected in the great circle of life — is the film's constitution, delivered in dawn walks above the kingdom. The threat is inside the family: Scar (Jeremy Irons, in a performance of languid, ironical villainy that set the Renaissance's standard), the king's brother, second in line until the cub's birth moved him down it ("I shall practice my curtsy"), who allies with the hyenas of the shadowy place — Shenzi, Banzai and Ed (Whoopi Goldberg, Cheech Marin, Jim Cummings) — the Pride Lands' excluded underclass, promised hunting rights in exchange for a coup. The coup is the film's wound: a wildebeest stampede engineered in a gorge, Mufasa killed saving his son, and the boy persuaded by his uncle that the death is his fault — "run away, Simba, and never return" — an exile the film treats with complete seriousness as a child's false guilt. The exile is the film's second philosophy: Timon and Pumbaa (Nathan Lane and Ernie Sabella), a meerkat and a warthog living under the doctrine of Hakuna Matata — no worries, no responsibility, the past at a safe distance ("when the world turns its back on you, you turn your back on the world") — raise Simba into a carefree adulthood that the film loves and judges simultaneously. The return is forced by the kingdom's collapse and carried by two emissaries: Nala (Moira Kelly), the childhood friend turned huntress, who reports the Pride Lands starving under Scar's misrule; and Rafiki (Robert Guillaume), the mandrill shaman, who leads Simba to the film's thesis in a night of stars — Mufasa's ghost in the clouds ("you are more than what you have become… remember who you are"), and a lesson in stick-craft: the past can hurt, but you can either run from it or learn from it. The climax — Simba's duel with Scar on a burning Pride Rock, the truth extracted mid-fight ("I killed Mufasa"), Scar's own hyenas turning on him when he betrays them in turn — restores the circle: rain, regrowth, and a new cub presented on the Rock, the film's first image repeated as its last argument. Its stage adaptation became one of the highest-grossing entertainments in history; its songs became the decade's lullabies; and its subject — a son's debt to a dead father, and the difference between forgetting the past and learning from it — has made it the Disney film adults most often report crying at, in public, without embarrassment.

Place in the culture

The film crowned the Disney Renaissance and became its most durable property: the Broadway adaptation (1997, Julie Taymor) redefined stage spectacle and out-grossed nearly everything in theatre history; the soundtrack was the best-selling of its year; and a generation of children learned the words "Hakuna Matata" before they learned the philosophy was the film's problem, not its solution — a misreading the culture has been happily correcting ever since.

The B-picture that ate the studio

The production's mythology is the Renaissance's favorite irony: developed under the title King of the Jungle, reshaped from a nature documentary concept into a musical after original director George Scribner's departure, and staffed largely by animators the studio considered its second string (the first string chose Pocahontas), the film was expected to be a modest performer. The crew researched in Kenya's Hell's Gate National Park; the wildebeest stampede — a then-novel computer-assisted sequence of individually animated animals — took some three years to realize; and Jeremy Irons's vocal performance, recorded after he had injured his voice, gave Scar his ruined grandeur. Elton John, initially baffled to be scoring an animated film about lions, delivered five songs, three of them Oscar-nominated; Zimmer's score, built on Lebo M's Zulu chants, won the Oscar and redefined the sound of the animated epic.

03 / NARRATIVE SUMMARY

Story Synopsis

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL FULL STORY SUMMARY

The film's first movement is an education and a trap. Simba's dawn lessons establish the kingdom's constitution — the circle of life, in which even lions become grass — while his uncle's coaching ("I Just Can't Wait to Be King," the cub's anthem of appetite) steers him toward the elephant graveyard and the hyenas, a first assassination attempt interrupted by Mufasa, whose rebuke to his son is the film's first piece of moral precision: being brave doesn't mean you go looking for trouble. Scar's second attempt is the gorge: Simba placed in the stampede's path as bait, Mufasa killed in the rescue (thrown back by Scar's claws at the cliff's edge, in the film's most traumatic image), and the boy's guilt manufactured in a single scene of whispered counsel — the uncle convincing the nephew that he is a murderer, and advising flight. The hyenas' pursuit, the thorn thicket, and the desert crossing deliver Simba to the film's second philosophy at the moment of his collapse.

The exile chapters are constructed as a genuine alternative, not a punishment: Timon and Pumbaa's oasis is the film's warmest place, their doctrine a coherent response to a world that has hurt them both (Pumbaa's flatulence, Timon's exile from the colony), and Simba's growth to adulthood inside it — "Hakuna Matata" as a montage of a life successfully not lived — is the film's most seductive sequence, which is the point. The return movement therefore has to defeat not Scar but the doctrine: Nala's arrival (and the love song that reopens the past), Rafiki's stick, and the ghost in the clouds successively argue that the exile's peace is purchased with the kingdom's starvation — the Pride Lands under Scar and the hyenas have been hunted to desert, in the film's ecological bookkeeping — and that the past Simba is fleeing is not the one that happened. The duel on Pride Rock closes both accounts at once: Scar's confession, extracted when he mistakes Simba for Mufasa in the firelight and then boasts of the truth, is heard by the pride; his fall and his hyenas' revenge close his; and the rain that breaks over the burning rock files the circle's restoration as weather.

The full narrative summary is an editorial paraphrase, not dialogue or a scene-by-scene transcript.

THE KING

Simba (Adult)

Voice of Matthew Broderick. The returned prince — raised in exile on a doctrine of forgetting, recalled by love, shame and a ghost to a kingdom his absence has starved. Broderick voices him with a gentle, unheroic decency that makes Simba's kingship an act of repair rather than conquest, and his function is the story's portrait of the heir as a debtor: his reign begins as an apology, which the film files as the only honest way reigns begin. The archive files him as the decade's most consequential king in film: a place in the circle, resumed, at the price of the past.

THE CUB

Simba (Young)

Voice of Jonathan Taylor Thomas. The heir as a child — appetite, bravado and a voice that wants the crown for its privileges, educated in a single morning by the gorge. Thomas voices him with a bright, heedless charm, and young Simba's function is the story's portrait of the child as the coup's true instrument: Scar's plot works because it is aimed at a boy who can be made to blame himself, and the film's tenderness toward that boy is its moral signature. The archive files him as the decade's most consequential cub in film: a roar, practiced, in an empty gorge.

THE FATHER

Mufasa

Voice of James Earl Jones. The king — whose lessons (the circle, the stars, the difference between bravery and trouble) constitute the film's constitution, and whose murder, a third of the way in, governs everything after it. Jones voices him with the century's most authoritative warmth, and Mufasa's function is the story's portrait of the father as a posthumous jurisdiction: he is the film's most powerful character precisely because he is absent — a voice in the clouds and a doctrine in his son's conscience. The archive files him as the decade's most consequential father in film: a place among the stars, kept, for a son who looks up.

THE USURPER

Scar

Voice of Jeremy Irons. The king's brother — the throne's disappointed intellect, whose grievance (second place, forever) curdles into regicide and whose reign converts the kingdom's plenty into desert within a few years. Irons voices him with a silken, contemptuous languor that made Scar the Renaissance's definitive villain, and his function is the story's portrait of resentment as a governing philosophy: he does not want the kingdom's good; he wants its chair, and the film's ecology grades the difference in drought. The archive files him as the decade's most consequential usurper in film: a confession, extracted, at the cliff's edge, twice.

THE HUNTRESS

Nala

Voice of Moira Kelly (adult); Niketa Calame (young). Simba's childhood friend and the pride's emissary — who leaves the starving kingdom to find help and finds, instead, its king, living on grubs under an assumed philosophy. Kelly voices her with a direct, unsentimental strength, and Nala's function is the story's portrait of the witness as the return's first cause: before the ghost and the shaman, it is her report — the kingdom is dying and you are alive — that makes exile indefensible. The archive files her as the decade's most consequential huntress in film: a message, delivered, at the end of a pounce.

THE MEERKAT

Timon

Voice of Nathan Lane. A meerkat exile and the doctrine of Hakuna Matata's senior author — a fast-talking survivor whose philosophy is a scar dressed as a slogan. Lane voices him with a Broadway engine of wisecracks and evasions, and Timon's function is the story's portrait of the comic as the exile's theologian: his doctrine genuinely saved Simba's life and genuinely must be outgrown, and the film honors both by letting Timon, at the end, fight for the kingdom he taught his friend to abandon. The archive files him as the decade's most consequential meerkat in film: a philosophy, retired, into a hula distraction.

THE WARTHOG

Pumbaa

Voice of Ernie Sabella. A warthog whose social exile (for reasons of atmosphere) made him the doctrine's gentlest adherent and the film's warmest heart. Sabella voices him with a big, unguarded sweetness, and Pumbaa's function is the story's portrait of kindness as the exile's true curriculum: what actually raises Simba in the oasis is not the philosophy but the friendship, which is why the philosophy can be discarded at the end and the friends cannot. The archive files him as the decade's most consequential warthog in film: a charge, delivered, at the words "they call me Mr. Pig."

THE SHAMAN

Rafiki

Voice of Robert Guillaume. The Pride Lands' mandrill priest — keeper of the tree, painter of cubs, and the film's licensed eccentric, whose stick delivers its thesis: the past can hurt, but you can run from it or learn from it. Guillaume voices him with a delighted, cracked wisdom, and Rafiki's function is the story's portrait of the spiritual guide as a practical man: his mysticism is all in the service of one intervention — getting a king up a hill on a rainy night. The archive files him as the decade's most consequential shaman in film: a stick, applied, exactly where it hurts.

THE MAJORDOMO

Zazu

Voice of Rowan Atkinson. The king's hornbill steward — a punctilious, terrified civil servant whose morning reports are the kingdom's comic liturgy and whose captivity under Scar is its saddest running joke. Atkinson voices him with a clipped, exasperated precision, and Zazu's function is the story's portrait of administration as a form of loyalty: he serves the throne under both kings, and the film files his service under Scar as the bureaucrat's tragedy — the reports continue; the kingdom starves. The archive files him as the decade's most consequential majordomo in film: a morning report, delivered, to whoever holds the rock.

THE QUEEN

Sarabi

Voice of Madge Sinclair. Mufasa's queen and Simba's mother — who endures Scar's reign as the pride's internal resistance, and whose public defiance ("if you were half the king Mufasa was") triggers the climax's confession. Sinclair, in her final film role, voices her with a grave, unbroken dignity, and Sarabi's function is the story's portrait of the keeper of the standard: she is the living measure against which both kings are graded, and her survival is the kingdom's continuity made flesh. The archive files her as the decade's most consequential queen in film: a standard, held, through the drought.

THE TRIO

Shenzi, Banzai & Ed

Voices of Whoopi Goldberg, Cheech Marin, and Jim Cummings. The hyena leadership — the shadowy place's excluded, whose bargain with Scar (hunting rights for a coup) is the film's class politics in miniature, and whose final turn on their patron, when he betrays them mid-duel, is its judgment on bargains with resentment. The Trio's function is the story's portrait of the underclass as an instrument that reads its contract: they were promised a kingdom's table and fed its scraps, and their revenge is filed by the film as arithmetic, not evil. The archive files them as the decade's most consequential trio in film: a promise, collected, in the fire.

THE ROCK

Pride Rock

The kingdom's throne, altar and weather station — the promontory where cubs are presented, kings address the dawn, and the film's first and last images are staged. Pride Rock's function is the story's portrait of the seat as a sacrament rather than a prize: Scar possesses it for years and it confers nothing on him, because the rock's authority is the circle's, and the circle is not his. The archive files Pride Rock as the decade's most consequential fictional seat in film: a presentation, repeated, as the credits of a restored world.

The archive files this Pair as the decade's most consequential fictional landscapes in film: a wound and a hammock, both required.

08 / THE CIRCLE OF LIFE, PRIDE ROCK & HAKUNA MATATA

The Circle of Life, Pride Rock & Hakuna Matata

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

The circle of life — the constitution

Mufasa's doctrine: everything is connected; kings become grass; the antelope eat the grass; balance is the whole of the law. The circle's function is the story's portrait of a political philosophy complete in one dawn walk: it gives the monarchy a limit (the king is a link, not an owner) and the subject a dignity (every death feeds the kingdom). The archive files the circle as the decade's most consequential fictional doctrine in film: a constitution, sung, over the opening credits.

Hakuna Matata — the counter-doctrine

Timon and Pumbaa's philosophy: no worries, no responsibility, the past put behind you — a problem-free philosophy for a life in exile. The doctrine's function is the story's portrait of forgetting as a technology with a service life: it is true medicine for a traumatized child and a slow poison for a king, and the film's maturity is its refusal to file it as either one alone. The archive files Hakuna Matata as the decade's most consequential fictional philosophy in film: a slogan, outgrown, with honors.

The kings in the stars — the afterlife clause

Mufasa's promise: the great kings of the past look down from the stars, and will be there, in the dark, when the heir feels alone. The clause's function is the story's portrait of grief given an address: it converts a father's death from an absence into a jurisdiction, and its payoff — the voice in the clouds — is the film's one miracle, staged as weather and memory rather than magic. The archive files the stars as the decade's most consequential fictional afterlife in film: a promise, kept, in cumulus.

The presentation — the succession rite

The raising of the cub on Pride Rock before the assembled kingdom: the film's first image and its last, the rite by which the circle announces its continuation. The rite's function is the story's portrait of succession as a public sacrament: the kingdom does not merely get a king; it witnesses one, and the witnessing is the legitimacy. The archive files the presentation as the decade's most consequential fictional rite in film: a cub, raised, twice — the second time as the argument.

10 / THEMATIC ANALYSIS

Themes & Analysis

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Remember who you are — identity as an inheritance

The film's governing theme is its ghost's single instruction: Simba's crisis is not that he lacks a self but that he has adopted one — the exile's — to avoid a debt, and the cure is not self-invention but recollection. The theme is worked out in the film's two philosophies (the circle assigns a place; Hakuna Matata declines one) until the audience holds its doctrine of the resumed name: some identities are not chosen but owed, and maturity is the moment the owing outranks the comfort. Its proposition reaches every adult in the audience living at a careful distance from an origin. The reach is the story's question to every exile in an oasis: whose voice, in which clouds, are you currently declining to hear — and what is the grass doing, back home, in your absence?

The past can hurt — run from it or learn from it

The film's second theme is Rafiki's stick: the past is not denied, outrun or therapized away; it is struck, once, and the pain is the tuition. The theme is worked out in Simba's two responses to the same blow (the cub flees it for a decade; the adult stands for the second strike and laughs) until the audience holds its doctrine of the educated wound: learning from the past is not revisiting it for blame — Simba's guilt was false — but for fact, and the facts, once faced, are what set the itinerary home. Its proposition reaches every keeper of a gorge in the audience. The reach is the story's question to every runner with a stick-mark: what is the one true fact inside your false guilt — the fact you could act on, this week, at walking pace?

False guilt — the child's manufactured confession

The film's third theme is its most serious: a child is persuaded by a trusted adult that he caused his father's death, and organizes a decade of exile around the persuasion. The theme is worked out with unusual care — the film never treats Simba's guilt as a misunderstanding he should have outgrown, but as an installed program requiring an external correction (a witness, a ghost, a confession) to uninstall — until the audience holds its doctrine of the audited conscience: some guilts are evidence and some are inheritances from interested parties, and telling them apart is not a solo exercise. Its proposition reaches every adult in the audience carrying a gorge of their own. The reach is the story's question to every exile by whisper: who benefited from your confession — and have you ever once checked it against a witness?

The circle — governance as ecology

The film's fourth theme is its constitution's political science: a reign is legible in grass and rain, because a ruler's appetites propagate through a system that keeps perfect accounts. The theme is worked out in the two reigns' weathers (plenty, drought, restoration) until the audience holds its doctrine of the readable kingdom: leadership is not finally a matter of proclamations but of watersheds — the state of the commons, some years in, is the audit of the throne. Its proposition reaches every steward of a commons in the audience. The reach is the story's question to every king of a small rock: what is your grass doing — and how long have you been explaining it instead of reading it?

11 / FULL SPOILER CONCLUSION

Ending Explained

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL THE ENDING

Simba confronts Scar on Pride Rock; Scar, mistaking him for Mufasa and then cornered, confesses before the pride that he killed Mufasa. In the ensuing battle Scar is thrown from the rock and, attempting to blame the hyenas, is killed by them. Rain restores the Pride Lands, and Simba and Nala's cub is presented on Pride Rock as the film's opening is repeated.

What the ending establishes

  • The confession files the reign's foundation as its solvent: the lie that built Scar's kingdom dissolves it in one public sentence.
  • The hyenas' turn files the grievance coalition's contract as enforced against its maker: resentment, underfed, collects from the patron.
  • Simba's mercy — he spares Scar at the edge and orders exile, his father's sentence inverted — files the new reign's first act as the refusal of the old one's methods.
  • The rain files governance as weather: the circle's restoration is announced by precipitation, not proclamation.
  • The presentation files succession as the film's closing argument: the first image, repeated, is the doctrine — the circle continues.

What remains uncertain

Whether the hyenas' exclusion — the kingdom's founding injustice — is addressed by the new reign; how long the oasis doctrine's graduates serve in a restored court; and what the next drought will ask of Simba's line — all stand open at the film's close (the sequels and stage version audited several of these accounts). Within this film, the archive files the uncertainty as the story's own last word on its theme: the circle turns, and each generation's king must read the grass for himself.

12 / PURPOSES OF THE STORY

Moral of The Story

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

The Lion King uses a prince's exile and return to examine guilt, inheritance and rule: a child carrying a manufactured confession for a decade; a philosophy of no worries that saves his life and must still be outgrown; and a kingdom whose grass keeps the only honest accounts of its kings. Through the gorge, the oasis and the burning rock, the film argues that identity is an inheritance to be resumed, not a comfort to be chosen; that the past can be run from or learned from, at identical prices in pain; and that a reign — of a kingdom, a company, a household — is audited in the condition of its commons.

Why tell this story?

Because Disney's second-string team reached for the oldest plot in the cupboard — the prince, the usurper, the ghost — and discovered it was also the newest: a story about a generation's sons and their dead fathers, told at the exact moment that generation was taking the rock. The result became the Renaissance's crown, the archive files: Hamlet, restaged, for everyone who has ever been eight and everyone who has ever been a father.

1. Audit your oldest guilt

Simba's confession is the film's first counsel, for every adult still serving a sentence installed in childhood — the family story about whose fault it was, absorbed before the age of evidence: the film's doctrine is that such guilts are programs, and programs have authors, and authors have interests. The counsel is an audit doctrine with the gorge as its standard: take your oldest guilt to one living witness — a sibling, a record, a grave-side conversation — and check it against a source that was actually there. Simba needed a huntress, a shaman and a ghost. Start with the witness; the ghost keeps office hours for those who have done the legwork.

2. Let the stick land twice

Rafiki's lesson is the film's second counsel, for every practitioner of a well-maintained avoidance — the unopened letters, the unvisited town, the unspoken name: the film's physics are exact; the second blow hurts the same and teaches more, because the student is standing still for it. The counsel is a tuition doctrine with the laugh as its standard: choose one artifact of the past you are running from — one gorge, revisited at walking pace, in company — and let it hit you with its facts, which are almost always smaller than its shadow. The pain is the same either way. Only one direction pays it off.

3. Honor the oasis; don't retire there

Hakuna Matata is the film's third counsel, for every keeper of a recovery philosophy — the sabbatical doctrine, the boundaries creed, the season of no: the film's maturity is its double verdict; the doctrine saved a life and would have wasted a reign, and the difference is a date. The counsel is a service-life doctrine with the hula as its standard: write your philosophy's review date into the philosophy — the season at which the medicine is re-examined against the kingdom's grass — and let the friends who carried you be honored in the next chapter rather than filed as the phase you're past. Timon fights in the finale. Your Timon should be invited to yours.

4. Read your grass

The circle is the film's fourth counsel, for every steward of a commons — the manager, the parent, the chair of anything: the film's political science is meteorological, and its counsel is an audit doctrine with the drought as its standard: once a year, inspect the condition of the thing itself — the team's energy, the household's weather, the land's actual grass — before you read any report about it, including your own proclamations. Scar's reign issued excellent announcements throughout the famine. The grass was the minutes of the meeting. Read the grass.

5. Keep the standard like Sarabi

The queen's endurance is the film's fifth counsel, for everyone who must live through a bad reign — in a company, a country, a family: Sarabi's resistance is not sabotage but measurement; she holds the old standard in public, in one sentence, at the moment it can do the most work. The counsel is a standard doctrine with the defiance as its standard: in a drought you did not cause, your office is to remember, accurately and aloud, what the place was like when it was governed well — neither nostalgic nor silent — so that the restoration, when it comes, has a specification to build to. Kingdoms are restored by the people who kept the minutes of the good years.

6. Present the cub

The presentation is the film's sixth counsel, its benediction, for every founder of a next generation — the parent, the mentor, the retiring king of any rock: the film ends where it began because its doctrine of succession is a rite, performed in public, in which the community witnesses its own continuation. The counsel is a succession doctrine with the rock as its standard: whatever your kingdom, stage its presentation — the introduction, the raising-up, the public act by which the next bearer is shown to the assembled — because legitimacy is a witnessed thing, and the witnessing is your last gift to both the cub and the crowd. Then step to your place among the stars, and let the rain come.

The moral of the story is…

Audit your oldest guilt; let the stick land twice; honor the oasis, don't retire there — and read your grass.

The Lion King teaches that identity is an inheritance to be resumed; that the past can be run from or learned from at the same price in pain; and that every reign is audited, in the end, by the condition of its commons.

The film's final question is therefore not whether you are the king — the film files that under "the light touches what it touches." It is whose whisper installed your exile; what your grass is doing; and whether, when the voice comes out of the clouds, you will already be walking home.
