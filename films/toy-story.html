01 / FILM IDENTITY

Overview

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Toy Story is a 1995 American animated adventure comedy produced by Pixar Animation Studios for Walt Disney Pictures, directed by John Lasseter. Released November 22, 1995, it grossed $401.2 million worldwide including reissues ($373.6 million in its original run) on a $30 million budget — the second-highest-grossing film of 1995 — received three Academy Award nominations (including the first Best Original Screenplay nomination ever given an animated film) plus a Special Achievement Oscar for Lasseter, and was inducted into the National Film Registry in 2005, its first year of eligibility. It is, before anything else, a hinge in cinema history: the first feature film animated entirely by computer, made by a small technology company whose founder, Steve Jobs, had bought it from Lucasfilm — and its makers understood that the technology would only matter if the story was older than the medium. So they built it on the oldest story there is between two men: the favorite, displaced. In a world where toys live full emotional lives the moment humans leave the room, Woody (Tom Hanks), a pull-string cowboy doll, is the beloved sheriff of Andy Davis's bedroom — the favorite, the leader, the name written in marker on a boot. Andy's birthday party brings the threat every favorite dreads: Buzz Lightyear (Tim Allen), a space-ranger action figure with lights, sounds, a karate-chop action and one catastrophic distinction — Buzz does not know he is a toy. He believes he is a real Space Ranger, marooned on a strange planet, and his delusion is played by the film with perfect straightness, which makes him simultaneously the comedy's engine and its tragedy: Woody's jealousy, escalating from sabotage (a rescue mission for a toy behind the desk that knocks Buzz out the window instead) to catastrophe, strands both of them at a gas station, delivered by a Pizza Planet truck into the hands of Sid Phillips, the toy-torturing neighbor kid whose bedroom is a gulag of mutilated mutant toys. The film's middle is an escape story with a theology: in Sid's room, Woody discovers that the mutants are not monsters but surgeons and rescuers; Buzz, watching his own television commercial — a warehouse of identical Buzzes, "you are a toy!" — suffers the decade's most efficient identity collapse, is briefly Mrs. Nesbitt at a deranged tea party, and is strapped by Sid to a rocket, "The Big One," for a dawn execution. Woody's redemption is a conspiracy of the discarded: he organizes Sid's own victims into a jailbreak that breaks the toys' first law — they move, and speak, in front of a human — to traumatize Sid out of toy-torture forever ("we toys can see everything… so play nice"), and the finale, a moving-day chase involving a rocket, a match Woody cannot keep lit, a magnified sunbeam through Buzz's helmet, and RC's remote control car, ends with Buzz's reinvention of his own false promise: he cannot fly — but he can fall, with style, gliding on plastic wings into Andy's car and into the friendship that redefines them both. The film's final beat is its thesis in miniature: Christmas at the new house, Woody and Buzz together, nervous about the new arrivals exactly as before — "what could Andy possibly get that is worse than you?" — and then a puppy's bark, and two veterans of displacement grinning at the sequel of their own anxiety. Beneath the buddy comedy runs the film's real subject, which is why it has outlived its technological novelty entirely: what a person is for when their one job — being loved first — is taken by someone newer, and whether worth survives the loss of rank. The answer it files, in marker, on two boots, is that Andy writes both names.

Place in the culture

The film founded modern computer animation and the Pixar era — the studio's run of original classics begins here — while its screenplay's nomination announced that animation could compete as writing, not merely as novelty. Its sequels, including Toy Story 2 (1999), are archived elsewhere in this Galaxy; its catchphrases ("to infinity and beyond," "there's a snake in my boot") and Randy Newman's songs entered the permanent furniture of childhood on several continents.

The near-death of the first CGI feature

The production nearly died of its own first draft: Disney, wanting edge, pushed early versions in which Woody was a sarcastic tyrant, and a disastrous story-reel screening in November 1993 — remembered at Pixar as "Black Friday" — led Disney to halt production and prepare to close the feature unit. Given one chance, Lasseter's team rewrote the film in weeks around the principle that would define Pixar: Woody's flaw would be a virtue in excess — love of his kid, fear of losing him — and the film's emotion, not its edge, would be its distinction. Rendered on a farm of workstations at a then-unimaginable scale (each frame taking hours; the film containing over 1,500 shots), the finished work premiered to a standing ovation and reviews that reached for the word "landmark" without embarrassment. Newman's "You've Got a Friend in Me" was Oscar-nominated, as were the screenplay and score.

03 / NARRATIVE SUMMARY

Story Synopsis

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL FULL STORY SUMMARY

The film's structure is a buddy picture built as a pilgrimage home. Act one establishes the bedroom's constitution — Woody's benevolent sheriffdom, the toys' liturgy of staff meetings and reconnaissance (the Green Army Men at the birthday party, walkie-talkie in a potted plant, in the film's funniest set piece of military procedure at one-twelfth scale) — and introduces the usurper as a man who cannot be argued with, because his error is total: Buzz surveys Andy's room as an alien planet, files Woody as a friendly native, and treats his own toy features as classified Star Command technology. Woody's sabotage misfires into apparent murder — Buzz out the window, the room in revolt, Woody's guilt established in the eyes of every toy he leads — and the film's moral engine is set: the favorite's fall is not the arrival of Buzz but the act of jealousy itself, a distinction the film maintains with unusual rigor. Stranded together at the Dinotrux station and delivered to Sid's house in a claw machine's catch (the little green Aliens, worshipping The Claw, in the film's smallest and most perfect theology), the rivals are filed in the story's true location: not the villain's lair but the discarded's ward.

Sid's room is where the film's argument completes itself. The mutant toys — a doll's head on an erector-set spider, a fishing rod with doll's legs, a jack-in-the-box hand — are introduced as horror and revealed as the film's moral aristocracy: toys whom the world has broken and reassembled, who repair each other (they reattach Buzz's arm without comment) and who owe their solidarity to their damage. Buzz's collapse and recovery are the film's twin poles: the commercial that tells him what he is, and Woody's counter-testimony — the finest speech in the Pixar canon, delivered under a cardboard box — that being a toy is not the consolation prize for not being a Space Ranger but a greater office: "You are a toy — and you are going to be loved by a kid for a very long time." The jailbreak, the rocket, the chase and the glide resolve the plot, but the film's last piece of business is its signature: Andy, arriving at the new house, finds both names in his hands at once, and the favorite's chair turns out to have been a bench all along.

The full narrative summary is an editorial paraphrase, not dialogue or a scene-by-scene transcript.

THE SHERIFF

Woody

Voice of Tom Hanks. A pull-string cowboy doll — Andy's favorite since kindergarten, leader of the bedroom's toys, and the film's study in what rank does to a good man when the rank is threatened. Hanks voices him with a decency that survives his worst act, and Woody's function is the story's portrait of the displaced favorite: his jealousy is not vanity but terror of uselessness, and his redemption is not recovering first place but discovering the place was never the point. The archive files him as the decade's most consequential sheriff in film: a name in marker, joined, by a second name.

THE RANGER

Buzz Lightyear

Voice of Tim Allen. A Space Ranger action figure who believes, entirely, that he is a Space Ranger — with a mission log, a damaged ship and a nemesis named Zurg — until a television commercial files his true ontology in thirty seconds. Allen voices him with a magnificent, unironic conviction that makes the delusion heroic before it makes it funny, and Buzz's function is the story's portrait of identity as a story that can break: his recovery — falling, with style — is the film's doctrine that a self rebuilt on the truth outperforms the fiction. The archive files him as the decade's most consequential ranger in film: to infinity, revised, to the moving truck and beyond.

THE SKEPTIC

Mr. Potato Head

Voice of Don Rickles. A detachable-featured spud — the bedroom's licensed insult comic and Woody's chief prosecutor, whose parts literally fall off in indignation and whose suspicions of the sheriff drive the room's revolt. Rickles voices him with a lifetime's timing in every syllable, and Potato Head's function is the story's portrait of the loyal opposition: a community's health is measured by whether its Rickles is allowed to accuse its sheriff in open session. The archive files him as the decade's most consequential skeptic in film: an ear, misplaced, in the interests of justice.

THE LOYALIST

Slinky Dog

Voice of Jim Varney. A dachshund with a coil for a body — Woody's oldest friend and the room's conscience of kindness, torn (in one scene, literally, between floors) by his loyalty to a leader he believes has done wrong. Varney voices him with a down-home gentleness, and Slink's function is the story's portrait of friendship as the film's court of equity: he cannot acquit Woody, and he cannot stop loving him, and the film files both facts without resolving them. The archive files him as the decade's most consequential loyalist in film: a spring, stretched, between duty and love.

THE NEUROTIC

Rex

Voice of Wallace Shawn. A plastic Tyrannosaurus with a crippling anxiety about his own inadequacy — worried his roar isn't scary, his arms are too short, and his place in the room is a clerical error. Shawn voices him with an intellectual's tremble in a predator's body, and Rex's function is the story's portrait of the imposter in every company: the mightiest-looking member of the firm, privately certain he's about to be exposed. The archive files him as the decade's most consequential neurotic in film: a roar, rehearsed, at a mirror.

THE BANKER

Hamm

Voice of John Ratzenberger. A piggy bank of comfortable cynicism — the room's channel-surfer and odds-maker, who narrates crises from the bed with the detachment of a man whose job is storage. Ratzenberger (in the first of his every-Pixar-film appearances) voices him with a barroom dryness, and Hamm's function is the story's portrait of the institution's memory: he has seen favorites come and go and files the current emergency accordingly — as weather. The archive files him as the decade's most consequential banker in film: a cork, unmoved, in a crisis.

THE SHEPHERDESS

Bo Peep

Voice of Annie Potts. A porcelain lamp-figure — the room's calm center and Woody's confidante, the one toy whose faith in him never requires evidence and whose counsel ("Andy will find a way to put us both on his 'must-keep' list") is the film's thesis spoken early and quietly. Potts voices her with a warmth that anchors the bedroom's emotional register, and Bo's function is the story's portrait of love as the room's supreme court: her verdict on Woody precedes and outlasts the prosecution's. The archive files her as the decade's most consequential shepherdess in film: a crook, raised, in one man's defense.

THE KID

Andy Davis

Voice of John Morris. A boy of six, moving house — the bedroom's god, whose love is the film's entire economy and whose name, written on two boots, is its constitution. Morris voices him in a handful of scenes of total, unselfconscious devotion, and Andy's function is the story's portrait of the beloved as the toys understand him: not a judge ranking them but a player whose play is their purpose. The archive files him as the decade's most consequential kid in film: a marker, applied, to two soles.

THE TORMENTOR

Sid Phillips

Voice of Erik von Detten. The neighbor kid — a toy-torturer with a skull t-shirt, a magnifying glass and a bedroom laboratory of mutilation, who serves as the film's villain and, in the jailbreak, its most thoroughly educated man. Von Detten voices him with a gleeful, suburban menace, and Sid's function is the story's portrait of cruelty as ignorance with tools: he does not know the toys are alive, which the film files as his excuse and his sentence in the same scene. The archive files him as the decade's most consequential tormentor in film: a childhood, reformed, by a talking doll at midnight.

THE SERGEANT

Sergeant

Voice of R. Lee Ermey. The commander of Andy's Green Army Men — a bucket of soldiers who conduct birthday reconnaissance with the full procedure of a special-forces unit, at one-twelfth scale. Ermey, the screen's definitive drill instructor, voices him dead straight, and the Sergeant's function is the story's portrait of professionalism as scale-independent: the film's funniest and most touching claim is that duty performed completely, however small the theater, is indistinguishable from heroism. The archive files him as the decade's most consequential sergeant in film: a potted plant, held, against all birthdays.

THE WORSHIPPERS

The Aliens

Little green squeeze-toys from the Pizza Planet claw machine — a congregation of serene, identical devotees of The Claw, which chooses who will go and who will stay. The Aliens' function is the story's portrait of faith at its most cheerful and least examined: their theology is three words ("the Claw") and a posture of gratitude, and the film loves them without a trace of mockery. The archive files them as the decade's most consequential worshippers in film: a chosen one, surrendered, without a murmur.

THE DISCARDED

The Mutant Toys

Sid's creations — Babyface, Legs, the Frog, Ducky, Roller Bob and their ward-mates: toys mutilated and recombined by their tormentor, who have built, in his bedroom, a hospital and a resistance. The Mutants' function is the story's portrait of the discarded as the film's moral aristocracy: broken by the world, they spend the film repairing each other and, at the climax, rescuing the sheriff who feared becoming one of them. The archive files them as the decade's most consequential discarded in film: a ward, organized, into an army of mercy.

A boy's bedroom with cloud wallpaper: the film's whole world for its first act and its definition of paradise — a place where every toy has a role in one child's imagination. 10 / THEMATIC ANALYSIS

Themes & Analysis

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Displacement — worth after rank

The film's governing theme is the favorite's fall, and its rigor is in the diagnosis: Woody is not undone by Buzz's arrival but by his own response to it — the sabotage, not the succession, is the exile's cause. The theme is worked out in the exile itself (the favorite learning his trade among the discarded) until the audience holds its doctrine of the bench: worth conferred by love is not a ranking but a covenant, and covenants, unlike chairs, do not have a capacity of one. Its proposition reaches every senior person in the audience watching a newer model arrive with lights and sounds. The reach is the story's question to every displaced favorite: is your panic about your value, or only about your rank — and have you checked whether the marker is already on the new boot too?

The toy's purpose — being loved as an office

The film's second theme is its theology of purpose: a toy's meaning is not self-generated but relational — to be played with, slept beside, written on — and the film's heresy, delivered under a cardboard box, is that this dependence is not a lesser existence but the whole of the law. The theme is worked out in Buzz's recovery (the Ranger's cosmic mission traded for a kid's bedroom, at a profit) until the audience holds its doctrine of the conferred self: some identities are not achievements but assignments, and they are not diminished by being received. Its proposition reaches every person in the audience whose worth arrives through being needed. The reach is the story's question to every Buzz at a tea party: is your Mrs. Nesbitt phase a breakdown — or the corridor between a fiction you performed and a purpose you can actually hold?

The discarded — solidarity of the broken

The film's third theme is its ward of mutants: the toys the world broke and recombined, who respond to their damage by building the story's only fully functioning community of mercy. The theme is worked out in the jailbreak (authored by the discarded, executed for the privileged) until the audience holds its doctrine of damage's dividend: the broken know things about repair that the intact have never needed to learn, and a society is graded by whether its Sid's-room citizens are a ward or a warning. Its proposition reaches every institution in the audience with a back room of reassembled people. The reach is the story's question to every intact sheriff: when you fall — not if — do you know the way to the ward, and have you been kind enough, beforehand, to be taken in?

Falling with style — limitation as technique

The film's fourth theme is its finale's doctrine: Buzz's plastic wings cannot fly him, and the film's climax is not the wings becoming real but the falling becoming aimed — acceptance converting a delusion's debris into a capability. The theme is worked out in the glide (which works precisely because Buzz has stopped claiming it is flight) until the audience holds its doctrine of the honest glide: a limitation admitted, engineered around and believed in will carry a person further than the fiction it replaces ever did. Its proposition reaches every aging favorite and grounded ranger in the audience. The reach is the story's question to every Buzz on a roof: what is your version of the glide — the real capability, at your real specifications, that the delusion has been preventing you from practicing?

11 / FULL SPOILER CONCLUSION

Ending Explained

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL THE ENDING

Woody organizes Sid's mutant toys to break the toys' code — moving and speaking before a human — terrifying Sid into reforming, and frees Buzz from the rocket. In the moving-day chase the rocket is lit by sunlight through Buzz's helmet; Buzz cuts Woody free, they are launched, and Buzz glides — falling, with style — into Andy's car. At the new house Andy finds both toys together; at Christmas the two friends face the next arrivals side by side.

What the ending establishes

  • The jailbreak files the discarded as the story's executive arm: the rescue of the favorite is authored by the ward.
  • Sid's terror files the toys' first law as a weapon of last resort: the code exists to protect humans from knowledge, and breaking it is filed as an act of war in a just cause.
  • The helmet-lit rocket files Buzz's equipment as redeemed by his acceptance: the toy's features, honestly used, outperform the Ranger's fictions.
  • The glide files falling with style as the film's doctrine enacted: limitation, aimed, becomes arrival.
  • The Christmas coda files the anxiety as permanent and survivable: there will always be a next birthday, and the bench now has two veterans on it.

What remains uncertain

What the puppy will do to the room's constitution; how long a marker covenant lasts against a growing boy; and whether Sid's reformation survives adolescence — all stand open at the film's close (the sequels audited several of these accounts, at length and at cost). Within this film, the archive files the uncertainty as the story's own last word on its theme: the favorite's chair was a bench, the bench is at a new address, and the next arrival is already being barked at.

12 / PURPOSES OF THE STORY

Moral of The Story

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Toy Story uses a displaced cowboy and a deluded ranger to examine rank, purpose and damage: a favorite exiled by his own jealousy rather than his successor; a toy who discovers his worth is conferred by love, not claimed by fiction; and a ward of the broken who turn out to run the world's mercy. Through the bedroom, the gulag and the moving truck, the film argues that worth is a covenant, not a chair; that a purpose received is not a lesser purpose; and that the discarded, organized, are the rescue the intact will one day need.

Why tell this story?
