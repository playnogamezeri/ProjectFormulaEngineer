Avengers: Infinity War

A Titan who watched his world starve has a simple, merciful arithmetic: half of everything, chosen at random, so the other half may live. The heroes of ten years of films assemble to argue with the arithmetic — on three battlefields at once — and lose, not because they were weak, but because the universe's defender arrived with a philosophy and they arrived with a plan.

01 / FILM IDENTITY

Overview

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Avengers: Infinity War is a 2018 American superhero film directed by Anthony and Joe Russo and written by Christopher Markus and Stephen McFeely, the nineteenth film in the Marvel Cinematic Universe and the culmination of its first decade. Thanos of Titan, having concluded that life unchecked will consume the universe, moves to collect the six Infinity Stones and erase half of all living things with a snap of his fingers. Against him, a fractured Avengers, the Guardians of the Galaxy, Doctor Strange, Spider-Man and the armies of Wakanda fight a losing war across New York, Scotland, deep space, Titan and Wakanda itself.

Place in the franchise

The hinge of the entire MCU: the largest ensemble ever assembled in the series to that point, one of the most expensive films ever made, a $2 billion box-office landmark — and, structurally, a tragedy. Infinity War is the rare blockbuster built as its villain's biography: Thanos is the protagonist, the heroes are the obstacles, and the ending belongs to the antagonist, seated on a porch, watching a grateful universe's sunrise. This archive treats the film accordingly, as the first half of the Infinity Saga's two-part finale, completed by Avengers: Endgame (2019).

Filmed back to back with its sequel

Infinity War and Endgame were shot consecutively as one vast production — an industrial undertaking that let the filmmakers hold the saga's entire cast, and its secret, for a year between the Snap and its answer. Critics singled out Brolin's performance: a fully digital antagonist carrying a film's emotional center, played not as a monster but as a pilgrim — the quality this archive's character section treats as the film's foundation.

03 / NARRATIVE SUMMARY

Story Synopsis

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL FULL STORY SUMMARY

Thanos has the Power Stone from Xandar, and within minutes of the opening he has the Space Stone too: his Black Order boards the Asgardian refugee ship, Heimdall dies sending the Hulk to Earth as a warning, and Loki — offering the Tesseract in a final, doomed act of mischief — is strangled before his brother's eyes. The hunt fans out. In New York, Ebony Maw comes for the Time Stone in Doctor Strange's amulet; Strange is taken, and Stark and Peter Parker stow away on the ship that takes him. In Edinburgh, Proxima Midnight and Corvus Glaive ambush Wanda Maximoff and Vision for the Mind Stone in his forehead; the exiled Captain Rogers and his companions drive them off, and the reunited, still-fractured Avengers make the film's defining decision: rather than destroy the Stone — and Vision with it — they will take him to Wakanda, where Shuri may be able to separate stone from man while an army holds the line. "We don't trade lives," Rogers says, and the whole film is the price of the sentence. Meanwhile Thor, rescued by the Guardians, reads the board correctly: Thanos is collecting Stones. He takes Rocket and Groot to Nidavellir, where the last dwarf, Eitri, forges him a king's weapon — Stormbreaker — while the Guardians split: Gamora, Quill, Drax and Mantis to Knowhere, where Thanos is already waiting with the Reality Stone.

Gamora is taken. To stop her sister Nebula's torture, she gives up the secret she swore to die with: the Soul Stone is on Vormir. There, its keeper — the exiled Red Skull — names the price: the stone demands the sacrifice of what the seeker loves most. Gamora laughs, believing the loveless Titan has finally been refused by the universe. Then she sees his tears. Thanos throws his daughter from the cliff, and wakes with the Soul Stone in his hand. On Titan, Stark, Strange, Parker and the Guardians lay the saga's best trap: they come within a glove's breadth of pulling the Gauntlet from his hand — until Quill, told in that moment that Gamora is dead, strikes Thanos and breaks the spell that held him. Thanos wins the fight, drives a blade through Stark, and stops: Strange, who has viewed 14,000,605 possible futures and found exactly one victory, offers the Time Stone for Stark's life. "There was no other way," he tells the man he saved. In Wakanda the defense is magnificent and insufficient: Thor arrives with Stormbreaker and turns the battle in a single entrance — but Thanos comes himself, walks through the Avengers one by one, and reaches Vision just after Wanda, weeping, has done the unthinkable and destroyed the Mind Stone and the man she loves in one blast. Thanos is gentle with her. Then he turns back time, plucks the stone from Vision's restored head, and completes the Gauntlet. Thor's axe finds his chest — the chest, not the head. "You should have gone for the head," Thanos tells him, and snaps his fingers.

The full narrative summary is an editorial paraphrase, not dialogue or a scene-by-scene transcript.

04 / STORY STRUCTURE

Story & Chronology

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

The film unfolds over roughly two days, intercut across five fronts, following the Infinity Stones' convergence. The sequence below follows the film's narrative order.

Act I — The warning

A refugee ship massacred, a Hulk delivered to a New York sanctum like a dispatch: the war is announced by its casualties, and the scattered heroes begin, separately, to understand what is coming.

Act II — The dispersal

New York, Edinburgh, Knowhere, Nidavellir: the defense fragments by geography and old wounds — the Avengers still broken by their civil war, the Guardians split by a daughter's secret.

Act III — Vormir and Titan

A father's tears on a cliff, a trap undone by grief on a dead world: the film's two set-pieces are the same scene in mirror — love, arriving at the worst possible moment, costing everything.

Act IV — Wakanda

An army, a princess's laboratory, a king with a new axe: the last stand is the saga's most hopeful battle and its most futile, because the enemy's goal is not the field but one forehead.

Epilogue — The garden

A wounded Titan on a farm at sunrise; a world counting its missing. The film ends inside its villain's contentment, which is its final argument about what the heroes were actually fighting.

05 / CHARACTER DATABASE

Character Database

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Search the principal characters documented in this film. Roles and motivations below summarize their function in the 2018 movie.

THE PROTAGONIST · THE MAD TITAN

Thanos

Josh Brolin. A survivor of a world that starved, carrying a diagnosis and a cure no one will vote for. The film is built as his quest: he weeps at his daughter's cliff, thanks his torturers' victims for their service, and retires when the work is done. His serenity is the horror — a mass murderer played as the only character who finishes what he starts, whose philosophy the film states fairly precisely so that its victory can be measured in full.

IRON MAN · THE KNIGHT

Tony Stark

Robert Downey Jr. The man who has been dreaming of this invasion since New York, finally meeting it — and the film prices his preparedness honestly: the best armor he ever built buys him one wound, given and received, on a dead world. Stark's survival, purchased by Strange at the price of the Time Stone, is the plot's most expensive transaction and its clearest promissory note: this life was bought for a reason.

THE GOD OF THUNDER · THE GRIEF

Thor

Chris Hemsworth. Who loses, in the film's first ten minutes, his brother, his best friend and half his surviving people — and converts the loss, at a forge, into the one weapon that can end the war. Thor's arc is the film's study of aim under grief: given his moment, he drives Stormbreaker into Thanos's chest so the Titan will know who did it, and the universe pays for the sentence he needed to say.

THE CAPTAIN · THE REFUSAL

Steve Rogers

Chris Evans. An exile leading a team of fugitives, whose contribution to the war is a doctrine: we don't trade lives. The film honors the doctrine and bills it — the Wakanda gamble, Vision's stand, the army's losses are all purchased by Rogers's refusal to make the utilitarian cut. He is Thanos's true opposite in the story's argument: the man who will lose the universe rather than price a single life in it.

THE SORCERER · THE GAMBLER

Doctor Strange

Benedict Cumberbatch. Keeper of the Time Stone, who views 14,000,605 futures, finds one victory, and spends the rest of the film quietly executing a plan he cannot explain — including surrendering the very stone he swore an oath over, to save a man he barely likes. Strange's serenity in defeat is the film's buried thesis: somewhere inside this catastrophe is a single thread, and he has seen where it leads, and it leads through the Snap.

SPIDER-MAN · THE COST

Peter Parker

Tom Holland. A schoolboy who talks his way onto a spaceship and is knighted, mid-battle, for it — "you're an Avenger now" — in the film's warmest moment. His death is its coldest: the only dusting played at full length, in Stark's arms, apologizing. Parker is the argument the film makes against every abstraction in it: the half of all life has faces, and this is the one it chooses to show you last.

THE DAUGHTER · THE PRICE

Gamora

Zoe Saldaña. The favorite child of the universe's worst father, who escaped him, built a life against him, and is brought back to be its final price. Gamora's scenes with Thanos are the film's dark heart — a childhood of balanced knives and throne-room lessons, recalled at the edge of a cliff. Her laugh on Vormir, and its dying in her throat as she understands his tears, is the performance the whole saga pivots on.

THE WITCH · THE EXECUTIONER

Wanda Maximoff

Elizabeth Olsen. The only person on Earth who can destroy an Infinity Stone, asked to do it to the man she loves while an army dies buying her the minutes. Wanda's arc is the doctrine's invoice: Rogers's refusal to trade lives becomes, at the end, her trade to make — and she makes it, perfectly, and Thanos undoes it with a gesture, and the film lets her watch. Her grief is the battle of Wakanda's true result.

THE SYNTHETIC · THE ALTAR

Vision

Paul Bettany. A mind built around a stone, spending the film volunteering to be unmade — the one hero who agrees with the utilitarian math, overruled by everyone who loves him. Vision's serenity is the counterweight to Wanda's anguish: he dies twice in ten minutes, once by her hand as an act of love and once by Thanos's as an act of bookkeeping, and the difference between the deaths is the film's whole moral vocabulary.

THE KING · THE HOST

T'Challa / Black Panther

Chadwick Boseman. Who opens his hidden country to the world's war and stakes it on a friend's laboratory — Wakanda's new doctrine, tested the day it is declared. T'Challa's role is the host's: his kingdom is the battlefield because his kingdom volunteered, and his dusting — reaching for Okoye, "this is no place to die" — is staged as the bill arriving at the address that chose to receive it.

STAR-LORD · THE MOMENT

Peter Quill

Chris Pratt. Whose plan works. The film is exact about this: the Titan trap succeeds, the Gauntlet is sliding off — and then Quill learns, mid-hold, that the woman he loves is dead, and his grief does what Thanos's army couldn't. The story refuses to make him a villain for it; it simply records that the universe turned on a bereaved man's three punches, which is its bleakest statement about what outcomes are made of.

THE SCIENTIST · THE WARNING

Bruce Banner

Mark Ruffalo. Delivered to Earth as a message in a broken body: the Hulk, beaten for the first time, refuses to come out for the rest of the film, and the saga's strongest weapon spends its biggest war in a borrowed suit of armor. Banner's arc is the film's running joke with a thesis inside it — even the heroes' own powers are negotiating their participation; everyone in this war is, in some part, not showing up.

06 / THE AVENGERS, THE GUARDIANS & THE BLACK ORDER

The Avengers, the Guardians & the Black Order

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
THE BROKEN TEAM

The Avengers

Entering the war still split by the Sokovia Accords — Stark and Rogers do not share a scene, and the film's structure is the fracture's map: two half-teams on two battlefields, each missing the other's strength. The story's cold arithmetic is that the universe's defense failed first in a German airport two years earlier; Thanos merely collected on the division.

THE FAMILY

The Guardians of the Galaxy

Brought into the war by a body floating in space, and carrying its emotional detonators: Gamora's parentage, Nebula's torture, Quill's grief. The Guardians are the film's argument that this war is, at root, a family matter — the Titan's daughters are on both sides of it — and that the cosmic conflict will be decided less by fleets than by what one father did to his children.

THE CHILDREN OF THANOS

The Black Order

Ebony Maw, Cull Obsidian, Proxima Midnight and Corvus Glaive: the heralds and enforcers, each a liturgy of the mission — Maw preaches it, the others execute it. The film uses them as the war's working level, the opponents the heroes can actually beat, which is its structural honesty: the lieutenants all fall, and it changes nothing, because the war was never about them.

THE HOSTS

Wakanda

A nation in its first week of openness, receiving the universe's end as its inaugural state visit. Wakanda's armies, shield and princess-scientist are the defense's high-water mark — proof of what the hidden century built — and its fields are where the film stages its verdict: the finest army on Earth, fully deployed, correctly led, is a delaying action. The war was lost at the level of the Stones, not the spears.

Conflict over the price of a life

The war's every front is the same negotiation in different dress. Thanos prices life wholesale — half, at random, as policy. The heroes price it retail and refuse all sales: Vision's life against the universe, Stark's life against the Time Stone, Gamora's life against her sister's pain. The film keeps the ledger with unusual care, and its ending is the audit: the refusal to trade lives loses the war, and the one trade the heroes finally make — Strange's — is the one the sequel will reveal as the purchase of everything. The argument is not settled here; it is invoiced.

07 / WORLD & SETTING

World & Setting

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Titan — the cautionary world

A dead planet of leaning ruins, where the film's villain was once its unheeded prophet. Titan is the story's founding exhibit: the civilization that called Thanos mad, died entire, and made him certain. The final battle is staged there deliberately — the heroes fight the universe's future on the grave of its warning, among the tilted towers of the last people who declined to listen to him.

Vormir — the altar

A mountain, a keeper, a cliff: the Soul Stone's shrine strips the cosmic quest to a folktale's elements. Vormir is the film's still center — no army reaches it, no technology applies, and its rule is older than the Stones' fame: to gain the soul, surrender one. The setting's desolation is the point; the universe's most valuable object is guarded by the simplest possible lock, a heart.

Nidavellir — the forge

A dying star harnessed in rings, where a giant dwarf with ruined hands builds one last weapon. Nidavellir is the war's mythology department: the place where the film insists that even in an age of stones and starships, the turning point is a crafted thing, made by a survivor, at a price — Eitri's hands for his people's lives, a bargain with Thanos that the forge scene quietly lays beside all the others.

Wakanda — the last field

The hidden kingdom, its shield opened by hand to let the war in and its armies deployed on the grass where its kings are crowned. The film's geography converges here because its themes do: the nation that hid from the world's suffering hosts the universe's, and learns in one afternoon what the hiding had been sparing it — and what it had been sparing the world.

08 / THE SIX INFINITY STONES

The Six Infinity Stones

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Power — taken first, off-screen

From Xandar, before the film begins: the story opens with the war already lost once. The Power Stone's absence from the plot is structural — it is the premise, the reason no one in the film can simply fight Thanos and win.

Space — the Tesseract's price

Surrendered by Loki to spare Thor, then used by its thief's killer to leave: the Space Stone's transfer is the film's first lesson in its economics — every stone changes hands over a body or a bond, and this one costs the saga its oldest villain, mid-redemption, in the first reel.

Reality — the collector's ruin

Taken on Knowhere inside an illusion: Thanos arrives before the heroes and stages the scene they walk into. The Reality Stone's sequence is the film's thesis about its villain's preparation — he does not react to the plot; the plot is one of his sets.

Soul — the price that proves the premise

Vormir's rule converts the film's abstract argument into a single act: the stone demands what the seeker loves, and Thanos can pay. The Soul Stone is therefore the story's character witness — the universe's own testimony that the monster's love is real, which is precisely what makes him, rather than any mere conqueror, the saga's final problem.

Time — the surrendered stone

Strange's trade is the film's most debated transaction: the stone he swore to protect with lives, given up for one life, on the evidence of fourteen million futures. The Time Stone's function in the story is double — within the plot it completes Thanos's power to undo Wanda's sacrifice; within the saga it is the down payment on the single victory Strange saw.

Mind — the stone with a person attached

The only stone that is also a character: the Mind Stone cannot be taken without killing Vision, which makes it the war's moral ground zero. Every faction's philosophy is stated in its plan for this stone — destroy it (Vision), save both (Rogers, Shuri), rewind and extract (Thanos) — and the film's ending is simply the record of which plan the universe permitted.

The war as a family matter

Infinity War's sprawling cast resolves, on inspection, into a handful of households: Thanos's ruined one, the Avengers' fractured one, the Guardians' improvised one, Wakanda's royal one. Every decisive event in the film is a family event wearing a battle's clothes — a daughter sacrificed, a brother avenged, a lover executed, a son-figure lost. The film's scale is cosmic and its mechanics are domestic, and that mismatch is its method: it prices the end of the universe in the only currency audiences actually hold.

10 / THEMATIC ANALYSIS

Themes & Analysis

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Utilitarianism, taken seriously

The film's distinction is that it lets the utilitarian argument be strong. Thanos's math is internally sound, his diagnosis has a corpse of a planet for evidence, and his willingness to pay personally — his daughter, his soul's peace — is greater than any hero's. The story opposes him not with better math but with a refusal: Rogers's doctrine that some prices void the purchase. Infinity War is the rare blockbuster that stages the trolley problem at universal scale and then reports, honestly, that refusing to pull the lever lost — this time.

Grief is the war's decisive weapon

Every hinge in the plot turns on bereavement: Thor's aim, Quill's fists, Wanda's hesitation, Thanos's tears. The film's covert thesis is that outcomes are not decided by the strongest arsenal but by the distribution of grief at the critical minute — who is mourning, how freshly, and within arm's reach of which lever. It is a strangely intimate claim for a film of this size, and the ending ratifies it: the Snap happens because a brother needed his enemy to know, and a lover needed his enemy to hurt.

The fractured team as a failed state

The Avengers enter the film as the sequel to an argument — the Accords split dramatized as geography. The film's political observation is quiet and total: institutions rarely fall to the enemy they were built for; they fall to the unresolved internal dispute that keeps their strength in two rooms. Thanos never defeats the Avengers. He defeats their halves, sequentially, which the story presents as the same enemy winning a different, earlier war.

Sacrifice, audited

The film is a catalogue of offerings, each priced differently: Loki's life for Thor's, Gamora's for the stone, Vision's offered and refused, Wanda's lover by her own hand, Strange's stone for Stark's life, a universe's half for its other half. Its argument emerges from the accounting — the worth of a sacrifice is not its size but its direction: what is given for others compounds; what is taken from others, however tearfully, is theft at scale wearing sacrifice's clothes.

The villain as protagonist

Structurally the film belongs to Thanos: he has the goal, the arc, the losses and the final scene. The heroes are distributed across his path as a series of examinations, each testing his resolve and finding it sufficient. The device is the film's boldest inheritance from its source comics and its greatest risk — the audience is made to follow a pilgrim's progress toward atrocity, and the discomfort of understanding him is the intended souvenir.

11 / FULL SPOILER CONCLUSION

Ending Explained

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL THE ENDING

The Snap is not a battle scene; it is weather. Thanos, Stormbreaker in his chest, snaps his fingers and is gone through a portal before the wound can matter, and the film slows down to watch the consequence arrive person by person: Bucky, calling for Steve as he comes apart; T'Challa reaching for his general; Groot's last word to Rocket; Wanda, granted the mercy of following Vision within the minute; and on Titan, Peter Parker — the youngest, given the longest goodbye, apologizing to the man who knighted him as he goes. Strange's last words are the film's riddle, delivered as he dissolves: Tony, there was no other way. The count of the vanished is half of everything that lives. What remains assembles in silence on a Wakandan field, around the grey corpse of Vision, in the company of a raccoon and a king's guard and a soldier who has now lost everyone he ever followed.

And the film ends with its antagonist. Thanos limps out of a hut on a garden world, sits down with his ruined arm in a sling of light, and watches the sun rise on the universe he has, by his accounting, saved — the smile of a man whose work is finished and whose bill, a daughter, was paid in full and on time. After the credits, the film's final note belongs to the old world: Nick Fury, dissolving on an Atlanta street, gets one message out on a modified pager before he goes — and the screen that answers is a star-spangled emblem the audience has not yet met. The cavalry, the film says, exists. It is simply, like everything else in this story, too late for today.

What the ending establishes

  • Thanos has won completely: all six stones, the Snap accomplished, half of all life erased at random, and the architect retired, content, beyond reach.
  • The named dead include Bucky Barnes, T'Challa, Groot, Wanda Maximoff, Sam Wilson, Peter Parker, Peter Quill, Drax, Mantis, Doctor Strange, Nick Fury and Maria Hill — alongside half of all living things, everywhere.
  • Strange's bargain is the ending's planted seed: he traded the Time Stone for Stark's life after seeing exactly one victorious future, and his final words assert the defeat was inside the plan.
  • The surviving core — Stark, Rogers, Thor, Banner, Romanoff, Rhodes, Rocket, Nebula, Okoye — is left with nothing but each other and the arithmetic of the loss.
  • A distress call has gone out to Captain Marvel: the wider universe's reserves exist, and the story's second half will be fought by the people this ending leaves standing.

What remains uncertain

Whether the Snap can be undone, what Strange's one victory requires, and what a retired Thanos will do if the universe objects are all left open — the film ends the war's first day, not the war.

12 / PURPOSES OF THE STORY

Moral of The Story

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Avengers: Infinity War uses a pilgrim-genocidaire and the heroes who fail to stop him to examine the ethics of the necessary evil: what a true diagnosis licenses, what a refusal to price life costs, and what grief does to the best-laid plans at the decisive minute. Through a daughter traded for a stone, a stone traded for a life, and an axe aimed at a chest instead of a head, the film argues that outcomes are moral events before they are strategic ones — and that the side which treats people as numbers will beat, every time, the side too divided to be a side.

Why tell this story?

A decade of franchise storytelling had taught audiences that the heroes assemble and the world is saved; this film exists to retire the guarantee. Infinity War tells the middle of the story — the part where the diagnosis is real, the villain is sincere, the heroes are divided and grief is unplanned-for — and insists the middle be sat with, at full length, before any rescue is permitted. Its purpose is weight: to make the saga's eventual victory cost what victory costs.

1. A true diagnosis does not license any cure

Titan died as Thanos said it would; the film never disputes his facts, only his prescription. The transferable discipline is the audit of remedies: when a frightened age produces a man with a real problem and a monstrous solution, the solution is not validated by the problem's reality. It is indicted by it — the worse the diagnosis, the more carefully the cure must be examined, because catastrophe is the worst reason ever offered for suspending the audit.

2. Settle the internal war before the external one arrives

The Avengers lose to their own unfinished argument: two half-teams, two battlefields, one unmade phone call. The film's institutional lesson precedes all its spectacle — division is not a private matter among the strong; it is the enemy's first and cheapest victory, banked years in advance. Whatever your airport fight was, finish it in peacetime. The universe will not schedule its invasion around your reconciliation.

3. Grief needs a plan, because it will have a vote

Quill's punches and Thor's aim are the same event in different registers: mourning, unbriefed, seizing the controls at the decisive second. The film does not condemn either man — it simply totals the cost, which is everything. The counsel is practical and unglamorous: the people most likely to be grieving at the critical moment should not be the people holding the critical lever, and a team that cannot say this to itself will have it said by the outcome.

4. Some prices void the purchase

Rogers's doctrine loses the war and is not refuted by the film — the sequel's entire existence is its vindication fund. The distinction the story draws is between costs and prices: a cost is what you pay of your own; a price is what you charge to someone else's account. Vision understood it, volunteering; Thanos never did, invoicing his daughter. Before any necessary evil, the film asks one question: whose name is on the bill? If it is not yours, it is not sacrifice. It is spending.

5. The long game is played inside the defeat

Strange's surrender is the film's hope, engineered to look like its opposite: the one victory in fourteen million futures runs through this loss, and the sorcerer's job is to lose correctly. The lesson is the strategist's version of faith — that a defeat can be a position rather than an ending, if someone has done the arithmetic and is willing to be misunderstood by everyone, including the audience, for a year.

6. Finish the sentence later; aim now

Thor's chest-shot is the film's smallest and largest error: a king's weapon, a clear target, and one second spent on the emotional requirement that his enemy know who had come. The universe's bill for that second is itemized in the credits. The counsel is not coldness — it is sequence. There are moments whose entire content is the act, with the meaning to be made afterward, among the living, at leisure. Rehearse knowing which moment you are in.

The moral of the story is…

The hardest choices do require the strongest wills — the film agrees with its villain's slogan and disputes only his ledger. Strength of will in the story's accounting is not the nerve to spend other people's lives on a true idea; it is the nerve to hold a line that costs you the war today, to lose correctly when losing is the position, and to pay your own bills in full, in advance, with your own name on them.

Avengers: Infinity War teaches that division is defeat pre-paid, that grief will vote in every crisis whether or not it is seated, and that the difference between a sacrifice and an atrocity is never the size of the loss but the direction of it — given, or taken.

The film's final question is therefore not whether the Snap can be undone. It is whether the audience, in its own divided houses and frightened age, can tell the difference between a man with a diagnosis and a man with a cure — before the sunrise he is sitting in becomes theirs to share.
