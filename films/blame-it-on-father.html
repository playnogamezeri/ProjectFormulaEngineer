01 / FILM IDENTITY

Overview

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Blame It on Father （父之過 — "the father's fault"; also Father's Fault and It's Father's Fault) is a 1953 Hong Kong Cantonese family drama directed by Suen Wai, written by Tsui Tai, produced by the Dacheng (Tai Seng) Film Company, and running 93 minutes — the shortest of Lee's 1953 pictures and, by the Hong Kong Film Archive's account, one of the most precious. The Archive's programme note is the film's fullest English document, and this file follows it: the film "ambitiously strives to enlighten the audience while entertaining them, drawing drama and comedy out of the age-old issue of child rearing, offering as solution a concerted effort by family, education, religion, law enforcement, literature and popular culture. The story centres on a group of children as they negotiate the complexity of life, each one's ways a confluence of nature and nurture." The title files the thesis with a lawyer's directness: when a child goes wrong, the fault is the father's — the era's child-rearing debate given a defendant. And at the film's center stands its famous casting: "Lee plays a bully, an older version of his character in the classic The Kid (1950) had he not gone back to China" — Kid Cheung's road not taken, the street boy unredeemed and grown harder, played by the same boy three years on. HKMDB names the role: Dai Gnan-Gau — "Big-Eyed Dog." Then the moment the Archive calls "one for the ages": Lee performs a few lines of the Cantonese opera song "Robbing the Road to the Afterlife" — the signature tune of his father, Lee Hoi-chuen, the opera star — a son, inside a film about fathers' faults, singing his own father's signature. The government's 2020 announcement for the Archive's gems series files the performance among "the most precious records of Lee as a child," and the Archive has screened the film from a digitization of its sole existing 35mm copy, with special showings held on the anniversary of Lee's death. The cast records name Chiang Kwai-lam, Siu Kei-lun, Pong Bik-wan and Wong Cho-san around the children. The archive files the picture as the 1953 group's parable exhibit: the year's thesis film about who is to blame for a child — answered, characteristically for its era, with a consortium of every institution the culture could muster.
