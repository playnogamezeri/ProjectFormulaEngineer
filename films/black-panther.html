01 / FILM IDENTITY

Overview

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Black Panther is a 2018 American superhero film directed by Ryan Coogler, written by Coogler and Joe Robert Cole, and produced by Marvel Studios — the eighteenth film in the Marvel Cinematic Universe and the first Marvel film with a Black director and a predominantly Black cast. After his father's death, T'Challa returns to the hidden African nation of Wakanda to take the throne, and is challenged by Erik Killmonger, a soldier with royal blood and a revolutionary's program: end Wakanda's centuries of hiding, and arm the oppressed of the world with vibranium.

Place in the franchise

The MCU's landmark: the first superhero film nominated for the Academy Award for Best Picture, winner of three Oscars (costume design, production design, original score), and for years the highest-grossing film ever directed by a Black filmmaker. This archive records the honors because they are part of the film's fact — a genre picture that was received, immediately and permanently, as a cultural event, and whose argument (what does the fortunate owe the suffering?) is conducted at the level of statecraft.

A world built on purpose

Coogler's Wakanda was designed as a thesis: production designer Hannah Beachler and costume designer Ruth Carter built a nation from real African cultures, languages and architectures, uncolonized and therefore free to be futuristic on its own terms. The design work is not decoration but argument — the film's central question is what Africa might have built had it never been interrupted, and the screen answers at the level of cities, garments and typefaces.

03 / NARRATIVE SUMMARY

Story Synopsis

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL FULL STORY SUMMARY

A vibranium meteorite fell on Africa in the deep past; five tribes warred over it until Bashenga, guided by the goddess Bast to a heart-shaped herb, became the first Black Panther and united four of them as Wakanda. The fifth, the Jabari, withdrew to the mountains. Wakanda used the metal to become the most advanced nation on Earth — and hid, posing as a poor farming country behind a holographic shield. In 1992, in Oakland, California, King T'Chaka confronts his own brother: Prince N'Jobu, a Wakandan War Dog, has helped the arms dealer Ulysses Klaue steal vibranium, meaning to arm the oppressed Black people of the world against their oppressors. N'Jobu's closest friend is revealed as Zuri, another spy. N'Jobu pulls a gun on Zuri; T'Chaka kills his brother to save him — and flies home, leaving N'Jobu's small son Erik behind, and ordering the truth buried. Twenty-four years later T'Chaka is dead, killed at Vienna, and his son T'Challa comes home to be crowned. At Warrior Falls he defeats the Jabari leader M'Baku in ritual combat and spares him; he takes the herb and hears his father's wisdom in the Ancestral Plane: a good man finds it hard to be a king.

The past arrives in stages. Klaue resurfaces, selling a Wakandan artifact stolen from a London museum; in Busan, T'Challa, Okoye and Nakia capture him — and lose him to a rescue raid led by a scarred American black-ops soldier who carries a Wakandan royal ring. The soldier, Erik Stevens, called Killmonger, kills Klaue himself and delivers the body to Wakanda's Border Tribe as his passport. Before the elders he names himself: N'Jadaka, son of N'Jobu, of the royal blood — and he challenges T'Challa for the throne under the law. Zuri, the last keeper of the Oakland secret, steps into the duel to confess and offer his own life; Killmonger takes it, defeats the distracted king, and throws him over the falls. Crowned, Killmonger takes the herb, orders the entire heart-shaped herb garden burned so no Panther can ever follow him, and commands the War Dogs of the world activated: vibranium weapons are to fly to London, New York and Hong Kong to begin a global revolution of the oppressed, with Wakanda as its empire. The royal family flees to the Jabari mountains — where M'Baku, repaying a life with a life, reveals T'Challa: found by Jabari fishermen, frozen, alive. Revived by the one herb Nakia stole from the burning, T'Challa returns to the Ancestral Plane and does the film's bravest thing: he indicts his ancestors. They were wrong — all of them — to turn their backs on the rest of the world. He wakes, and goes back to finish the challenge his deposition interrupted, while Shuri, Nakia and the Dora Milaje fight to stop the weapons ships and Ross, from a remote cockpit in Shuri's lab, shoots the transports down.

The full narrative summary is an editorial paraphrase, not dialogue or a scene-by-scene transcript.

04 / STORY STRUCTURE

Story & Chronology

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

The main action unfolds days after the events of Captain America: Civil War, framed by the mythic founding of Wakanda and the 1992 Oakland prologue that the whole plot exists to answer. The sequence below follows the film's narrative order.

Prologue — Oakland, 1992

A king kills his brother to protect a secret, abandons a boy, and calls it necessity: the film's original sin, committed by its most revered figure.

Act I — The coronation

A rescue in Nigeria, a duel at the falls, a spared challenger, a father's blessing from the far side of death: the kingdom at its most beautiful and most certain.

Act II — Klaue

A museum theft, a casino in Busan, a captured arms dealer and a prison break: the outside world's chaos keeps arriving addressed to Wakanda.

Act III — The challenger

A corpse as a passport, a name as a claim, a confession at the falls, a king over the edge: the buried past takes the throne by the kingdom's own law.

Act IV — The burning and the return

The herb garden in flames, the weapons fleet launching, a dead king thawing in the mountains: revolution and restoration race each other to the same runway.

Epilogue — Oakland, again

The king returns to the street where the sin was committed and builds the answer on the site: an outreach center, and a secret given up on purpose.

05 / CHARACTER DATABASE

Character Database

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Search the principal characters documented in this film. Roles and motivations below summarize their function in the 2018 movie.

THE KING

T'Challa / Black Panther

Chadwick Boseman. A good man inheriting a perfect kingdom and an imperfect truth. T'Challa's heroism in this film is unusually unphysical for the genre: his decisive acts are a mercy (sparing M'Baku), an indictment (rebuking his ancestors to their faces), and a policy (opening Wakanda) — the duel he wins matters less than the doctrine he reverses. He is the rare franchise lead whose arc is learning that his father's kingdom was wrong.

THE CHALLENGER · N'JADAKA

Erik Killmonger

Michael B. Jordan. The abandoned prince of Oakland, raised by the streets his uncle's kingdom refused to see, credentialed by America's wars — every scar on his body a kill he counted. Killmonger is the genre's most argued-over antagonist because his indictment is accurate: Wakanda watched. His revolution is his father's dream executed as vengeance, and the film honors the grievance while refusing the empire he builds on it.

THE WAR DOG · THE CONSCIENCE

Nakia

Lupita Nyong'o. A spy who has seen the world's suffering at ground level and returned with the film's thesis in her mouth: Wakanda could help, and should. Nakia is the future arguing with the crown from inside its own heart — she loves the king and disagrees with his kingdom, saves the herb that saves his life, and is finally handed the outreach mission that is her idea made policy.

THE GENERAL

Okoye

Danai Gurira. Head of the Dora Milaje, the king's guard and the throne's instrument — loyal not to the man but to the office, which the film tests cruelly when the office changes hands by law. Okoye's anguish under Killmonger, and her stand against her own husband in the final battle, make her the story's study of institutional honor: what it costs, whom it serves, and where its limit finally stands.

THE PRINCESS · THE LAB

Shuri

Letitia Wright. The king's sister and the kingdom's chief scientist, whose laboratory is Wakanda's argument for itself: genius, joyful and unbothered by reverence. Shuri's function is generational — she is the future the tradition exists to protect, irreverent about everything except the work. In the final battle she fights in person, because in this film the lab and the front line are the same country's two hands.

THE QUEEN MOTHER

Ramonda

Angela Bassett. The crown's continuity in person: widow, mother of the king, and the one who carries Wakanda's grief with its full dignity intact. Ramonda's flight to the Jabari — a queen begging the kingdom's exiles for her son's life — is the film's portrait of royalty stripped to its essence: she has nothing to offer M'Baku but the truth, and it is sufficient.

THE MOUNTAIN KING

M'Baku

Winston Duke. Leader of the Jabari, the tribe that refused the founding bargain and kept the old gods and the cold. M'Baku enters as the challenger and becomes the film's standard of honor: defeated and spared, he repays the mercy exactly, preserving the king he tried to depose and arriving with his warriors at the battle's hinge. The exile's loyalty shames the court's.

THE BORDER LORD

W'Kabi

Daniel Kaluuya. Head of the Border Tribe, T'Challa's closest friend, and the door through which Killmonger enters — because W'Kabi's parents were killed by Klaue, and the crown never delivered him justice. His defection is the film's warning about unaddressed grievance inside the gates: the revolution's first recruit is always the loyalist whose wound the state declined to treat.

THE KEEPER OF THE SECRET

Zuri

Forest Whitaker. The spy who played N'Jobu's friend, the priest who administers the herb, the old man who carried Oakland for twenty-four years. Zuri's confession at the falls — stepping between the duelists to claim his share of the guilt and accept its price — is the film's moral fulcrum: the secret's last keeper spends it, too late to save the past and just in time to define the future's judgment of it.

THE BROTHER · THE FIRST SIN

N'Jobu

Sterling K. Brown. The prince who went out as a spy and came back, in his heart, as a revolutionary: Oakland showed him the world's wound, and he chose the wound over the kingdom. N'Jobu appears in one scene and generates the entire film — his dream, stolen and weaponized by his son; his death, concealed by his brother; his street, finally, redeemed by his nephew. The saga's most consequential ghost per minute of screen time.

THE THIEF

Ulysses Klaue

Andy Serkis. The arms dealer who stole Wakanda's vibranium and its peace of mind, the only outsider to breach the kingdom and live — for a while — on the proceeds. Klaue is the plot's catalyst and its red herring: everyone believes he is the problem, including two kings, while the actual claim on the throne grows up in Oakland. He dies as he lived, a transaction — his corpse the price of another man's admission.

THE GUEST

Everett K. Ross

Martin Freeman. A CIA agent shot in Wakanda's quarrel, healed in Shuri's lab, and held — politely — as the kingdom's accidental witness. Ross's arc is the outside world's: from interrogating Klaue to piloting a Wakandan fighter by remote link to shoot down the weapons fleet, he becomes the first outsider the kingdom trusts with its guns, and his wonder stands in for the audience's at the reveal.

06 / TRIBES, THE DORA MILAJE & THE WAR DOGS

Tribes, the Dora Milaje & the War Dogs

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
THE FIVE TRIBES

The River, Border, Merchant, Mining — and the Jabari

Four tribes under the crown and one in the mountains that refused it: Wakanda's founding compromise, still load-bearing. The film's political map is tribal before it is ideological — the Border Tribe's grievance admits the challenger, the Jabari's exile preserves the king — and its lesson is that a nation's oldest arrangements decide its newest crises.

THE KING'S GUARD

The Dora Milaje

The all-women royal guard, recruited from every tribe as the crown's living treaty — their unity is the nation's, rehearsed daily in spear-work. Under Killmonger they serve the throne they hate, because the office is the office; in the final battle they are the force that holds the field while kings duel elsewhere, and Okoye's stand against W'Kabi is the civil war ended by a marriage's question.

THE EYES ABROAD

The War Dogs

Wakanda's spies, embedded worldwide under orders to watch and never act — the institution where the kingdom's doctrine does its human damage. N'Jobu and Nakia are the War Dogs' two answers to the same posting: he broke, she argues. Killmonger's masterstroke is to activate the network as a revolutionary army-in-waiting: the watchers, ordered at last to act, by the wrong king, for the wrong war.

THE OUTSIDE

The CIA and the world's powers

Present as one decent agent and a great deal of appetite: the world's governments in this film are what Wakanda always said they were — ready to take the metal and call it policy. Ross's individual honor is the film's concession that institutions are judged, finally, by whether anyone inside them will pilot the jet against the fleet.

Conflict over what the fortunate owe

Every faction's position is an answer to one question: what does a hidden utopia owe a suffering world? The elders answer: survival — we owe the world nothing that endangers us. N'Jobu and Nakia answer: aid — we owe help proportioned to our strength. Killmonger answers: arms — we owe the oppressed the means of empire, ours. The film adjudicates across its whole length and lands, deliberately, between the second answer and the third: help, yes; empire, no; and the hiding itself is struck from the list of options.

07 / WORLD & SETTING

World & Setting

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Wakanda — the hidden utopia

A nation that answered colonization by vanishing: behind its shield, maglev trains, flying craft and medicine that heals spinal wounds overnight, while the world pities its farmers. The film's Wakanda is beautiful and indicted in the same frame — every marvel is evidence for both the defense (look what protection built) and the prosecution (look what it cost everyone outside the shield).

Warrior Falls — the constitution in water

The cliff where kings are made by single combat, stripped of the herb's power, before all the tribes. The falls are Wakanda's democracy of one ritual: any challenger of the blood may claim the throne by strength. The film uses the setting twice as mirror scenes — a coronation and a deposition — and lets the audience watch the same law produce a king and then a catastrophe, and judge the law.

Oakland — the other capital

A basketball court under a landing strip's flight path, in the city where the Black Panther Party was founded: the film's geography is an argument. Oakland is where Wakanda's secret did its damage and where its future is finally built; the bookending is the story's map of responsibility, drawn from a throne room to a street corner.

The Ancestral Plane — the audit

A sunset savanna where kings consult their fathers: the film's most beautiful setting and its courtroom. T'Challa goes there twice — first as a son seeking blessing, then as a king delivering judgment. The plane is where Wakanda's past is literally enthroned, panthers on the branches, and the sequel in the same space is the story's hinge: the ancestors, for once, are the ones being examined.

08 / VIBRANIUM, THE HEART-SHAPED HERB & THE ANCESTRAL PLANE

Vibranium, the Heart-Shaped Herb & the Ancestral Plane

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Vibranium — the metal that absorbs

Sound- and energy-absorbing, woven into suits, ships, medicine and the Panther's necklace of claws: vibranium is the material form of Wakanda's whole philosophy — take the world's force and keep it, return nothing. Killmonger's program is the same metal read backwards: vibranium as distribution, the stored force of centuries released into the world's streets in a single order.

The heart-shaped herb

Grown in one garden, administered by one priesthood, granting the Panther's strength and the Ancestral Plane's door: the herb is the monarchy's sacrament and its single point of failure. Killmonger understands this instantly — his burning of the garden is the film's most revolutionary act, an attack not on the king but on kingship's supply chain. That one stolen flower resurrects the old order is the film's concession to how fragile, and how persistent, succession is.

The ritual of challenge

Any royal-blood challenger may claim the throne at the falls, with the Panther's power chemically withdrawn for fairness. The film treats the rite with genuine respect and genuine alarm: it is Wakanda's proudest safeguard against tyranny-by-bloodline and the exact mechanism by which a tyrant legally takes the throne. Institutions, the film notes, cannot distinguish between their safeguards and their vulnerabilities when the claimant is sincere.

The shield and the lie

A holographic dome and a maintained fiction — poor farmers, no resources, nothing to see: the hiding is Wakanda's founding technology, older than its aircraft. The film prices it honestly. It bought five centuries of uncolonized flourishing; it cost the kingdom its soul's ease, one prince's life, one boy's childhood, and a front-row seat, unoccupied, at every catastrophe of the modern age.

Kimoyo beads and the lab

Communication, medicine and remote piloting in a bracelet; a princess's workshop that outclasses the world's aerospace firms: Wakandan technology in the film is intimate rather than monumental, worn on the wrist and built by a teenager who teases her king. The design is the doctrine's best self — advancement as craft, held close, in service of the person wearing it.

The Ancestral Plane

Reached through the herb and burial in sand: a dusk-lit savanna where the Panther meets the line of Panthers. The film uses it as monarchy's internal audit — counsel, blessing, and finally indictment — and its theology is precise: the ancestors are real, honored, and wrong, all three, and a good king's duty includes telling them so. Reverence, in Wakanda's own rite, turns out to have an appeals process.

The two funerals the film is about

Black Panther is structured by two deaths in Oakland's orbit: N'Jobu's, concealed, which festers into a challenger; and Killmonger's, witnessed at sunset, which the film deliberately gives the dignity the first was denied. Between them runs the story's whole ethic — that what a kingdom does with its dead determines what its living become. T'Challa cannot unbury his uncle. He can refuse, at the end, to bury his cousin in a lie — and the outreach center rises on the exact ground where the concealment happened, which is the film's definition of atonement: not apology, but construction on the site.

10 / THEMATIC ANALYSIS

Themes & Analysis

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Isolation is a policy with a body count

Wakanda's hiding is the film's central moral object, examined from every side: it preserved a civilization intact through the centuries of slavery and colonization — a genuine, staggering achievement — and it did so while watching those centuries happen to everyone else. The film refuses both easy verdicts. The shield is neither celebrated as wisdom nor condemned as cowardice; it is priced, in N'Jobu's life, Erik's childhood and the world's unaided suffering, and the price is presented to the one man with authority to discontinue it.

The antagonist as the hero's evidence

Killmonger is built as the prosecution's exhibit against the hero's kingdom: everything he says about Wakanda's abandonment is true, and the film gives him its best lines because the argument requires them to be. His failure is not his grievance but his remedy — liberation administered as empire, freedom distributed as weapons, a father's dream inherited as a body count. The film's structure is a trial in which the defendant-king, uniquely, ends by pleading guilty to the lesser charge and changing the law.

Fathers, audited

Three fathers govern the film from offstage: T'Chaka, whose sin is the plot; N'Jobu, whose dream is the program; and the line of Panther ancestors, enthroned in the savanna, whose doctrine is the defendant. T'Challa's heroism is filial and adversarial at once — he is the son who tells the assembled fathers of his nation that they were wrong, in the sacred place, as the rite allows. The film proposes that reverence without audit is how good kingdoms rot politely.

Tradition is a tool, not a verdict

The challenge rite makes a king and unmakes one; the herb empowers and endangers; the Dora Milaje's oath sustains the state and serves its usurper. The film's treatment of tradition is a craftsman's: every inherited instrument is shown working exactly as designed, for whoever holds it. Wakanda's renewal, when it comes, is not the abolition of its rites but their re-aiming — the War Dogs become outreach workers; the falls remain; the doctrine changes.

What the fortunate owe

The film's question scales from the personal to the geopolitical without changing shape: a hidden utopia and the world's wound; a king and an abandoned cousin; a audience member and the street outside the theater. Its answer is deliberately institutional rather than sentimental — not guilt, not charity, but construction: centers built, knowledge shared, doors opened on purpose. Bridges, in the king's own closing figure, over barriers.

11 / FULL SPOILER CONCLUSION

Ending Explained

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL THE ENDING

The revolution is stopped at the runway and in the mine. Ross, piloting from Shuri's lab, shoots down the weapons transports; the Border Tribe's charge breaks on Okoye's question and her husband's surrender; the Jabari's arrival turns the field. Below, in the vibranium mine, T'Challa fights Killmonger along the maglev track and uses the sonic stabilizers — which nullify their suits' charge in pulses — to open the one gap he needs. He stabs his cousin, and then does the thing the film has been building toward since Oakland: he stops fighting him. He carries Killmonger up to see the Wakandan sunset his father used to describe — the most beautiful in the world, Erik says — and offers him the kingdom's medicine. Killmonger refuses it in the film's defining sentence: bury him in the ocean, with the ancestors who jumped from the slave ships, because they knew death was better than bondage. He pulls the blade free himself and dies a prince of Wakanda, unhealed and unbowed, having lost the war and won the argument that will govern the peace.

What follows is policy, which the film treats as the true climax. T'Challa returns to Oakland and buys the buildings where his uncle died — the site of the sin becomes the site of Wakanda's first international outreach center, with Nakia and Shuri to run it: the War Dogs' mission inverted, spies replaced by teachers. And at the United Nations in Vienna — the city where his father died defending the old order's diplomacy — the king gives up the founding secret on purpose, telling the world what Wakanda is and what it intends: in times of crisis, the wise build bridges while the foolish build barriers. The hiding is over. The film's last gift is small and perfect: in a Wakandan village, a recovering Bucky Barnes wakes to children and quiet, and Shuri tells the White Wolf he has much more to learn.

What the ending establishes

  • Killmonger is dead by his own choice, having forced the kingdom to hear his indictment; his revolution is defeated and his grievance is adopted — the peace is governed by the loser's argument.
  • Wakanda's isolation is ended as doctrine: outreach replaces surveillance, beginning on the exact street where the old policy committed its crime.
  • The monarchy survives its own audit: T'Challa keeps the throne, the falls and the herb (one flower survived) — and changes the law they serve.
  • The Dora Milaje, the tribes and the Jabari end the film as one nation again, reassembled by mercy and marriage rather than by command.
  • The world's governments now know what Wakanda is; the film closes the age of the secret and opens every question that follows from the reveal.

What remains uncertain

Whether the world's powers will accept Wakanda's terms, whether the tribes will hold under the new doctrine, and what grows from the herb garden's ashes are left open — the film ends a reign of hiding, not the argument about what replaces it.

12 / PURPOSES OF THE STORY

Moral of The Story

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Black Panther uses a hidden utopia and its abandoned prince to examine the ethics of fortune: what the protected owe the exposed, what the living owe the truth about their fathers, and what a nation owes the child it left behind. Through a king who indicts his own ancestors, a challenger whose grievance is just and whose remedy is monstrous, and a garden burned to end a bloodline, the film argues that the only defensible use of inherited advantage is to end the conditions that made it a hiding place.

Why tell this story?

Because the superhero genre had never asked its favorite question — what do you do with power? — at the level of a whole civilization, with the bill itemized. Black Panther is the genre's statecraft film: it imagines the most fortunate nation on Earth and the most wronged man alive, gives them the same grandfather, and makes them argue. Its cultural weight rests on that construction — a blockbuster whose climax is a policy reversal and whose villain's defeat is incomplete by design.

1. Audit your ancestors while you honor them

T'Challa's second visit to the Ancestral Plane is the film's model of inheritance done right: full reverence, full candor, and a verdict. The counsel transfers directly: the doctrines you inherit — family, national, personal — are entitled to your respect and not to your obedience. A tradition that cannot survive being told it was wrong, in its own sacred place, by its own heir, was never a tradition; it was a threat with good music.

2. A true grievance does not sanctify a remedy

Killmonger's facts are the film's facts; his program is its catastrophe. The distinction is the story's central discipline: an accurate account of injury licenses repair, not empire — aid, not arms. Whenever a wronged man's plan requires becoming the thing that wronged him, with better weapons and a sadder story, the film's answer is the one T'Challa finally embodies: adopt the grievance, refuse the method, and build the outreach center instead of the arsenal.

3. Concealment is where institutions sin

T'Chaka's killing of N'Jobu is presented as a split-second act; his cover-up is the reign's true crime — twenty-four years of compounded interest on one buried night, paid by a boy in Oakland. The film's institutional lesson is exact: organizations rarely fall for the act; they fall for the filing. Whatever your Oakland is, the cover story is compounding, and the interest is always collected by someone you were supposed to protect.

4. Mercy is the only currency that appreciates in exile

M'Baku's arc is the film's feudal proof: the mercy shown at the falls returns as an army in the mountains and a king preserved in snow. T'Challa's reign is saved twice by the same deposit — the challenger he refused to kill, the cousin he refused to stop offering healing to. The counsel is strategic as much as moral: in a long life, the people you spare become the load-bearing members of your worst week.

5. Open the gate before the gate is stormed

Wakanda's opening, when it comes, is nearly too late and entirely self-authored — which is the film's preference and its warning. Fortresses that open on their own schedule become hosts; opened on the challenger's, they become spoils. The transferable form: whatever you are protecting by hiding — resources, knowledge, advantage, a true story — the terms of its sharing are best set by you, early, on a street in Oakland of your own choosing.

6. Build the answer on the site of the sin

The outreach center rises where N'Jobu died, not in a neutral capital: atonement, in the film's ethic, has an address. Apology is weather; construction is climate. The story's closing instruction to anyone carrying an inherited wrong is to skip the statement and buy the building — put the remedy physically where the harm is remembered, staff it with your best people, and let it outlast the news cycle of your remorse.

The moral of the story is…

Fortune that hides becomes complicity, and grievance that arms becomes empire; the film's whole wisdom is the narrow road between them — hear the indictment in full, adopt what is true in it, refuse its method, and convert the fortress into a bridge while the choice is still yours. Honor your fathers by auditing them, spare your challengers because you will need them, and never let a cover-up do your governing.

Black Panther teaches that a good man can be a king if he is willing to be corrected by everyone his crown was built to silence — the exile, the spy, the sister, the ancestors' own defendant — and that the measure of an inheritance is not what it protects but what it finally, deliberately, gives away.

The film's final question is therefore not whether Wakanda will survive being known. It is whether the audience's own hidden advantages — every shield behind which the fortunate watch — will be opened as policy, in Oakland, on purpose, or pried open later by the challenger the hiding made.
