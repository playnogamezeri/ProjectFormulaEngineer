01 / FILM IDENTITY

Overview

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Hancock is a 2008 American superhero film directed by Peter Berg, written by Vy Vincent Ngo and Vince Gilligan, and starring Will Smith — a film that arrived the same summer the Marvel Cinematic Universe began, and spent its $629.4 million worldwide gross arguing the opposite thesis: that a superhero is not a brand but a person, and that this one is a disaster. John Hancock (Smith) is an immortal, invulnerable amnesiac who has protected Los Angeles for decades in the manner of a natural disaster with good intentions: he flies drunk, lands through freeways, throws a beached whale into a sailboat and a boy bully into the stratosphere, and is rewarded with lawsuits, arrest warrants and a public that boos him on the news. His rescue of PR executive Ray Embrey (Jason Bateman) — saved from a train at the cost of the train — produces the film's engine: Ray, a failing idealist whose "All-Heart" logo no client will buy, takes Hancock on as the ultimate rebranding project, prescribing apology, surrender to outstanding warrants, prison time served with theatrical patience, and a comic-book costume. The makeover works — until the film performs the mid-story turn for which it remains famous and argued-about: Ray's wife Mary (Charlize Theron) is revealed as Hancock's own kind, his pair-bonded mate across millennia, whose proximity is draining both their powers toward mortality, and whose buried marriage to Hancock — ended by the amnesia of a 1928 Miami attack — reframes the whole comedy as a story about two immortals who have been divorcing, on instinct, for three thousand years. The film grossed $629.4 million on a $150 million budget, the fourth-highest gross of 2008 (ahead of Iron Man), on mixed reviews that have warmed considerably with time: its first half is now filed as a founding text of the revisionist superhero wave that The Boys and its kin would industrialize a decade later, and its second half as a strange, sincere myth about love as a mortal condition. It endures because its question outlasted its tonal argument: what would we actually do with a hero we could not control, insure or stand?

Place in the culture

The film's deposits are the genre's revisionist foundations: the superhero as a public-relations problem, collateral damage as an accounting line, "asshole" as the crowd's one-word review of its savior — a grammar that the following decade's darker superhero works would adopt wholesale. Its other legacy is the argument it never finished: critics split, then and now, on whether the Mary revelation deepens the comedy into myth or hijacks it, and the film's streaming-era reputation has risen steadily on the strength of the first reading. The sequel that its gross seemed to guarantee has been "in development" for over fifteen years — a delay the archive files as accidentally faithful to the material: Hancock, like his film, does his best work unfranchised.

A decade in development, two R ratings, and a last-minute director

Ngo's script (originally titled Tonight, He Comes) circulated for nearly a decade, attracting Tony Scott, Michael Mann (who stayed on as a producer), Jonathan Mostow and Gabriele Muccino before Berg took the chair with Smith attached — a development saga whose fingerprints are the film's famous two halves: Gilligan's darker, more adult draft progressively negotiated toward a summer tentpole, twice rated R by the MPAA before edits secured the PG-13, with the film's melancholy (an immortal who has forgotten everything but his own unpopularity) surviving every negotiation intact. Shot in Los Angeles in 2007, the production built its set pieces as practical civic destruction (the freeway pile-up, the train, the hospital climax) and its mythology as a single dinner-table exposition. Released July 2, 2008, the film was a global phenomenon — $629.4 million, fourth for the year — despite reviews (42% on Rotten Tomatoes) that praised the premise and Smith while faulting the tonal swerve; the audience's verdict, delivered at the box office and sustained across fifteen years of streaming revivals, has been kinder, and a sequel has been announced, staffed and stalled in cycles ever since.

03 / NARRATIVE SUMMARY

Story Synopsis

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL FULL STORY SUMMARY

The film opens with its thesis in a single chase: Hancock, woken hungover on a bench by a child, pursues fleeing gang suspects through Los Angeles in a flight path that totals cars, a freeway sign and several million dollars of civic infrastructure — the suspects delivered, the city furious, the news anchors reading the damage estimate like a weather report. Hancock's file, as the city keeps it: an amnesiac immortal, found in a Miami hospital eighty years ago with two movie tickets and no memory (he named himself off the John Hancock signature he was asked for), who has been saving Los Angeles, at ruinous expense and in worsening spirits, ever since. The turn comes on a train track: Ray Embrey, a PR man whose car is stuck on the rails, is saved by Hancock at the cost of the train — and Ray, alone in the crowd, says thank you. His pitch, delivered over his wife Mary's skepticism at their own dinner table, is a full rehabilitation: Hancock will apologize to the city, surrender to his outstanding warrants, serve his time like a penitent, and let Los Angeles discover it misses him; then emerge, costumed and courteous, to a hero's welcome. The prison sequence is the film's comic centerpiece — Hancock patient, enormous, declining the inmates' provocations with a therapist's calm and one unforgettable anatomical threat held in reserve — and the rehabilitation works on schedule: crime rises, the city clamors, and the police chief calls him out to a bank robbery in progress, where the new Hancock — precise, courteous, complimenting hostages — saves the day and loses a hand's worth of public goodwill to the robber Red Parker, whose amputated hand becomes the film's ticking grudge. It is in the afterglow, at the Embreys' home, that the film turns: Mary, alone with Hancock, responds to his drunken pass not with outrage but with a warning and a demonstration — she throws him through a wall with the strength of his own kind, and the mythology arrives in her kitchen: they are the last of a created race, built in pairs across millennia — gods, angels, heroes, depending on the century's word — who grow mortal as they converge, who have been husband and wife, and whose every past approach has ended with one saving the other at the price of everything. In 1928, in Miami, an attack left Hancock with a fractured skull and no memory; Mary, following the pair's oldest instinct, walked away so that at least one of them would keep the powers — and has been walking away, through lifetimes, ever since.

The final act prices the revelation. Proximity is already taxing them: Hancock, shot intervening in a liquor-store robbery, bleeds — the film's most quietly shocking image — and heals slowly in the hospital where Red Parker, escaped and rearmed, arrives with accomplices to finish the city's hero at his most mortal. Mary, drawn by the bond, comes to the hospital and is shot herself; the two immortals bleed out in adjoining rooms while Ray — who has learned, in one evening, his wife's entire buried history — holds the family line, and Hancock makes the pair's oldest decision in its newest form: he drags himself to the roof and throws himself away from her, distance as medicine, his heart restarting hers across the widening miles as he crashes through the street below and lives. The coda is the myth domesticated into grace notes: a month later, Hancock — sober, suited, courteous — takes Ray's call from New York, where he has relocated to give Mary her mortal life with her family; Red is dealt with in prison, permanently, off-screen in a post-credits accounting; Aaron has his hero's phone number; and the Moon, in the final shot, wears Ray's All-Heart logo painted across its face — the PR man's unsellable emblem, installed in the sky by the only client who ever bought it, as a thank-you visible from his family's street. The hero is still an exile. He is simply, at last, a grateful one.

The full narrative summary is an editorial paraphrase, not dialogue or a scene-by-scene transcript.

04 / STORY STRUCTURE

Story & Chronology

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

The film unfolds across one Los Angeles summer, over a backstory of three thousand years. The sequence below follows the film's narrative order.

Act I — The hated hero and the pitch

A rescue that totals a train, a thank-you no one else offers — and a PR man's prescription: apologize, surrender, serve the time.

Act II — The makeover and the revelation

A costume, a compliment for every hostage, a severed hand's grudge — and a wife, in her own kitchen, throwing the hero through a wall.

Act III — Mortality and the distance

Blood in a liquor store, a hospital under siege — and an immortal saving his pair the only way his kind ever has: by leaving, at speed, through the roof.

05 / CHARACTER DATABASE

Character Database

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Search the principal characters documented in this film. Roles and motivations below summarize their function in the 2008 movie.

THE HERO

John Hancock

Will Smith. An immortal amnesiac — three thousand years old, eighty years into a life he cannot remember starting — whose superhuman strength, flight and invulnerability have curdled, under decades of ingratitude, into alcoholism and contempt: he saves Los Angeles the way weather saves a coastline. Smith plays him as the decade's most original superhero performance — the charisma deliberately withheld, the hangover and the hurt worn openly — and Hancock's function is the story's portrait of power without a self: a man with a god's résumé and no past, whose rehabilitation is therefore not a makeover but a manufacture, the building of a person (apologies, patience, gratitude, distance) where the amnesia left a vacancy. The archive files him as the revisionist era's founding hero: a superman whose kryptonite is that nobody likes him, and whose greatest feat in the film is a compliment delivered to a hostage.

THE PAIR

Mary Embrey

Charlize Theron. Ray's wife and Aaron's mother — and, beneath that biography, the last other of Hancock's kind: his pair across three thousand years, his wife in lifetime after lifetime, who has spent the modern era in hiding inside a mortal life she chose with her eyes open. Theron plays her as the film's buried second lead — the warmth in the Embrey home revealed, scene by scene, as an immortal's performance of ordinariness so complete it fooled her own family — and Mary's function is the story's portrait of the greater sacrifice misfiled as the lesser one: she has given up godhood, repeatedly, in installments, to let him keep his; the walking away that looks like abandonment is the pair's oldest act of love. The archive files her as the decade's most consequential secret identity: a suburban mother who is also a god, keeping both facts in the same kitchen.

THE BELIEVER

Ray Embrey

Jason Bateman. A failing PR executive of unshakeable idealism — inventor of the All-Heart logo, a charity brand no client will touch — whose career is dying of the same sincerity that makes him, alone in Los Angeles, say thank you to Hancock and mean it. Bateman plays him as the film's moral engine disguised as a schlemiel, and Ray's function is the story's portrait of faith as a professional skill: he rehabilitates a god with the tools of a press release because he genuinely believes the product — that people, given the chance to miss their hero, will; that gratitude, like its opposite, is a public habit that can be retrained. The archive files him as the decade's most effective mortal in a superhero film: a man whose superpower is a pitch meeting, and whose logo ends the film painted on the Moon.

THE SON

Aaron Embrey

Jae Head. Ray and Mary's young son — the Embrey household's truth-teller, a boy whose admiration for Hancock is uncomplicated by the city's accounting and whose questions (about the hero's past, his loneliness, his prospects) file the audience's own. Head plays him with a directness that disarms every adult in the film, and Aaron's function is the story's portrait of the next generation's verdict: children have not yet learned to price a rescue by its damage report, and Aaron's fandom is the city's future opinion, arriving early. The archive files him as the decade's most useful child in a superhero film: the one person whose thank-you is never strategic, and the keeper, at the end, of the hero's phone number.

THE GRUDGE

Kenneth "Red" Parker Jr.

Eddie Marsan. A bank robber of theatrical vanity — the heist's hostage-negotiating peacock — whose hand Hancock severs (cleanly, surgically, as an alternative to his head) in the course of the hero's public relaunch, and who converts the mutilation into the film's most durable human emotion: a small man's total, organizing hatred. Marsan plays him with a snarl that keeps curdling into self-pity, and Red's function is the story's portrait of collateral damage with a memory: he is what the old Hancock made by the hundreds — an enemy manufactured by carelessness — and his hospital siege is the old style's invoice arriving at the new hero's most mortal hour. The archive files him as the decade's most human supervillain: no powers, no plan worth the name, just a hook for a hand and a grievance with perfect recall.

THE MUSCLE

Man Mountain

David Mattey. Red's enormous accomplice in the hospital assault — a wall of a man whose role in the finale is to be the physical scale against which Hancock's new mortality is measured: the hero who once threw whales now bleeds, staggers and fights a large mortal at a disadvantage, in a corridor. Mattey plays him in near-silence, and Man Mountain's function is the story's portrait of the action genre's oldest unit of measurement — the big man in the climax — repurposed as a gauge of the hero's diminishment: every labored blow Hancock lands on him is the pairs' mythology expressed in choreography. The archive files him as the decade's most eloquent heavy: a mountain whose entire performance is the altitude the hero has lost.

THE EXTINCT

The Pairs

Never seen. Hancock and Mary's kind: beings created in pairs across the ancient world — called gods, angels, heroes by the centuries that housed them — each pair invulnerable apart and mortal together, dwindling across history (by battle, by convergence, by time) until two remain. The pairs' function is the story's portrait of mythology as a population problem: every legend in the film's backstory is a census record, and the romance at its center is also a species' endangerment — two immortals whose love is, mechanically, their extinction. The archive files them as the decade's most economical mythology: an entire pantheon, with its theology of paired mortality, delivered in one kitchen-table exposition and never contradicted by a frame of the film.

THE CAMPAIGN

The All-Heart

Ray's unsellable creation: a red heart logo and a proposition — that corporations give a product away with every sale, for the world's poor — which every client in Los Angeles has declined, and which Hancock, alone, adopts: painted, at the end, across the face of the Moon. The All-Heart's function is the story's portrait of the idealist's asset correctly priced at last: worthless in every boardroom, priceless as a thank-you from a god — the film's whole argument about value (gratitude's economy versus the damage report's) compressed into one red emblem in the sky. The archive files it as the decade's most successful failed logo: a brand that cleared no focus group and ended up visible, nightly, to half the Earth.

THE WOUND

Miami, 1928

Never shown in full. The attack — a mugging, a fractured skull, a hospital ward where a man with two tickets to a movie he never saw wakes with no name — that divided the pair for the modern era: Hancock's amnesia begins there, and Mary's long withdrawal (the kind's instinct: separate, so that one keeps the power to protect) is dated from its ward. The incident's function is the story's portrait of the origin story as an injury report: no radioactive spider, no exploding planet — a head wound, a stranger's kindness, a signature copied for a name. The archive files 1928 as the decade's most human superhero origin: a god, mugged in Miami, who has been introducing himself by the signature he borrowed ever since.

THE VENUE

The Prison

Hancock's chosen address for the rehabilitation's central act: a cell, accepted voluntarily, where the most powerful being in California serves his warrants among men who test the new patience daily — and where the city's opinion is reversed, in his absence, by the crime statistics. The prison's function is the story's portrait of surrender as the strongest public language: the hero who cannot be arrested choosing arrest is the campaign's whole argument in one image — accountability as a spectacle, staged by its subject. The archive files the prison as the decade's most effective PR venue in a superhero film: a cell from which a god conducted a comeback, armed with a basketball, a pudding cup and one anatomical threat, held in reserve.

THE CLIENT

The City of Los Angeles

The film's collective character: a metropolis that keeps its hero the way it keeps its weather — as a topic of complaint — suing him, booing him, itemizing his damage on the evening news, and missing him, on Ray's schedule, the moment he withdraws his services. The city's function is the story's portrait of the public as a relationship partner with a recognizable pathology: it takes the rescues as an entitlement, prices them as a nuisance, and learns their value only in the hero's absence — the oldest story in the superhero genre, told here as municipal accounting. The archive files Los Angeles as the decade's most honestly portrayed superhero city: a client who fires its savior quarterly, rehires him at every emergency, and is learning — slowly, on camera — to say thank you.

THE THANK-YOU

The Moon

The film's final image and address: Earth's satellite, wearing the All-Heart across its face in red — Hancock's parting gift to the Embreys, painted from a distance he now keeps permanently, visible from the street where the mortal half of his pair is raising her son. The Moon's function is the story's portrait of the pair's oldest arrangement at its most gracious: proximity is poison, so love, at the end, is conducted at orbital range — a message in the sky, renewed nightly, from the exile to the family. The archive files it as the decade's most romantic superhero image: a heart on the Moon, filed under public relations, legible as what it is — a god's forwarding address, and his signature, at last, in his own hand.

06 / THE EMBREYS, THE IMMORTALS & LOS ANGELES

The Embreys, the Immortals & Los Angeles

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
THE HOUSEHOLD

The Embreys

A PR man, a hidden god and a boy — the film's model family, assembled (unknown to two of its members) across a fault line of three thousand years: their dinners, school runs and hospital vigils are the mortal life that Mary chose and that Hancock's arrival keeps endangering by proximity alone. The Embreys' function is the story's portrait of the ordinary as an achievement: this family's breakfasts are a goddess's life's work, defended with a ferocity the film gradually reveals as the strongest force in it. The household ends the film intact and in possession of the truth, the archive files it as the decade's most quietly extraordinary family: a home where the mother is a god, the father is a believer and the son has a superhero's phone number.

THE KIND

The immortals

A created race of paired beings — the film's entire supernatural population, past and present, amounting to two survivors and a kitchen-table history: gods to some centuries, angels to others, heroes to the rest, now filed in Los Angeles as a nuisance and a homemaker. The kind's function is the story's portrait of mythology at its end of days: not a pantheon in its glory but a pair in their kitchen, negotiating a mortgage's worth of ordinary life against three millennia of convergence and loss. The kind ends the film at its chosen distance, the archive files it as the decade's most domesticated mythology: two gods, one restraining geography, and a heart maintained at orbital range.

THE PUBLIC

Los Angeles & its institutions

The city entire — its police (who summon Hancock like a utility), its courts (whose warrants he finally honors), its news (which narrates him as a damage estimate), its crowds (whose one-word review is unprintable) — the institutional weather in which a hero's reputation is made and unmade. The public's function is the story's portrait of gratitude as a civic system: individually felt, collectively withheld, and trainable — Ray's whole campaign is a proof that a city's manners, like a hero's, are a practice rather than a nature. The city ends the film applauding on cue and meaning some of it, the archive files it as the decade's most improvable superhero public: a metropolis in rehabilitation, mirroring its hero's.

THE OPPOSITION

Red's crews

The film's human criminality, in two registers: the bank crew of the relaunch (professional, costumed, theatrical) and the hospital assault team (desperate, vengeful, led by a man with a hook) — the opposition a weakened god must face at mortal scale. The crews' function is the story's portrait of crime as the hero's externalized past: every enemy in the film was manufactured by the old Hancock's carelessness (a severed hand, a humiliated robber) and returns, in the new one's most vulnerable hour, as the old style's invoice. The crews end the film imprisoned or dead, the archive files them as the decade's most thematically literate villains: a grudge with a hook, collecting on a hero who no longer bounces bullets.

Two rehabilitations, one city

The film's factional design is a mirror campaign: Ray rehabilitates Hancock's image while the plot rehabilitates Los Angeles's manners, each party's progress measured by the other's — the hero learns to say sorry and thank you in the same weeks the city learns to say them back. The immortals stand outside the campaign as its stakes: beneath the PR story runs the older one, in which the city's whole drama is a season in two gods' three-thousand-year negotiation of distance. The archive files the design as the story's durable insight: a superhero film whose real subject is not the hero's powers but his reviews — and whose reviews, in the end, are a referendum on the reviewers.

07 / WORLD & SETTING

World & Setting

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Los Angeles — the city that bills its savior

A sunlit, congested, litigious Los Angeles — freeways to land on, trains to derail, a bench for a hero's hangovers — where superhumanity is a municipal fact of long standing and has been fully absorbed into the city's systems of complaint: insurance, news cycles, arrest warrants. The city's function is the story's argument in geography: heroism here is not rare or new; it is infrastructure, with infrastructure's fate — taken for granted, sued when it malfunctions, missed only in outage. The archive files this Los Angeles as the decade's most plausible superhero setting: a town that has had eighty years to get used to its god, and has used them to develop a sophisticated, total ingratitude — the most American civic condition the genre ever portrayed.

The Embrey home & the hospital — the myth's two rooms

The film's intimate poles: a suburban kitchen where a goddess has hidden in plain sight (the room where the mythology is finally spoken, across a table, in whispers) and a hospital where the myth is priced in blood (the ward where two immortals bleed like anyone, and where the pair's decision must be made at corridor speed). The rooms' function is the story's portrait of scale: three thousand years of gods and heroes resolve, in the end, into a kitchen confession and a hospital roof — the largest story the genre tells, filed in its smallest rooms. The archive files them as the decade's most efficient mythic settings: a dinner table that holds a pantheon, and a rooftop from which a god departs the way his kind always has — alone, upward, and for love.

08 / THE PAIRS, THE AMNESIA & THE ALL-HEART

The Pairs, the Amnesia & the All-Heart

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

The pairs — mortality as a function of love

The mythology's single law: the kind are created in pairs, invulnerable apart, and mortal in convergence — the closer and longer a pair unites, the more human (aging, woundable, killable) both become, until the union that completes them also ends them. The law's function is the story's portrait of love as a literal mortal condition: every romance in the film's three-thousand-year backstory is a countdown, and every separation a life-extending mercy — the cruelest physics in the superhero genre, delivered as a love story's terms. The archive files the law as the decade's most elegant superhero mechanism: a mythology in which the hero's greatest vulnerability is not a mineral but his wife's proximity, and in which distance is the highest form of devotion available.

The amnesia — a self with the first chapters missing

Hancock's condition: eighty years of continuous memory beginning in a Miami hospital — no childhood, no origin, no Mary — and three thousand years behind a locked door that a fractured skull closed in 1928. The amnesia's function is the story's portrait of identity as an editorial problem: Hancock is not a good man or a bad one but an unfinished one, and the film's rehabilitation is the construction of a self in the archive's absence — manners, duties and gratitudes laid down like new memories over the vacancy. The archive files the amnesia as the decade's most functional superhero wound: a hero who cannot remember being loved, and must therefore be taught gratitude as a foreign language, by a PR man, in his eighties.

The rehabilitation — apology as a technology

Ray's program, itemized: a public apology, a voluntary surrender to warrants, time served without privilege, a costume (the eagle-emblazoned suit, comic-book correct), a courtesy protocol (compliment the rescued; thank the police), and a patience that lets absence do the persuading. The program's function is the story's portrait of reputation as a manufactured good — with the film's honesty about the manufacture (it is a campaign, and everyone including Hancock knows it) balanced by its finding: the performed virtues, practiced long enough, become the real ones, and the costume's wearer grows into it. The archive files the rehabilitation as the decade's most complete superhero origin: a hero made not by a spider or a serum but by a surrender, a sentence and a stylist.

The damage ledger — heroism, invoiced

The city's accounting system for its hero: every rescue priced (the freeway, the train, the whale, the satellite Hancock once threw a car into orbit against), every invoice read aloud on the news, the hero's public worth computed as a net of lives saved minus property destroyed. The ledger's function is the story's portrait of gratitude's opposite as a bureaucracy: the city is not ungrateful in its heart but in its bookkeeping, and Ray's campaign is at bottom an accounting reform — a new ledger in which the hero's column includes his apologies and the city's includes its thanks. The archive files the ledger as the decade's most quietly radical superhero device: a damage estimate, read on the evening news, as the true measure of a civilization's manners.

The archive files them as the decade's most quietly mythic mother and son: a boy doing his homework in a kitchen where a god has decided, on the evidence of him, that forever was overrated.

The thank-you is the whole economy

The film's relationships all finally report to a single transaction: the thank-you — offered by Ray on a train track, withheld by a city for eighty years, painted on the Moon by a god. The archive files gratitude as the decade's most underrated superhero currency: the only payment the hero accepts, the only one the city was bankrupting itself by refusing, and the denomination in which every debt in the film is finally settled.

10 / THEMATIC ANALYSIS

Themes & Analysis

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Gratitude is a civic system — the hero and the reviewed

The film's governing theme is its economy of thanks: a hero destroyed not by a villain but by eighty years of unacknowledged service, and a city whose ingratitude is portrayed not as wickedness but as a habit — a bookkeeping practice that can be reformed, like the hero's own manners, by program and patience. The theme is worked out in the rehabilitation's mirror structure (Hancock learns apology as the city learns thanks; both performances become sincere by repetition) and in the ledger's final form, a heart on the Moon: gratitude, in the film's accounting, is the only currency that appreciates. Its proposition reaches every unreviewed worker in the audience — the maintainers, the carers, the infrastructure people of every household and firm: being taken for granted is not a verdict on your value but a system failure in your public, and it can, sometimes, be campaigned against. The reach is the story's question to every Hancock in a kitchen or an office: who is your Ray — the one person whose thank-you is unstrategic — and have you let them run your campaign, or are you still landing through freeways?

Love as a mortal condition — the pairs' law

The film's second theme is its mythology's law, which is a thesis about all love wearing a fantasy costume: union, for the pairs, is literally mortalizing — to be fully with the beloved is to become woundable, aging, finite — and every couple in the film's three-thousand-year backstory has faced the same treaty negotiation the audience's couples face in miniature: how much convergence can the selves survive? The theme is worked out in Mary's two choices (the withdrawal of 1928, which preserved him; the convergence with Ray, which is spending her) and in the finale's geometry, where love's correct action is a departure at maximum speed. Its proposition is the story's most quoted wisdom in its fandom and its most argued in its criticism: that distance, rightly kept, is not love's failure but one of its instruments — and that some bonds are best maintained at a range where both parties stay alive. The reach is the story's question to every pair, mortal or otherwise: what is your convergence currently costing, in powers — and who, in your treaty, is keeping the distance that keeps you both?

The performed self becomes the self — costume, courtesy, practice

The film's third theme is its rehabilitation's philosophy: Hancock's virtues begin as a campaign — the costume fitted like a comic book's, the courtesies scripted, the apology a press strategy — and the film's finding, delivered without cynicism, is that the performance is the mechanism: a man who practices gratitude for a summer becomes a grateful man, and the suit he was dressed in becomes his skin. The theme is worked out in the bank-robbery sequence (the scripted compliments landing as genuine grace under fire) and in the coda's unforced courtesy, where no camera is being served. Its proposition reaches every skeptic of self-improvement in the audience: the distinction between "faking it" and "making it" is largely a matter of duration — manners are morality's rehearsal, and the rehearsal, kept up, is the show. The reach is the story's question to every reluctant reformer: which virtue are you currently only performing — and what would ninety days of the performance, at Hancock's dosage, make of it?

Collateral damage has a memory — the invoice of the old style

The film's fourth theme is its villain's origin: Red's hatred is not an evil essence but a receipt — the old Hancock's carelessness, itemized in one severed hand — and the film generalizes the receipt into a law of the careless life: every shortcut past other people's dignity accrues, with interest, in a ledger kept by its victims. The theme is worked out in the city's parallel ledger (the lawsuits and warrants are Red's grievance at municipal scale) and in the rehabilitation's first article, which is not a costume but an apology — the acknowledgment that the old style's costs were real and billed to real people. Its proposition is a standing audit for every powerful person in the audience, at whatever scale their power operates: your carelessness is being recorded by its objects in a handwriting you will meet again, possibly in a hospital corridor of your own weakest season. The reach is the story's question to every fast-moving rescuer of situations: whose hand is on your ledger — and is the apology cheaper now, as the film insists, than the siege will be?

11 / FULL SPOILER CONCLUSION

Ending Explained

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL THE ENDING

The end is a departure at speed. Both immortals shot and bleeding out in the hospital, Hancock drags himself to the roof and throws himself away from Mary — the distance restarts her heart and saves her mortal life with Ray and Aaron. A month later he is in New York, sober and courteous, keeping the pair's treaty — and Ray's All-Heart logo is painted across the Moon as his thank-you.

What the ending establishes

  • The pair's law is honored in its oldest form: separation as salvation — the husband saves the wife by leaving her, and the film files the leap as the marriage's most loving act in three thousand years.
  • Mary's choice is ratified: she keeps the mortal life she purchased — husband, son, aging — with the treaty's price (Hancock's exile) paid by him, gladly, as his half of the convergence's cost.
  • Red's account is closed in the post-credits accounting: the old style's last invoice is settled in prison, off-screen, permanently — the new hero's world tidied by the system's ordinary machinery.
  • The All-Heart's installation settles the film's economy: the unsellable logo is priceless in the only market that matters — a god's gratitude, displayed nightly, over the believer's street.
  • Hancock's relocation establishes the hero's final form: not the city's resident weapon but its distant correspondent — sober, suited, on call — a god maintaining his treaty at the range of a phone call and a moon.

What remains uncertain

Whether the pair can ever converge safely, whether Mary's mortality will run its full course beside Ray, and whether Hancock's new self survives the centuries of solitude it is built for — all stand open at the film's close, the sequel that would answer them having spent fifteen years in development. Within this film, the archive files the uncertainty as the story's own last weather report: two gods, one treaty, one painted Moon — and a city, below, learning to look up and say the word.

12 / PURPOSES OF THE STORY

Moral of The Story

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Hancock uses a hated hero, a PR campaign and a three-thousand-year marriage to examine gratitude, love and the manufacture of a self: an immortal with no past building a person out of apologies, a goddess spending her eternity on a family, and a believer whose thank-you reorganizes both their lives. Through the freeway, the prison and the painted Moon, the film argues that gratitude is a trainable civic system rather than a feeling; that love, at its most devoted, sometimes keeps the distance that keeps both parties alive; and that a performed virtue, practiced at full dosage, becomes the person.

Why tell this story?

Because Berg, Ngo and Gilligan saw, in 2008, where the superhero century was heading: a world saturated with heroes would soon be asking not how they fight but how they are reviewed — and they told the review's story first, as a comedy with a myth smuggled inside it. They told it with a drunk, amnesiac, unlikable god because the question required the hardest case: if gratitude and rehabilitation can work on Hancock — sued, booed, hungover on a bench — they can work on anyone's public, including the audience's own unreviewed maintainers. The result is the revisionist superhero film's founding text, the archive files, and the only one whose final battle is won by a departure and whose final image is a thank-you note visible from space.

1. Run the campaign on yourself

Ray's program is the film's first counsel, for everyone whose reputation — at work, at home, in their own head — has become a damage report: the rehabilitation's articles are transferable in full. Apologize, specifically and in public, for the old style's itemized costs. Surrender to the outstanding warrants — the unfiled taxes of the self, the unreturned calls of the record — and serve the time without privilege or theater. Accept the costume: dress, schedule and speak as the person the campaign describes, especially on the days the description feels like a lie. And then let absence and consistency do the persuading, on the city's schedule rather than yours. The counsel's evidence is the film itself: the performance, kept up for one summer at full dosage, became the man. Your version requires no PR firm. It requires a Ray — one believer, unstrategic in their thanks — and the willingness to be their client.

2. Say the thank-you like Ray

The train track is the film's second counsel, for everyone who consumes the labor of the unreviewed — the maintainers of households, systems, parents, roads: gratitude is a practice with a technique, and Ray demonstrates it entire. Say it at the scene, to the person, in front of the crowd that is booing. Say it without a strategy (Ray's thank-you precedes his pitch by a full dinner; the pitch works because the thank-you wasn't one). And say it in the currency the thanked actually accept — Hancock takes no money and no worship; he takes acknowledgment, and a logo in the sky is merely its largest denomination. Audit your own ledger of the unthanked: the names will assemble themselves, in order of years unreviewed. Begin at the top of the list this week, at the scene, out loud.

3. Keep the treaty distance where love requires it

The pairs' law is the film's third counsel, for every bond whose convergence is costing both parties their powers — the merged identities, the finished sentences, the slow mutual diminishment filed as intimacy: some distances are not failures of love but its load-bearing structure, and the treaty's terms (range, rhythm, the sovereignties preserved) deserve the same deliberate negotiation as the union's. The counsel is a geometry audit: identify where your closest bond is making both parties mortal in the bad sense — woundable by every mood, aging at double speed — and renegotiate one degree of range, in the open, as an act of devotion rather than withdrawal. Hancock's leap off the roof is the doctrine at its most theatrical. Its domestic form is smaller and identical: the separate room, the kept friendship, the work that is yours — distance, maintained, so that both parties stay alive to love each other at range.

4. Itemize your severed hands

Red's hook is the film's fourth counsel, for everyone whose speed has ever been other people's damage: your carelessness is kept in a ledger by its objects, with interest, and the siege always arrives in your weakest season — so settle the accounts in your strong one, at the apology's price rather than the corridor's. The counsel is a restitution practice with Hancock's own syllabus: name the hands (the colleague ridden over, the friend used as a landing strip, the family charged for your emergencies), price the damage without the old style's discount, and pay it in the currency of acknowledgment first and repair where repair is possible. Some accounts will not settle; the film is honest that Red's doesn't. But the ledger's other entries — the city's, the family's, the mirror's — are waiting on exactly one article of Ray's program, and it is the first one: the apology, delivered before the warrants compel it.

5. Build the self in the archive's absence

The amnesia is the film's fifth counsel, for everyone whose past is unavailable as a guide — by loss, by estrangement, by a history that cannot be borne as an instruction manual: Hancock's method is the available one. Where memory is silent, lay down practice: the manners, duties and gratitudes of the person you are constructing, performed daily until they are indistinguishable from a nature. The counsel is a construction doctrine with the film's optimism and its patience: the self built this way is not a forgery of the lost one but a successor to it, and its evidence is cumulative — a summer of courtesies outweighs, in the final accounting, eighty years of vacancy. Keep the costume pressed. Answer for the warrants. Compliment the hostages. The archive you are writing, one kept appointment at a time, is the only past the future will consult.

6. Paint your heart where your people can see it

The Moon is the film's sixth counsel, its benediction, for everyone whose gratitude has been felt at full strength and expressed at bench-hangover scale: the thank-you should be sized to the debt and installed where its recipient lives — in the sky over their street, renewed nightly, impossible to miss and unnecessary to answer. The counsel is an expression doctrine: identify your Embreys (the believers whose unstrategic thanks carried your worst season) and pay them in the largest denomination your powers allow — not money, which Hancock never offers and Ray never wants, but the public, permanent, slightly excessive emblem: the logo in the sky, the name in the credits, the heart where the whole street can see whose it is. Gods paint moons. Mortals have smaller skies and the same instruction: make the gratitude visible from their window, and keep it lit.

The moral of the story is…

Apologize for the old style, serve the warrants, practice the courtesies until they are the person — keep the treaty distance where love requires it, and paint your gratitude where your believers can see it from their street.

Hancock teaches that a hero can be unmade by ingratitude and remade by a single thank-you, professionally administered; that love between immortals is a distance kept for each other's survival; and that a self, like a reputation, is built by performance at full dosage until the costume and the courtesy are simply the man.

The film's final question is therefore not whether you would be a good superhero — the film files that under "powers, unissued." It is who keeps your damage ledger and whether you have read it; who your Ray is and whether their campaign has your signature; and what heart, in what sky, your gratitude is currently failing to paint over the street of the people who carried you.
