01 / FILM IDENTITY

Overview

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

The Wise Guys Who Fool Around (詐痲納福, also known in English as Sweet Time Together) is a 1956 Hong Kong Cantonese comedy directed by Chun Kim, written by Cheung Wai-Kwong, produced at Diamond Hill Studio, and released February 15, 1956. It is a vehicle for Sun Ma Si-tsang (Sze Tsang Sun-Ma) — the era's beloved opera-and-screen comedian, famed for his impersonations — playing Lee Fuk Cheung, with Woo Fung as Lam Tin Chi and the fifteen-year-old Bruce Lee, in one of his busiest years, as Yeung Siu Lung (the catalogues romanize the role variously; HKMDB gives the character as 楊小龍). The surviving synopsis — IMDb's, the fullest in English — describes "a Sun Ma Si-tsang comedy with the usual masquerades and hijinks from the master," and preserves two facts that give the film its permanent value. The first is photographic: it "contains locations of the Lai Chi Kok Amusement Park in the 50's (now defunct), precious footage that shows film's role as a preserver of Hong Kong's history" — a vanished landmark, kept in a comedy's backgrounds. The second is Lee's part: he "plays a young pubescent kid developing his first taste for the opposite sex, disguising himself as an adult to watch a striptease show and even demonstrating how to become an 'Ah Fei' lady killer" — and is "also seen in sailor's outfit and in glasses speaking with a stammer, recalling a similar turn in Fist of Fury more than ten years later." That last observation, from the catalogue itself, is the film's claim on this Galaxy: the teenage Lee, in a supporting comic turn, is already stocking the toolbox — disguises, drag-age impersonation, the stammering bespectacled innocent — that the adult superstar would unpack, to immortal effect, in 1972. The picture belongs to the Sun Ma Si-tsang masquerade tradition, in which the master's impersonations (of officials, foreigners, grandees) carried the comedy and the stock company orbited him; Lee's role is the apprentice's position in that orbit, learning the trade of being other people in public. This archive documents the film from its catalogue record — credits, synopsis and locations — and marks, as with its shelf-mates, where the accessible documentation ends: no box office, no contemporary reviews and no scene-by-scene account survive in the English sources this file could reach.

The master's masquerade, the apprentice's disguises

Sun Ma Si-tsang (born Tang Kei-chen) was Cantonese culture's great impersonator — an opera star whose screen comedies were built on his transformations, and whose company Lee's own father, the opera actor Lee Hoi-chuen, moved in. A Sun Ma vehicle was therefore a family-trade production for the fifteen-year-old: the master's masquerades up front, the opera dynasty's son in the youth part, learning the same craft from inside it. Chun Kim, one of the era's most literate Cantonese directors (later of Union Film's classics), directs; Diamond Hill, the studio in the Kowloon hills that gave the district's film colony its name, produces. The location work at Lai Chi Kok Amusement Park — the era's beloved funfair on the Kowloon shore, long since demolished and buried under housing and a park — was routine for a contemporary comedy and is now the footage's archival fortune: the film preserves, in motion, a landmark that survives otherwise in stills. The archive files the production as the record's neat demonstration that a comedy's backgrounds can outvalue its foregrounds, given sixty years.
