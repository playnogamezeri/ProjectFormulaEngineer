01 / FILM IDENTITY

Overview

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Spider-Man is a 2002 American superhero film directed by Sam Raimi from a screenplay by David Koepp, based on the Marvel Comics character created by Stan Lee and Steve Ditko. Released May 3, 2002, it grossed $825 million worldwide on a $139 million budget — the third-highest-grossing film of 2002, then the most successful comic-book film ever made, and the first film in history to earn $100 million in a single weekend. Peter Parker (Tobey Maguire), a brilliant, bullied orphan raised in Queens by his Uncle Ben (Cliff Robertson) and Aunt May (Rosemary Harris), is bitten on a school trip by a genetically engineered "super-spider" and wakes with its powers scaled to a boy: strength, adhesion, webbing, and a spider-sense that hears danger coming. The film's first movement is a wish-fulfillment idyll conducted with unusual honesty about its hero's smallness — Peter humiliates his tormentor Flash Thompson, designs a costume, and enters a wrestling cage against Bonesaw McGraw to win $3,000 for a car to impress Mary Jane Watson (Kirsten Dunst), the girl next door he has loved since childhood. Cheated of the prize by the promoter, Peter lets the man's robber run past him — "I missed the part where that's my problem" — and minutes later finds Ben dying on the sidewalk, shot by that same thief: the superhero genre's founding debt, incurred in a single act of spite. Ben's doctrine — "with great power comes great responsibility" — becomes the film's constitution as Peter, guilt-forged into Spider-Man, begins rescuing a city that J. Jonah Jameson's (J.K. Simmons) Daily Bugle promptly brands a menace. The antagonist is the mirror across the generational divide: Norman Osborn (Willem Dafoe), father of Peter's best friend Harry (James Franco), a weapons magnate who tests his own performance-enhancing formula on himself to save his company and emerges doubled — the Green Goblin, a cackling id in armor who courts Spider-Man as a fellow exception ("the one thing they love more than a hero is to see a hero fall") and, learning his identity, wages the war through his family: May hospitalized, MJ seized, a tram car of children and the woman he loves held over the East River in the genre's defining dilemma. Peter chooses both — saves the children and MJ with the city's help, a barge horn and New Yorkers pelting the Goblin in the film's famous post-9/11 civic embrace — and wins the final fight in an abandoned building, where Norman, unmasked, begs for mercy and dies by his own glider's treachery, extracting a deathbed promise ("don't tell Harry") that poisons the peace. The film closes at Ben's grave, where MJ confesses her love and Peter — holding the doctrine above his own happiness — declines her, walking out of the cemetery into the vocation's loneliness: "This is my gift. This is my curse. Who am I? I'm Spider-Man."

Place in the culture

Alongside X-Men (2000), the film founded the modern superhero era — proving a comic-book property could open like a cultural event while remaining, at heart, a neighborhood melodrama about a boy, a girl and a rent-paying aunt. Its upside-down kiss became one of the century's most imitated images, and its earnest, pre-ironic tone set the template that a decade of franchise filmmaking would both inherit and react against.

Twenty-five years to the screen

Spider-Man's film rights wandered for decades through Cannon Films, Carolco and a famously unmade James Cameron treatment before Sony consolidated them in 1999; Raimi — a lifelong fan who had grown up with the character — won the director's chair over bigger names and shot the film in 2001 with John Dykstra's visual-effects team inventing "webslinging" cinematography for the swings through a digital New York. The production's most famous casualty was civic: the original teaser — a helicopter webbed between the Twin Towers — was withdrawn after September 11, and the finished film's embrace of New York (the bridge crowd's "you mess with one of us, you mess with all of us") was widely read as the city's own appearance in its defense. Maguire, cast against the era's action-hero type, anchored the film in a nebbish sincerity that became its signature. Released May 3, 2002, it opened to $114.8 million — a record that reset the industry's calendar around superhero summers — and was nominated for Academy Awards for Best Sound and Best Visual Effects.

03 / NARRATIVE SUMMARY

Story Synopsis

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL FULL STORY SUMMARY

The film's first act is a power fantasy with its conscience left in: the bite, the cafeteria tray catch (MJ saved, Flash humiliated, and Peter frightened by his own reflexes), the rooftop leaps and the alley experiments — Raimi stages the discovery of power as pure adolescent joy, a boy play-testing godhood against brick walls. The wrestling sequence is the idyll's priced ticket: Bonesaw, the rigged prize ($100 for three minutes' work against the promised $3,000), and the elevator's long ride down beside the thief he could have tripped — the film holds on Peter's face and the promoter's shrug until the audience understands that the genre's most famous origin is not a bite but a grudge, exercised once, at the worst possible address. Ben's death scene is played at a whisper on a public sidewalk, and the carjacker chase that follows — Peter's first swing through the city in pursuit of the killer, the warehouse confrontation, the face recognized — completes the doctrine's installation: the man he let go killed the man who taught him the sentence, and the film never lets either of them, Peter or the audience, file it under accident. Graduation, the Bugle, the first rescues (a baby from a fire, MJ from a mugging — the upside-down kiss in the rain, the rescued girl thanking a mask) build the hero's double ledger: the city saved daily, the rent unpaid, the girl courted by his best friend, the photographs of himself sold to the editor who defames him.

Norman's parallel descent is the film's second engine: the board's ultimatum, the formula's green vapor, the murdered scientist, and Dafoe's mirror performances — Norman and the Goblin conversing in a penthouse like a landlord and his tenant from hell — give the blockbuster a chamber-play of possession at its center. The Goblin's campaign escalates by intimacy: the Thanksgiving unmasking-by-gravy (a cut hand, a dropped roll, a suspicion confirmed by a bloodied tissue), the fire set for May mid-prayer, and the bridge's dilemma — MJ in one hand, a tram of children in the other — resolved not by the hero's strength alone but by the city's: a barge, a horn, and New Yorkers raining debris on the Goblin in the film's thesis of civic reciprocity. The final fight is the trilogy's most brutal — a derelict building, a beating administered at human tempo — and ends with the villain's two masks falling in sequence: Norman begging "I've been like a father to you," the glider rising behind his back, Peter's sense screaming the warning the audience has been trained to hear, and the impalement staged as the formula's own treachery collecting its inventor. The deathbed clause — "don't tell Harry" — is the film's last and coldest mechanism: a father's shame converted into a son's future vendetta, filed in the hero's keeping. At the cemetery Peter pays the doctrine's final installment in the only currency left: MJ's offered love, declined in silence, the girl walking away from the boy she will never know saved her — the vocation's price, itemized, as the credits' city opens beneath a swinging figure who has chosen the bill.

10 / THEMATIC ANALYSIS

Themes & Analysis

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Responsibility — the doctrine and its price list

The film's governing theme is Ben's sentence, treated not as a slogan but as an accounting system: power creates obligations automatically, refusals are invoiced immediately, and the vocation is a lifelong repayment schedule on a debt incurred in one elevator ride. The theme is worked out in the plot's every price (the rent, the girl, the friend's father, the name in the headlines) until the audience holds the film's doctrine of power: it is not a gift or a curse but a liability, and maturity is the decision to keep paying. Its proposition reaches every newly powerful person in the audience — the promoted, the platformed, the suddenly visible: your elevator moment is coming, and it will not announce itself as the important one. The reach is the story's question to every holder of a fresh power: which stranger's crime are you currently waving past?

The double — Norman's mirror

The film's second theme is doubling as the villain's form: Norman and the Goblin as a man and his unfiltered ambition, conducting their possession as a domestic arrangement — while Peter's doubling is the same structure under opposite management, a boy whose second self is his conscience's employee. The theme is worked out in the mirror scenes' horror grammar until the audience holds the film's doctrine of the alter ego: everyone in the film has two tenants; the hero is the one whose second self works for the first's doctrine, and the villain is the one whose second self has evicted it. Its proposition reaches every professional with a public and private register in the audience: audit which of your selves is signing the decisions. The reach is the story's question to every doubled reader: when your two voices argue in the penthouse, which one holds the glider?

The city as co-author — the bridge referendum

The film's third theme is civic reciprocity: a hero defamed by the press and defended, at the decisive moment, by the public itself — the barge, the horn, the debris — in a sequence inseparable from its post-9/11 moment. The theme is worked out against Jameson's campaign (the manufactured menace versus the witnessed one) until the audience holds the film's doctrine of the public: a constituency that has been rescued keeps its own ledger, and it does not read the tabloids. Its proposition reaches every defamed practitioner in the audience — the official, the officer, the founder under headline: your true electorate is the people at your bridge. The reach is the story's question to every menace of the headlines: who, at your tram car, will throw the debris — and have you given them a reason?

The beloved as the price — MJ and the grave

The film's fourth theme is its ending's refusal: the hero, offered the love the whole film has angled toward, declines it in the doctrine's name — because loving him, knowingly, would make her a target, and loving her secretly would make her a dupe. The theme is worked out in the alley kiss's inversion (she may have the hero's tenderness only without his name) until the audience holds the film's doctrine of the vocation's celibacy: some callings are paid for in the specific currency of the beloved's unknowing years. Its proposition reaches every keeper of a dangerous secret in the audience: your silence is a price somebody else is paying without an invoice. The reach is the story's question to every Spider-Man at a graveside: is the doctrine being served — or is the fear?

11 / FULL SPOILER CONCLUSION

Ending Explained

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL THE ENDING

Peter saves both MJ and the tram car with the city's help, then defeats the Goblin in a final fight. Norman, unmasked, pleads for mercy while his glider rises behind him; Peter dodges, and Norman is impaled by his own weapon. With his dying breath he asks Peter not to tell Harry. At Ben's grave, MJ confesses her love; Peter, bound by his responsibility, gently rejects her and embraces his life as Spider-Man.

What the ending establishes

  • The bridge files the city's verdict as delivered: the menace of the headlines is the defender of the tram, and the public says so with debris.
  • Norman's death files the formula's treachery as its final audit: the seized power collects its seizer, by his own machine, mid-plea.
  • The deathbed promise files mercy as the saga's escrow: Harry's peace is purchased with a secret that guarantees his war.
  • The graveside refusal files the doctrine's price as the beloved: the vocation's first full installment is the girl.
  • The final swing files the curse and the gift as one word: "This is my gift. This is my curse."

What remains uncertain

How long Peter's refusal can hold against MJ's recognition (she touches his face in the final scene with the air of a woman solving something); what Harry will find in his father's house; and whether the doctrine's price list ever balances — all stand open at the film's close (the story continued in two sequels). Within this film, the archive files the uncertainty as the story's own last word on its theme: the debt is permanent, the payments are the life, and the life, swinging out over the city, is not presented as a tragedy.

12 / PURPOSES OF THE STORY

Moral of The Story

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Spider-Man uses a bite, a burglar and a bridge to examine power, doubling and the public: a boy whose one spiteful refusal prices his whole life; a magnate whose seized power evicts his conscience; and a city that keeps its own ledger beneath the headlines. Through the elevator, the mirror and the graveside refusal, the film argues that power is a liability whose obligations arrive automatically; that the second self must work for the doctrine or it will evict it; and that the people you have actually rescued are the only electorate that finally matters.

Why tell this story?

Because Lee and Ditko's 1962 invention — the superhero as a broke, guilty teenager — was the genre's conscience from its birth, and Raimi understood that filming it faithfully, without irony, would read as revolution in an ironic age. He told it as a neighborhood melodrama with a glider in it. The result is the modern superhero era's founding text, the archive files: a blockbuster whose climax is a boy declining happiness at a grave.

1. Never wave the burglar past

The elevator is the film's first counsel, for every holder of a small, fresh power — the new manager, the witness, the bystander with a phone: the refusal that feels like a private grudge will be invoiced at an address you love, at a rate you would not have believed, within the hour. The counsel is a responsibility doctrine with the sidewalk as its standard: Peter's sentence is not that he failed to be a hero; it is that he chose, once, to be a creditor instead. When your moment offers you the spite discount, price it honestly. It is the most expensive bargain in this film, and it is offered daily.

2. Keep the second self employed by the doctrine

Norman's mirror is the film's second counsel, for every doubled professional — the founder and the pitch, the official and the operator, the artist and the brand: your second self is not going away; the only question is its management. The counsel is a governance doctrine with the penthouse as its boardroom: Peter's alter ego works for Ben's sentence; Norman's works for the quarter. Audit your mirror conversations. If one of your voices is proposing to eliminate the other's friends, the eviction has already begun, and the glider is already built.

3. Serve the bridge, not the headline

Jameson's campaign and the barge's horn are the film's third counsel, for every practitioner defamed in public — the official under investigation, the company under a headline, the professional under a thread: your true electorate is the people who have stood at your tram car, and their ledger is kept in a currency headlines cannot print. The counsel is a reputation doctrine with the debris as its dividend: the city defends Peter because Peter has been defending the city, nightly, unphotographed except by himself. Do the rescues. The bridge takes care of itself, and it outvotes the Bugle.

4. Price the promise before you swear it

"Don't tell Harry" is the film's fourth counsel, for every keeper of a deathbed confidence — the executor, the friend, the child of the scandal: a promise extracted at a deathbed is a contract signed under the maximum possible duress, and its installments will be collected from third parties who never consented. The counsel is a mercy doctrine with Harry's future as its invoice: Peter's silence is kind, and it is also the vendetta's incorporation papers. When the dying ask you to hold a weapon-shaped secret, consider — even at the graveside, especially there — what the holding will cost the person it is aimed at. Some mercies are just deliveries.

5. Let the doctrine be a gift on the days it can

The final swing is the film's fifth counsel, for every debtor of a lifelong obligation — the caregiver, the founder in year nine, the professional in the permanent on-call: the account does not close, so the art is in the carrying — Peter's last line holds "gift" and "curse" in one breath, and the film's final image is not a slumped figure but a swing at full extension over a morning city. The counsel is an endurance doctrine with the skyline as its evidence: the payments are the life, so the life must be built to be worth paying in. Find the swing inside your sentence. It is the difference between serving it and being served by it.

6. Tell the girl, eventually

The graveside refusal is the film's sixth counsel, its benediction, offered against the film's own hero, for every keeper of a protective secret — the parent, the patient, the agent of any bureau: Peter's silence is noble, and the film loves him, and the sequels will present the bill; a beloved paying for a doctrine she has never heard is an injustice wearing a vocation's clothes. The counsel is a disclosure doctrine with MJ's face in the final scene as its standard: she is already solving it, with her fingertips, at a funeral. The people who love us are detectives. Plan to be caught by yours on your own terms, before the terms are a glider's.

The moral of the story is…

Never wave the burglar past; keep the second self employed by the doctrine; serve the bridge, not the headline — and price every deathbed promise before you swear it.

Spider-Man teaches that power is a liability whose obligations arrive automatically and whose refusals are invoiced at the addresses we love; that a life of repayment can still be swung through at full extension; and that the public which has actually been rescued keeps the only ledger that finally matters.

The film's final question is therefore not whether you could survive the bite — the film files that under "field trips." It is which burglar you are currently waving past; which of your two voices holds the glider; and whether the people at your bridge, when it comes, will reach for the debris or the popcorn.
