01 / FILM IDENTITY

Overview

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Kill Bill: Volume 2 is a 2004 American martial arts revenge film written and directed by Quentin Tarantino, the second half of a single production split for release six months after Volume 1. Released April 16, 2004, it grossed $152.2 million worldwide on a $30 million budget. Where the first volume was a kinetic, eastern-facing barrage, the second is its western-facing mirror: talkier, dustier, crueler in quieter ways, and built around the saga's two withheld facts — what happened at the wedding chapel, and what the Bride's name is. Beatrix Kiddo (Uma Thurman) — her name finally spoken here, in a schoolyard flashback's roll call — has crossed O-Ren Ishii and Vernita Green off her list; remaining are Budd (Michael Madsen), Bill's brother, a washed-up Viper working as a strip-club bouncer in a desert trailer; Elle Driver (Daryl Hannah), the one-eyed Viper who hates Beatrix with a connoisseur's completeness; and Bill himself (David Carradine), the Snake Charmer, her mentor, lover, and the father of the child she believes dead. The film opens at the beginning: the Two Pines wedding rehearsal, played at last in full — the groom, the reverend (Bo Svenson), the organist Rufus (Samuel L. Jackson) — and Bill's arrival on the chapel steps for a conversation of perfect, lethal courtesy before he gives the order that fills the chapel with his squad's gunfire and ends with his own bullet in her head. In the present, Budd — warned by Bill — ambushes Beatrix with a shotgun of rock salt and buries her alive in a borrowed coffin, in a sequence the film plays in near-darkness and coffin acoustics: her escape, punching through the lid at the distance Pai Mei trained her knuckles for, is intercut with the film's great flashback, the Cruel Tutelage — the legendary, misogynist, ancient master (Gordon Liu) who tormented the young Beatrix into greatness and secretly taught her the one technique he taught no one: the Five Point Palm Exploding Heart Technique. Budd's triumph is collected by Elle, who arrives to buy Beatrix's Hanzo sword and pays with a suitcase containing a black mamba — the film's most cold-blooded transaction, snake for sword — and Elle's own account is settled in Budd's trailer, in the saga's ugliest fight: no choreography of honor, just two women, a trailer's close quarters, an eye for an eye, literally, and a snake left as the trailer's new tenant. The road to Bill runs through Esteban Vihaio (Michael Parks), a retired pimp in Mexico who raised Bill and betrays his address out of an old man's aesthetics — he would have liked her to find him; Bill would have wanted it — and the final act is the diptych's still center: Bill's hacienda, a truth-serum dart ("the most honest interrogation in cinema," the film wagers), a Superman monologue that is Tarantino's thesis on his heroine, the revelation that B.B. (Perla Haney-Jardine) is alive and has been waiting up for her mother — and then a fight of four moves, a scabbard trap, and five fingertips. Bill, granted the film's most courteous death, makes his peace, straightens his jacket, takes five steps, and dies; and the saga closes the next morning in a motel bathroom, Beatrix weeping on the floor with gratitude and grief in equal measure, before mother and daughter drive out of the film eating breakfast and watching cartoons — "the lioness has rejoined her cub, and all is right in the jungle."

Place in the culture

Together the two volumes constitute Tarantino's most beloved work and a landmark of the revenge film: Volume 2's reputation has grown past its predecessor's in critical esteem, its Pai Mei chapter a definitive homage to Shaw Brothers cinema (Liu, a Shaw legend, plays the master he spent a career opposing), its buried-alive sequence a benchmark of sustained dread, and its finale — a killing conducted as a reconciliation — the standard against which the genre's endings are measured. Carradine's Bill, withheld almost entirely from Volume 1, became the actor's defining late role, and the Five Point Palm entered the culture as shorthand for a delayed, elegant inevitability.

One film, two weathers

Tarantino shot the Kill Bill saga as a single production (principally in China, Mexico, and the United States) from the script he had written for Thurman as a thirtieth-birthday gift, and split it in post-production when the cut exceeded four hours — a division that hardened into the diptych's design: Volume 1 eastern, kinetic, nearly wordless in its climaxes; Volume 2 western, verbal, its violence intimate and its climax a conversation. Robert Rodriguez composed the score for a symbolic one-dollar fee (Tarantino returned the favor on Sin City), around a soundtrack of Ennio Morricone, Luis Bacalov, Johnny Cash and Malcolm McLaren's "About Her," which scores the finale. Gordon Liu — the Shaw Brothers star who had played the Crazy 88's leader Johnny Mo in Volume 1 — was recast as Pai Mei, the role his younger self would have fought, in a piece of casting the production regarded as the homage's keystone. The buried-alive sequence was shot with Thurman actually enclosed, its sound design built from coffin acoustics. Released April 16, 2004, the film finished at $152.2 million worldwide and has since been widely re-evaluated upward, frequently ranked as the finer half of the saga.

03 / NARRATIVE SUMMARY

Story Synopsis

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL FULL STORY SUMMARY

The film opens in black and white, at the rehearsal the first volume only glimpsed: Beatrix, pregnant, in a wedding dress she has chosen as her exit visa from the life — and Bill, materializing on the chapel steps with a flute and a blessing that is also a claim check, conducting with the groom's family a performance of the harmless father-of-the-bride before stepping outside and nodding to his squad. The massacre is played as sound and aftermath; the headshot is the volume's first image and its whole premise in one cut. The present-tense campaign resumes at Budd's trailer in the desert, where the film's western grammar takes over: Budd, warned, waits in the dark with rock salt and a speech about deserving ("that woman deserves her revenge, and we deserve to die — that's just the way it is") that files the saga's moral bookkeeping in the mouth of its most ruined man. The burial is the film's centerpiece of dread: the coffin, the dirt's percussion, the flashlight's death — and then the tutelage, intercut as her memory's flashlight: Pai Mei's temple, the carrying of water, the punching of boards at knuckle's distance, the rice eaten with chopsticks from a shaking hand, until the technique that matters is revealed to have been taught in secret, master to favorite, against his own rule. She punches through the lid at exactly that distance and claws up through the grave in a shot the film holds until it becomes a resurrection's paperwork.

The Elle transactions complete the list's undercard with the saga's coldest accounting: the suitcase of money with the mamba in it (Budd dies reading the snake's invoice aloud, in the film's most literary death scene — Elle's spoken dossier on the black mamba, delivered over his convulsions), and the trailer fight, in which Beatrix and Elle settle a decade of hatred in Budd's filthy close quarters — an eye plucked for the eye Pai Mei lost to Elle's insolence, the debt's symmetry exact — and Elle is left blind in a trailer with the mamba, her fate unsealed and unneeded. Esteban's villa supplies the address in the film's most courteous betrayal, and the finale is staged as a family evening: B.B., alive, playing dead with her father's gun in a game that has clearly been rehearsed for this exact guest; the truth serum, administered by dart over drinks, because Bill requires the real answer to his one question (why did she leave without a word) before the account closes; the Superman speech, in which he files her nature — a killer, playing at normalcy, as Clark Kent is Superman's impersonation of the weak — and her counter-testimony: she left because, in the instant of the pregnancy test, she was a mother, and the life had no vacancy for one. The fight, when it comes, is four seconds: his sword trapped in her scabbard, her fingertips already placed. His death is the saga's thesis in a single courtesy — he asks whether Pai Mei taught her, hears "of course he did," makes his peace with her and with the arithmetic, and walks his five steps like a man keeping an appointment he respects. The bathroom floor the next morning — Beatrix weeping, laughing, thanking whatever is listening — is the rampage's true final shot, and the drive out of the film, mother and cub and cartoons, is its pardon.

The full narrative summary is an editorial paraphrase, not dialogue or a scene-by-scene transcript.

THE BRIDE

Beatrix Kiddo

Uma Thurman. 10 / THEMATIC ANALYSIS

Themes & Analysis

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Revenge completed is revenge survived — the bathroom floor

The film's governing theme is its ending's emotional arithmetic: that the rampage's last scene is not the killing but the morning after — a woman on a motel bathroom floor, weeping with a gratitude indistinguishable from grief, because the account is closed and the cub is asleep in the next room watching cartoons. The theme is worked out in the diptych's whole design (Volume 1's kinetic triumph gives no satisfaction that survives its credits; Volume 2's satisfaction is precisely posthumous to the revenge) until the audience holds the film's doctrine: revenge, completed honestly, is not a pleasure but a price — paid in full, it purchases the right to feel everything else again. Its proposition reaches every keeper of a list in the audience — the grudge, the lawsuit, the feud: the settlement you imagine as a triumph will arrive, if it arrives honestly, as a bathroom floor. The reach is the story's question to every Beatrix with three names left: what is asleep in the next room of your campaign, and is the campaign priced to protect it?

The killer and the costume — Bill's Superman thesis

The film's second theme is its famous monologue: Bill's argument that Beatrix is a born killer wearing normalcy as a costume — as Superman, not Batman, wakes up as the hero and dresses as the civilian — and that her attempt to marry out of the life was therefore a masquerade the massacre merely interrupted. The theme is worked out in her counter-testimony under the serum (she did not leave Bill; she left the life, in the instant the test turned positive — the mother outranking the killer in a single heartbeat) until the audience holds the film's anthropology as a genuine dispute: whether identity is a nature that costumes conceal or a succession of true selves, each priced in the coin of the last. Its proposition reaches every escapee of a former life in the audience — the trade, the family business, the old self: your Bill is whoever files your change as a costume. The reach is the story's question to every bride in a rehearsal dress: whose reading of your nature are you living under — and what would your fingertips say?

Cruel tutelage — the master's secret favoritism

The film's third theme is its pedagogy: that Pai Mei's cruelty is a selection conducted as abuse — the torment is the admissions process, and the one secret lesson goes to the student who endured it without either breaking or currying — so that the saga's decisive weapon is distributed, decades early, by a dead misogynist's private judgment of character. The theme is worked out in the grave (the curriculum's knuckle-distance unit, cashed in the dark) until the audience holds the film's doctrine of hard teaching: a true master's withholding is a form of trust — the secret is the certificate that the torment was an investment. Its proposition reaches every survivor of a cruel tutelage in the audience — the coach, the editor, the parent: the question is not whether the forge was kind but whether anything in you was being saved for a coffin. The reach is the story's question to every student punching boards: what is your one-inch distance, and who is measuring it?

Deserve — the saga's moral bookkeeping

The film's fourth theme is its most quoted sentence, given to its most ruined man: "That woman deserves her revenge, and we deserve to die" — the underworld's own audit, in which the massacre's perpetrators price their crime exactly and file no appeal. The theme is worked out in the volume's settlement pattern (no Viper dies protesting the justice of it; their resistance is professional, never moral) until the audience holds the film's jurisprudence: this is a world whose criminals keep honest books, and its horror is thereby deepened — the massacre was not a misunderstanding but a policy, and the list is not vengeance's madness but its accounting. Its proposition reaches every participant in a settled wrong in the audience: the books you keep in private are the verdict you have already accepted. The reach is the story's question to every Budd in a trailer: what does your own ledger say you deserve — and who is coming, at knuckle's distance, to collect?

Ending Explained

REVEAL THE ENDING

Beatrix finds Bill through his foster father Esteban and discovers her daughter B.B. is alive. After a truth-serum conversation about why she left, they fight briefly: she traps his sword and strikes him with the Five Point Palm Exploding Heart Technique, which Pai Mei secretly taught her. Bill makes his peace, takes five steps and dies. The next morning Beatrix, reunited with B.B., weeps with relief and drives away with her daughter.

What the ending establishes

  • The five steps file the finale as a reconciliation with a body count of one: Bill's death is the saga's only killing conducted as a mutual courtesy, and its peace is genuine on both sides.
  • B.B.'s survival files the saga's premise as inverted at the last moment: the child whose death armed the rampage was its hostage all along, and the revenge's true object was a reunion.
  • Pai Mei's secret files the tutelage as the plot's author: the finale was decided on a mountain decades earlier, by a master's private judgment.
  • Elle's unsealed fate files the list as complete without being total: one account is left open in a trailer, and the film declines to need it closed.
  • The bathroom floor files the rampage's emotional settlement as posthumous: the feeling arrives after the work, in a motel, at dawn, in full.

What remains uncertain

Whether Elle survives the trailer, whether Vernita's daughter (promised, in Volume 1, her own chance at revenge) will one day come collecting, and whether Beatrix's nature is Bill's thesis or her own testimony — all stand open at the film's close (Tarantino has mused for decades about a sequel; none has been made). Within this film, the archive files the uncertainty as the story's own last word on its theme: the list is settled, the cub is rejoined, and the lioness drives out of the film with the books balanced and the jungle, provisionally, right.

12 / PURPOSES OF THE STORY

Moral of The Story

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Kill Bill: Volume 2 uses a buried bride, a cruel master's secret and a courteous final boss to examine revenge, identity and desert: a killer who left the life in a heartbeat's decision; a squad that keeps honest books about what it deserves; and a daughter, alive, in whose existence the whole rampage is retroactively repriced. Through the coffin, the serum and the five steps, the film argues that revenge completed is a price, not a prize; that a person's nature is theirs to testify to, against any lover's thesis; and that the hardest tutelage, honestly survived, leaves a technique in the knuckles for the day you are buried.

Why tell this story?

Because Tarantino understood that the revenge saga's second half had to be its reckoning: the first volume asks whether she can kill them all; the second asks what it costs, what it was for, and who she is when the list is done. He told it in the western register — talk, dust, courtesy, five steps — because the east's barrage could not hold the questions. The result is the finer half of the decade's defining revenge work, the archive files: a film in which the climax is a conversation and the killing takes four seconds, correctly.

1. Price your list before you start it

The ledger is the film's first counsel, for every keeper of a campaign — the feud, the lawsuit, the vendetta of the office: itemize what completion will actually purchase (a bathroom floor, a drive with the cub) against what it will certainly cost (four years, a grave, a piece of every morning), because the film's whole heroine is a woman who never priced hers and paid retail. The counsel is a campaign doctrine with the crossed names as its ledger: two volumes of expenditure bought one dawn of relief. If your list's dawn is worth its volumes, proceed with her discipline. If it is not, the cheapest name to cross off is the first one, today.

2. Testify to your own nature

Bill's Superman speech is the film's second counsel, for every escapee of a former life living under a former intimate's definition — the ex, the family, the old firm: their reading of you will be brilliant, affectionate and self-serving in exactly the proportion that it keeps you theirs. The counsel is an identity doctrine with the serum as its standard: the truth, taken neat, is that the heartbeat in which you changed is your testimony, and no monologue — however loving, however expert — outranks it. You will still have to fight the thesis's holder, perhaps. But fight as the author of the counter-testimony, not as its defendant.

3. Endure the tutelage; audit it later

Pai Mei's mountain is the film's third counsel, for every student in a cruel forge — the residency, the apprenticeship, the old-school mentorship: while you are in it, the only question that pays is what the torment is installing (a distance in the knuckles, a technique in reserve), and the audit of its cruelty belongs to the far side of the grave it is preparing you to exit. The counsel is a pedagogy doctrine with the coffin as its examination hall: some curricula can only be graded underground. Endure like a favorite. And if nothing is being installed but damage — the film is equally clear — that is a different mountain, and the correct technique is the road down.

4. Keep honest books about what you deserve

Budd's sentence is the film's fourth counsel, for every participant in a wrong that has not yet been collected — the partner, the colleague, the brother: the private ledger you keep is the verdict you have already accepted, and the film's ruined bouncer, pricing his own death correctly over a trailer's darkness, has more moral clarity than the saga's every protestation of innocence. The counsel is an accounting doctrine with the rock salt as its stationery: settle your books before the collector reaches your name, because the collection, in this world, is conducted by professionals and the discount is only ever for early settlement. "We deserve to die" is a terrible sentence. It is also the first honest one in Budd's film.

5. Make your peace inside the five steps

Bill's walk is the film's fifth counsel, for every loser of a final confrontation — the fired, the divorced, the defeated: there remains, after the technique has landed, a short walk in which the manner of your going is entirely yours — the jacket straightened, the peace made, the steps taken like an appointment kept. The counsel is a defeat doctrine with the patio as its stage: Bill loses everything in four seconds and spends his last five steps demonstrating that the loss did not include his courtesy, his clarity, or his love's accuracy about her excellence. When your five steps come — and the film's actuarial tables are undefeated — walk them as the person your enemy's respect describes.

6. Leave the last account open, if it is open

Elle's trailer is the film's sixth counsel, its benediction, for every executor of a settlement — the manager closing a feud, the friend ending a war: completeness is a ledger's virtue, not a life's, and the film's refusal to seal Elle's fate (blind, in a trailer, with the snake) is its certificate that some accounts are closed by being abandoned rather than collected. The counsel is a settlement doctrine with the trailer door as its seal: Beatrix walks out of the film's ugliest room and does not look back, and the saga's books balance anyway. Know which of your accounts is an Elle. Walk out of the trailer.

The moral of the story is…

Price the list before you start it; testify to your own nature against any lover's thesis; endure the tutelage that is installing something — and make your peace inside the five steps.

Kill Bill: Volume 2 teaches that revenge completed is a dawn on a bathroom floor, not a triumph; that identity is a testimony, not a costume; and that the deadliest technique in the world is useless without the courtesy to let its target walk his five steps in peace.

The film's final question is therefore not whether you could cross the names off — the film files that under "tutelage." It is what is asleep in the next room of your campaign; whose thesis about your nature you are still living under; and whether, buried at knuckle's distance from the lid, your hands remember what the mountain taught them.

Editorial note: This is an interpretive reading of the film's themes, not a claim about every private intention of its creators or a prediction of future franchise events.
