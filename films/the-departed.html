01 / FILM IDENTITY

Overview

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

The Departed is a 2006 neo-noir crime thriller directed by Martin Scorsese, written by William Monahan, and remade from the 2002 Hong Kong film Infernal Affairs, with its Boston Irish-mob world drawn loosely from the Winter Hill Gang — Jack Nicholson's Frank Costello modeled in part on Whitey Bulger, Matt Damon's Colin Sullivan in part on the corrupt FBI agent John Connolly. Released October 6, 2006, it grossed $291.5 million on a $90 million budget and won four Academy Awards — Best Picture, Best Adapted Screenplay, Best Film Editing, and Best Director, Scorsese's first Oscar after five decades of filmmaking. South Boston: Costello, the neighborhood's crime sovereign, adopts a bright local boy, Colin Sullivan, and grooms him for twenty years into the Massachusetts State Police — a mole grown, not planted. The police answer in kind and in mirror image: Billy Costigan (Leonardo DiCaprio), a trooper cadet from a criminal Southie family, is recruited by Captain Queenan (Martin Sheen) and Sergeant Dignam (Mark Wahlberg) to be expelled in disgrace, serve a staged prison term, and infiltrate Costello's crew as the department's only secret. The film is the two men's tightening orbit: Sullivan, golden and promoted, runs the very unit hunting Costello while telephoning warnings to his true employer; Costigan, beaten, jailed, medicated and unravelling, climbs inside the crew while reporting to handlers only two living men know he has. Each is assigned to find the rat in his own organization — each is, therefore, hunting himself by proxy — and the film's machinery (burner phones, a warehouse raid spoiled by seconds, a tail followed to a porn theater, a rooftop) keeps narrowing the distance until the two moles pass within feet of each other without knowing. Between them stands Madolyn Madden (Vera Farmiga), a police psychiatrist who treats Costigan's panic and loves Sullivan — the only civilian in either man's real life, and the film's unwitting courier: Costigan, at the end, mails her the evidence. The climax is a cascade of settlement: Costello, revealed as a protected FBI informant all along, is killed by Sullivan to cut his own leash; Queenan is thrown from a roof by Costello's men in front of Costigan's eyes; Costigan finally identifies Sullivan (an envelope, a desk, a recognized hand) and arrests him at gunpoint on the roof where Queenan died — and is shot dead in the elevator by a second, deeper mole, Trooper Barrigan, whom Sullivan then kills to complete his cover. Sullivan, the survivor, erases Costigan's file, recommends him for a posthumous medal, mourns beautifully at the funeral — and comes home, weeks later, to Dignam waiting in his apartment in surgical shoe covers, the film's last act of bookkeeping, executed on a plastic sheet while a rat crosses the balcony railing outside, silhouetted against the Massachusetts State House and its golden dome.

Place in the culture

The film is the rare remake that became its country's definitive version of a story: Infernal Affairs supplied the architecture, but Scorsese and Monahan rebuilt it as an American tragedy of identity and class — South Boston's two exits (the badge, the crew) worn by two men who each took the other's. Its Oscar night was a national catharsis (Scorsese's Director win, presented by Spielberg, Coppola and Lucas, was received as a debt finally paid), and its final fifteen minutes remain the era's benchmark for an ending that settles every account on screen without forgiving any of them.

From Hong Kong to Southie

Warner Bros. and producers Brad Grey and Brad Pitt acquired the Infernal Affairs remake rights in 2003 for a reported $1.75 million; Monahan — a Boston-raised writer — relocated the story wholesale into Irish-American South Boston and built Costello and Sullivan from the Bulger–Connolly scandal, in which a mob boss and his FBI handler grew up on the same streets. Pitt, originally to play Sullivan, stepped back to producing, judging the role needed a younger actor; Damon took it, DiCaprio (in his third consecutive Scorsese film) took Costigan, and Nicholson — given unusual freedom to improvise Costello's menace — supplied the film's demonic center. Shot in Boston and New York under Michael Ballhaus's cinematography and Thelma Schoonmaker's editing (her third Oscar), the film premiered September 26, 2006 and opened at number one. At the 79th Academy Awards it won Best Picture, Best Director (Scorsese's only personal Oscar), Best Adapted Screenplay and Best Film Editing; Wahlberg was nominated for Supporting Actor. It remains Scorsese's highest-grossing film of its era and the Academy's official correction of a forty-year oversight.

03 / NARRATIVE SUMMARY

Story Synopsis

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL FULL STORY SUMMARY

The film opens with Costello adopting a boy ("No one gives it to you. You have to take it") and cuts, twenty years on, to the two graduations: Sullivan, polished, into the State Police's Special Investigations Unit under Captain Ellerby — and Costigan, expelled from the academy in a staged disgrace arranged by Queenan and Dignam, into a cousin's drug trade, a real prison term, and finally Costello's crew, vouched for by his family's criminal name. The mirror structure is the plot: Sullivan, assigned to find the mole leaking to Costello, telephones Costello from the middle of the investigation; Costigan, assigned to identify himself out of danger, reports to Queenan from inside a crew where discovery is death. The set pieces are near-meetings of the two reflections: the warehouse raid, where Costigan's warning text and Sullivan's counter-warning arrive seconds apart and the crew escapes through the back as the police enter the front; the tail, where Costigan follows Sullivan's silhouette through Chinatown and loses him in a porn theater, the two men separated by a curtain and a ringing phone; the microprocessor heist, where Costigan watches Costello sell stolen chips to Chinese gangsters while Costello — who has been an FBI informant for years, the film's deepest rot — lectures him on the neighborhood's decline. Costigan's cover is eating him (the psychiatrist's office, where he begs Madolyn for real sedation and tells her the truth in every way except the words; the panic attacks; the painkillers), and Sullivan's is nourishing him (promotion, a State House-view apartment, Madolyn's love) — the film's cruelest symmetry: the false life is killing the honest man and feeding the false one.

The settlement begins when Costello, cornered by a federal net he can feel, is killed by Sullivan in a warehouse — the mole cutting his leash, shooting his own father-figure mid-taunt and taking a wound to sell the story. Queenan, meeting Costigan on a rooftop to extract him, is thrown to his death by Costello's men as Costigan watches from the shadows — the department's secret dying with the only two men who knew it, since Dignam, broken by the death and suspended for assaulting Sullivan, has resigned and vanished. Costigan's identification of Sullivan is the film's quietest scene: at the police station, collecting his pay, he sees on Sullivan's desk the envelope — his own handwriting, the "citizens" envelope he once handed Costello's crew — and walks out into his own arrest plan. The rooftop arrest is the film's moral summit performed as a hostage scene: Costigan, gun to Sullivan's head, demanding not escape but acknowledgment ("I just want my identity back"), riding the elevator down to die in its doorway at Barrigan's hand — the second mole neither man had counted — whom Sullivan kills and files as the rat, closing the case with himself as its hero. The coda is paperwork and a plastic sheet: Sullivan deletes Costigan's file, ornaments his funeral, impregnates his future with Madolyn — and opens his apartment door, weeks later, on Dignam in shoe covers, who has read Madolyn's mailed envelope (Costigan's posthumous evidence: the tapes, the truth, the instruction) and come to perform the film's final act of administration. The rat on the balcony, against the golden dome, is the film's signature under the ledger: in a city of two-way mirrors, the only honest citizen left is the vermin, and he at least is what he appears to be.

The full narrative summary is an editorial paraphrase, not dialogue or a scene-by-scene transcript.

04 / STORY STRUCTURE

Story & Chronology

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

The film unfolds across roughly a year of parallel infiltrations, framed by a twenty-year grooming. The sequence below follows the film's narrative order.

Act I — The two graduations

A boy adopted by a mob boss; a cadet expelled by design — and two telephones, ringing in opposite uniforms, inside each other's organizations.

Act II — The hall of mirrors

A raid spoiled by seconds, a tail lost in a theater, an informant inside the informant — and a captain falling from a roof in front of his only secret.

Act III — The settlement

An envelope recognized, a rooftop arrest, an elevator — and a sergeant in shoe covers, performing the last entry in a dead man's file.

05 / CHARACTER DATABASE

Character Database

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Search the principal characters documented in this film. Roles and motivations below summarize their function in the 2006 movie.

THE UNDERCOVER

Billy Costigan

Leonardo DiCaprio. A State Police cadet from a Southie crime family — recruited to be destroyed on paper (expelled, jailed) so he can live inside Costello's crew as the department's secret. DiCaprio plays him as a man being consumed by his own cover: the panic, the pills, the psychiatrist's couch, the terror that the performance has eaten the performer (his demand on the rooftop is not for rescue but for his identity back — the film's thesis in one line). Costigan's function is the story's portrait of the honest infiltrator: the state asked him to become a criminal convincingly enough to die as one, and kept the receipt in a file two men could open. The archive files him as the decade's most complete undercover in film: a cop whose only proof of being one is an envelope he has to mail to his own psychiatrist.

THE MOLE

Colin Sullivan

Matt Damon. Costello's adopted protégé — groomed from childhood into the State Police, rising through the Special Investigations Unit as its golden boy while reporting every move to his true father. Damon plays him with a polished, entitled ease that curdles, across the film, into the recognition that the performance is the person (his terror is not exposure but the discovery that there is nothing under the cover — Costello made him, and Costello's death leaves him an orphan with a pension). Sullivan's function is the story's portrait of the raised infiltrator: where Costigan is a good man playing a bad one, Sullivan is an artifact — a person manufactured by a criminal for a single use, who survives his maker and finds the manufacture is all he is. The archive files him as the decade's most complete mole in film: a son, filed undercover by his father, at birth.

THE SOVEREIGN

Frank Costello

Jack Nicholson. South Boston's Irish-mob king — philosopher, predator and, the film's deepest twist, a longtime FBI informant, feeding the Bureau selected truth while running the neighborhood as a private state. Nicholson plays him with an improvisational, demonic relish (the rat monologue, the opera, the cocaine, the casual theology) that makes him the film's black sun: every other character's orbit is his. Costello's function is the story's portrait of the sovereign as the system's secret employee: the state's war on him is partly his own arrangement, and his death — shot by his own adopted son, mid-sentence, in a warehouse — is the film's thesis that in this world every father is eaten by his filing system. The archive files him as the decade's most complete crime sovereign in film: a king who was also an asset, killed by his own investment.

THE SERGEANT

Sean Dignam

Mark Wahlberg. Queenan's staff sergeant — the undercover operation's co-keeper, a Southie professional whose contempt for Sullivan is instinctive, total and (the film's design) diagnostic: he is the one man who reads the mole correctly from the first interview, without evidence, on neighborhood knowledge alone. Wahlberg plays him as a coiled profanity with a perfect record, and Dignam's function is the story's portrait of the institution's immune system: suspended and resigned, he becomes the film's final instrument — the shoe covers, the plastic, the shot — the department's conscience operating, at the end, entirely off the books he once kept. The archive files him as the decade's most consequential sergeant in film: the file's last reader, arriving with the envelope's contents memorized.

THE CAPTAIN

Oliver Queenan

Martin Sheen. The undercover unit's captain — Costigan's handler, protector and, with Dignam, the only keeper of his secret; a decent man running an indecent necessity, whose paternal care for his buried asset (the meetings, the money, the insistence that the boy can be brought home) is the operation's moral cost made personal. Sheen plays him with a quiet, priestly gravity, and Queenan's function is the story's portrait of the handler as the asset's true family: his death — thrown from the roof of the building where he met his secret, landing at the feet of the crew hunting him — is the film's point of no return, the moment Costigan becomes a ghost in his own life. The archive files him as the decade's most complete handler in film: a captain who dies as his asset's last living document.

THE PSYCHIATRIST

Dr. Madolyn Madden

Vera Farmiga. A police psychiatrist — assigned to Costigan's crumbling cover (and lying, professionally, in his file to protect him) while living with Sullivan, whose performance she cannot see through because it is, domestically, entirely sincere. Farmiga plays her with a clinician's intelligence applied everywhere except the one diagnosis that matters, and Madolyn's function is the story's portrait of the civilian as the plot's unwitting infrastructure: she treats one mole, loves the other, and becomes — via Costigan's mailed envelope — the archive through which the truth finally testifies. The archive files her as the decade's most essential civilian in a crime film: the only person both men told anything true to, in different rooms, in different lies.

THE ENFORCER

Mr. French

Ray Winstone. Costello's lieutenant — a blunt, loyal instrument of the crew's violence, whose devotion to his sovereign is the organization's one sincere relationship: French believes in Costello the way the crew's recruits believe in nothing. Winstone plays him with a workingman's menace, and French's function is the story's portrait of loyalty in a hall of mirrors: in a film where every other allegiance is a cover, the enforcer's is real — which is why his death, in a car, by his own hand rather than capture, reads as the old world's last honorable exit. The archive files him as the decade's most complete mob lieutenant in film: the only man in the picture whose file says exactly what he is.

THE COMMANDER

Captain George Ellerby

Alec Baldwin. Head of the Special Investigations Unit — Sullivan's boss, a profane, funny, results-drunk commander whose manhunt for Costello is genuine and whose personnel file contains the mole he is hunting, promoted on his own signature. Baldwin plays him as the institution's id (every briefing a pep talk at maximum volume), and Ellerby's function is the story's portrait of the command level's blindness: he is an excellent hunter of everything except the man across his own desk — the film files his confidence as the mole's true camouflage. The archive files him as the decade's most entertaining commander in a crime film: a captain whose unit's leak is his favorite officer.

THE LIEUTENANT

Tim Delahunt

Mark Rolston. A senior member of Costello's crew — mortally wounded in the warehouse raid, who spends his last scene refusing a hospital (the crew's code) and whispering to Costigan the film's most ambiguous mercy: that he knew, or might have known, what Costigan was — and chose, dying, not to say it. Delahunt's function is the story's portrait of the underworld's own code of silence operating in the asset's favor: the crewman who could have killed the mole with a sentence instead carries the suspicion into the grave with him, for reasons (loyalty to a face, contempt for the police, a dying man's bookkeeping) the film leaves open. The archive files him as the decade's most quietly merciful gangster in film: a lieutenant whose last act is an omission.

THE SECOND MOLE

Trooper Barrigan

James Badge Dale. A State Police trooper — and Costello's second, deeper plant, revealed in the elevator in the film's most shocking single shot: he executes Costigan at the threshold of Sullivan's exposure, having been the insurance policy nobody counted. Barrigan's function is the story's portrait of the infiltration's true depth: the operation the audience has been watching — two moles, mirror images — was always three, and the third is the one who survives longest, until Sullivan, his own asset-brother, kills him to complete the cover. The archive files him as the decade's most consequential hidden character in a crime film: a footnote with a gun, standing in the elevator.

THE TROOPER

Trooper Brown

Anthony Anderson. A state trooper and academy contemporary of both moles — the film's control sample: an ordinary officer, neither planted nor buried, whose decency and ordinariness measure, by contrast, the deformity of the two careers he trained alongside. Brown's function is the story's portrait of the unbought life inside a bought system: he works the same raids, takes the same risks, and goes home himself — the film files his normality as the road neither mole was permitted to take. The archive files him as the decade's most quietly essential minor officer in a crime film: the classmate who stayed one person.

THE COUSIN

Brian Costigan & the Costigan Family

Costigan's criminal kin — the cousins and uncles whose names are his undercover passport and whose world (the drug trade, the neighborhood code) is the one he must perform as his own inheritance. The Family's function is the story's portrait of the cover's raw material: the state did not invent Billy Costigan's criminal identity; it requisitioned his actual family — his blood was the credential, which is why the performance is destroying him (he is not pretending to be from this world; he is pretending not to have left it). The archive files them as the decade's most consequential minor family in a crime film: a bloodline used as a badge, in reverse.

06 / THE STATE POLICE, COSTELLO'S CREW & THE FBI SHADOW

The State Police, Costello's Crew & the FBI Shadow

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
THE DEPARTMENT

The Massachusetts State Police

The film's law: two units at cross-purposes inside one building — Ellerby's SIU hunting Costello with a mole at its heart, Queenan's undercover shop running an asset it cannot acknowledge. The Department's function is the story's portrait of the institution as its own battlefield: the police are simultaneously the hunter, the hunted and the prize, and their final accounting (a medal for the asset, a promotion track for the mole, an off-book execution by their own resigned sergeant) is conducted in three different registers of honesty. The Department ends the film decorated and deceived, the archive files it as the decade's most complete fictional police force: an organization that solves its case in a eulogy and an apartment.

THE CREW

Costello's Organization

Southie's Irish mob: a neighborhood sovereignty run as a family business — collections, microprocessors, cocaine, and a king who treats his crew as children and his informant status as a pension plan. The Crew's function is the story's portrait of the underworld as the department's mirror and maker: it manufactures the police's mole, employs the police's asset, and is penetrated by the federal government at the very top — its sovereignty is a tenancy it never inspects. The Crew ends the film decapitated by its own adopted son, the archive files it as the decade's most complete fictional mob: a kingdom whose king was an asset and whose heir was a trooper.

THE SHADOW

The FBI (off-screen)

The film's unseen third power: the Bureau for whom Costello has informed for years — the arrangement (drawn from the real Bulger scandal) that protected the sovereign, warped every local investigation, and made the state's war on Costello partly a performance staged with its target's cooperation. The Bureau's function is the story's portrait of the deepest mirror: above the two moles sits a third arrangement, official and unmentionable, in which the government's asset is the city's crime lord. The Bureau ends the film untouched and unnamed in its consequences, the archive files it as the decade's most consequential off-screen institution in a crime film: the handler nobody handles.

THE NEIGHBORHOOD

South Boston

The film's true sovereign: a tribal, Catholic, working-class enclave whose two ladders (the badge, the crew) recruit from the same stoops, whose code (never rat) is the plot's supreme law, and whose golden dome view is the prize both moles are raised to want. Southie's function is the story's portrait of the place as the author of both men: Costigan and Sullivan are not opposites but neighbors — the same streets, the same mass, the same hunger, filed into opposite uniforms by two father-figures with offices. The Neighborhood ends the film exactly as it began, the archive files it as the decade's most consequential fictional neighborhood: a parish that manufactures cops and robbers from one catechism.

The war is a family business

The film's factional design is a kinship chart: Costello fathers Sullivan; the department fathers Costigan; the FBI keeps Costello; the neighborhood fathers them all — and the shooting starts when the sons begin settling their inheritances. The archive files the design as the story's durable structure: in Southie, every organization is a family with a filing system, and every filing system eventually requires a funeral.

08 / THE TWO MOLES, THE PHONES & THE ENVELOPE

The Two Moles, the Phones & the Envelope

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

The parallel infiltration — the mirror engine

The film's governing system: two organizations, each running one buried asset inside the other, each asset assigned to find the other — a structure in which the investigation and the crime are the same act performed in two uniforms. The system's function is the story's portrait of institutional symmetry: the department and the crew use identical methods (grooming, handlers, burner phones, staged biographies) and produce identical damage, differing only in their letterhead and their funerals. The archive files the parallel infiltration as the decade's most consequential fictional system in a crime film: a war in which both sides' secret weapon is the same wound.

The phones — the leashes

The film's nervous system: burner phones, pocket-dialed calls, a stolen phone's contact list reading simply "citizens" — every major turn in the plot is a call placed, intercepted, traced or missed by seconds. The phones' function is the story's portrait of the leash as the infiltrator's true uniform: neither mole is ever unaccompanied; each carries his handler in his pocket, and the film's tension is quite literally signal strength. The archive files the phones as the decade's most consequential fictional instruments in a crime film: two leashes, ringing in opposite pockets, in the same rooms.

The file & the envelope — the paperwork of identity

The film's documentary system: Costigan exists as a cop only in a sealed file (deleted, at the end, by his killer) and in an envelope of evidence he mails, as insurance, to the one civilian both moles share. The envelope's function is the story's portrait of identity as an administrative fact: a man is what his file says, and the film's whole tragedy can be stated as a records problem — the honest man's proof is a folder two people can open, one of whom is thrown off a roof. The archive files the envelope as the decade's most consequential fictional document in a crime film: a dead man's identity, delivered by post, executed by a sergeant.

The informant arrangement — the third mirror

Costello's FBI protection: the system above the systems, in which the sovereign's longevity is a federal filing — his tips purchase his tenancy, and every local war on him is partly choreographed. The arrangement's function is the story's portrait of the compromise that makes the other two possible: the moles exist because the war exists; the war exists, in part, because its target is an asset. The archive files the arrangement as the decade's most quietly damning fictional system in a crime film: a protection racket in which the protected is the racketeer.

10 / THEMATIC ANALYSIS

Themes & Analysis

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Identity is a file — and files get deleted

The film's governing theme is stated on its rooftop: "I just want my identity back." Costigan's selfhood is an administrative object — a sealed file, two living witnesses, a psychiatrist's falsified notes — and the film tracks its destruction with bureaucratic precision (a captain falls, a sergeant resigns, a file is deleted, a medal is recommended for a man the state will describe as a criminal it has posthumously forgiven). The theme is worked out in the mirror (Sullivan's identity is equally a file — Costello's, the academy's, the Bureau's — and equally deletable) until the audience holds the film's doctrine entire: in a world administered by records, a person is what survives in other people's filing, and the only identity that cannot be deleted is the one somebody carries in memory and acts on — Dignam's. Its proposition reaches every employee in the audience whose real work is invisible to their official record: your file is not your life, and the distance between them is a risk someone should be keeping for you, in writing, in an envelope. The reach is the story's question to every Costigan with a sealed record: who, exactly, can open yours — and what happens to you on the day both of them are gone?

The cover eats the covered — performance as erosion

The film's second theme is the two performances' opposite metabolisms: Costigan's false life is killing the true man (panic, pills, the terror in the psychiatrist's office that there will be nothing left to bring home), while Sullivan's false life is feeding the false one (promotion, love, the dome view) because there is increasingly nothing underneath to starve. The theme is worked out in the film's casting of its sympathies — the audience watches the honest man's self dissolve and the manufactured man's self consolidate, and is forced to file the decade's most uncomfortable finding about acting: a performance sustained long enough does not remain a performance; it becomes the person, in whichever direction it was pointed. Its proposition reaches every professional in the audience living a long assignment — the undercover of any kind, the expatriate executive, the caretaker, the spy of any bureaucracy: the cover has a diet, and it is you. The reach is the story's question to every Billy in a long role: what is your extraction plan, who holds it, and on what date does the performance's lease on you expire?

No one gives it to you — the neighborhood's catechism

The film's third theme is its opening line, Costello's gift to a child: that the world is taken, not given — the catechism both moles are raised on, in the same parish, from opposite pulpits (the mob's and the state's). The theme is worked out in the film's whole architecture of taking (a boy taken by a boss, a cadet taken by a captain, a heart of the operation taken by whichever son shoots first) until the audience holds the film's grim finding: the catechism is true and it is a curse — everyone in the film who takes successfully is destroyed by the taking's bookkeeping, and the film's one serene figure is a rat, who takes nothing and is exactly what he is. Its proposition reaches every striver raised on the same doctrine in the audience: the question is not whether to take, but what the taking will file you as, and whether the file will still open at the end. The reach is the story's question to every Sullivan with a dome view: what did the view cost, itemized — and who is keeping the receipt?

The state and the street use the same methods

The film's fourth theme is its mirror held up to the audience's allegiances: that the department and the crew are operationally indistinguishable — grooming children, running assets, staging biographies, deleting files, decorating the dead — and that the film refuses, to its final frame, to file the difference as anything more than letterhead and funeral honors. The theme is worked out in the parallel structure's every rhyme (two fathers, two graduations, two telephones, two funerals) until the audience holds the film's most Scorsesean doctrine: the war on crime and the crime are one city's single industry, employing the same boys from the same stoops, and its product is coffins with flags and coffins without. Its proposition reaches every citizen in the audience who funds one side of the mirror: your institution's methods are its character, whatever its mission statement says, and its assets are somebody's sons. The reach is the story's question to every Ellerby briefing a manhunt: if your enemy ran this exact operation, what would you call it?

11 / FULL SPOILER CONCLUSION

Ending Explained

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL THE ENDING

Costigan identifies Sullivan as the mole and arrests him on the rooftop, but is shot dead in the elevator by Barrigan, Costello's second mole — whom Sullivan kills and frames as the rat. Sullivan erases Costigan's file and survives as the hero. Weeks later, Dignam — who has received Costigan's mailed evidence from Madolyn — waits in Sullivan's apartment and executes him. A rat crosses the balcony rail against the State House dome.

What the ending establishes

  • Costigan's death files the asset's tragedy as an administrative event: he dies one floor from vindication, killed by the infiltration's uncounted third man — the mirror had a mirror.
  • Sullivan's survival files the mole's victory as total and temporary: he closes the case, deletes the file, ornaments the funeral — and inherits, with the apartment, a sergeant's patience.
  • Dignam's execution files the film's justice as entirely off-book: the institution's systems acquitted Sullivan; its conscience, resigned and in shoe covers, did not — the film files both facts without reconciling them.
  • Madolyn's envelope files the civilian archive as the truth's last address: the state deleted its record; the psychiatrist delivered hers — the film's one successful act of filing.
  • The rat on the balcony files the film's signature under the ledger: against the golden dome of the state, the vermin crosses in silhouette — the only citizen of the film who is exactly what he appears to be.

What remains uncertain

Whether Madolyn will raise Sullivan's child knowing the whole file, whether Dignam is ever charged, and whether the Bureau's arrangement with the next Costello is already filed — all stand open at the film's close (no sequel followed; the story is complete). Within this film, the archive files the uncertainty as the story's own last word on its theme: the accounts are settled, the dome is golden, and the rat is crossing — the city continues, exactly as administered.

12 / PURPOSES OF THE STORY

Moral of The Story

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

The Departed uses two mirrored moles, a thrown captain and a mailed envelope to examine identity, performance and institutional symmetry: an honest man consumed by the criminal he performs; a manufactured man nourished by the officer he performs; and a city whose police and mob run identical operations from opposite letterheads. Through the file, the phones and the rooftop, the film argues that a person is what survives in other people's records — so the records must be kept by someone who loves the person; that a cover sustained long enough becomes the self, in whichever direction it points; and that the difference between the state and the street is real but thinner than either admits, and paid for, on both sides, in sons.

Why tell this story?

Because Scorsese and Monahan understood that the Hong Kong original's elegant mechanism was, in American materials, a tragedy of class and catechism: two boys from one neighborhood, taken by two fathers, filed into opposite uniforms, and destroyed by the filing. They told it as a mirror story because Boston's real scandal (a mob boss and his FBI handler, raised on the same streets) had already written the doctrine: the war and the crime were one family business. The result is the decade's definitive American crime film, the archive files: a Best Picture about bookkeeping, in which every soul is an entry and the last entry is made by a sergeant in shoe covers.

1. Keep your identity in more than one file

Costigan's fate is the film's first counsel, for every professional whose real work is sealed, classified or simply undocumented — the undercover of any bureaucracy, the whistleblower, the indispensable deputy: your official self rests on a file and its keepers, and keepers fall off roofs. The counsel is a records doctrine with the envelope as its entire procedure: maintain, outside the institution, one complete and honest account of what you are and what you did, addressed to a person who will act on it, updated as the risks change. Sullivan deleted a database. He could not delete the mail. Be findable by your own evidence.

2. Set the cover's expiry date in advance

Costigan's disintegration is the film's second counsel, for every person in a long performance — the assignment, the posting, the caretaking, the role the family business requires: a cover without an extraction date is not an assignment; it is a slow change of ownership. The counsel is a performance doctrine with the psychiatrist's office as its warning: schedule the review at which the performer is asked, by someone with the power to end it, whether there is enough of the original left to bring home — and honor the answer the way Queenan could not, because his operation had no other asset. The performance will not schedule its own end. Its diet is you.

3. Audit the manufactured people around you gently — and early

Sullivan's career is the film's third counsel, for every institution that promotes its golden boys: a person manufactured by a single sponsor, whose biography has no witnesses outside the sponsor's account, is a risk that polish conceals rather than answers. The counsel is a vetting doctrine with Dignam's instinct as its model and its warning: the sergeant read the mole correctly at sight and was filed as a malcontent for it — build the channel where an instinct like his is investigated rather than suspended, before the instinct has to buy shoe covers. Your Barrigan is already in the elevator. Your Dignam is already on leave. Connect them sooner.

4. Count the mirrors before you condemn the reflection

The film's symmetry is its fourth counsel, for every commander of a righteous operation: list your methods beside your enemy's — grooming, assets, staged biographies, managed funerals — and strike from the list every item you would call criminal in his hands. What remains is your actual doctrine; the rest is letterhead. The counsel is an integrity doctrine with the two graduations as its examination: if your operation requires a boy to be expelled, jailed and terrified for years, price it as the enemy prices his — in sons — and let the pricing, not the mission statement, brief the room. The mirror does not flatter either side. That is its use.

5. Tell one civilian the truth

Madolyn's envelope is the film's fifth counsel, for every keeper of a dangerous double life: the state, the firm and the family all maintain files for their own purposes; the only archive kept for yours will be the person you chose, in person, outside the system. The counsel is a witness doctrine with the psychiatrist's couch as its unlikely venue: Costigan told Madolyn the truth in every way except the words, and then — his one flawless administrative act — he mailed her the words. Choose your witness before the rooftop. Brief her properly. The mail is slow, and it is the fastest thing that survives.

6. Beware the catechism you were raised on

Costello's first line is the film's sixth counsel, its benediction in reverse, for every striver formed by a hard neighborhood's doctrine — take it, no one gives it to you: the catechism may be true, and it may still be the instrument of your manufacture. The counsel is a formation doctrine with the golden dome as its examination: before you take the next thing the doctrine points at, ask whose son the taking makes you — which father's curriculum you are graduating from, and what his last three graduates are doing now. The view from the apartment is real. So is the balcony, and the rat crossing it, and the sergeant who has read your file. Take your life. Decline the filing system that comes with it.

The moral of the story is…

Keep your identity in an envelope somebody loves you enough to open; set your cover's expiry before it sets yours; audit your golden boys and your own methods in the same mirror — and ask, of every catechism, whose son it is making you.

The Departed teaches that a person is what survives in other people's records, so the records must be kept with love; that a performance sustained becomes the person, in whichever direction it points; and that the state and the street are one city's mirror, paid for in the same sons.

The film's final question is therefore not whether you are the cop or the criminal — the film files that under "costume." It is who can open your file when the captain falls; on what date your cover expires; and whether the envelope, when it is finally read, will say what you were — or only what you performed.

Editorial note: This is an interpretive reading of the film's themes, not a claim about every private intention of its creators or a prediction of future franchise events.
