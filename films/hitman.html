01 / FILM IDENTITY

Overview

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Hitman (殺手之王, "King of Killers"; released in the United States in an edited version as Contract Killer) is a 1998 Hong Kong action film directed by Stephen Tung, produced by Gordon Chan, and starring Jet Li, Eric Tsang, Simon Yam and Gigi Leung. It opens with a legend at work: the King of Killers, an assassin of mythic reputation, murders the wealthy ex-yakuza boss Tsukamoto — forcing him first to swallow old Chinese wartime promissory notes, the calling card of a killer who executes war criminals and human trash without payment. Tsukamoto, expecting exactly this, had long ago established a revenge fund: US$100 million, payable to whoever kills his killer. His power-hungry grandson Eiji (Keiji Sato) inherits the family and joins the hunt personally. Into the scramble walks Fu (Jet Li): a former soldier from the mainland, immensely skilled with his hands and entirely innocent of the underworld's ways, who is hired as muscle by Ngok Lo (Eric Tsang), a seedy small-time agent for hitmen, in exchange for a share of the bounty. Inspector Chan Kwan (Simon Yam) of the Hong Kong Security Bureau warns them off the case and watches. The investigation turns on the dead man's last meal — the promissory notes point to a wartime grievance, and to an old man named Uncle Leung — and it ends in Eiji's penthouse, where the film's two secrets detonate together: Eiji has rewritten the fund so that he collects no matter who does the killing, and the mild Inspector Chan is the King of Killers himself. Chan retires on his share, and the crown passes, by appointment, to the apprentice: Fu, the new King.

RELEASE

April 3, 1998

Li's farewell Hong Kong vehicle of the era, shot the same season as his Hollywood debut in Lethal Weapon 4; nominated for Best Action Choreography at the 18th Hong Kong Film Awards.

THE TWO TITLES

Hitman / Contract Killer

The Chinese title names the office, not the man — King of Killers. The 2002 American DVD edit, Contract Killer, arrived with a hip-hop soundtrack and the legend slightly dented.

THE PREMISE

A Bounty on a Ghost

The revenge fund is the film's engine: a dead man's money hunting his killer, in a city where the killer is a rumor and the hunters are mostly amateurs, agents and heirs.

Place in the culture

The film lives in the culture as the hinge of Li's 1998: in Hong Kong he is the hero of this scruffy, funny, oddly melancholy crime picture; in Hollywood, the same year, he is the silent villain of Lethal Weapon 4. The archive keeps the two side by side because together they file the whole transition — the Hong Kong industry handing its champion to America inside a story that is itself about succession: an old King retiring, an apprentice crowned, and a fortune divided among the survivors. Its premise — a posthumous bounty, an assassin who kills only the deserving, a crown that is a job rather than a person — has proved more durable than its reputation.

The production's signature is its director: Stephen Tung was one of Hong Kong's most decorated action choreographers, and the film's fights — built without wires, per contemporary reviews — carry a craftsman's plainness: no flight, no fantasy, just a soldier's hands in rented rooms. The archive records the film's double life in this regard: dismissed in its day as routine, it is now reissued and reassessed (Eureka's "Heroes and Villains" set) as a period piece of an industry mid-handover — Hong Kong a year after 1997, its biggest star a year from Hollywood, its killer-for-hire genre itself about to be succeeded.

03 / NARRATIVE SUMMARY

Story Synopsis

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL FULL STORY SUMMARY

The King of Killers strikes in Hong Kong: Tsukamoto, a wealthy former yakuza boss and wartime criminal, is executed — made, before the end, to swallow old Chinese wartime promissory notes, the signature of a killer settling history's accounts. Tsukamoto's revenge fund activates at once: US$100 million for the King's head, administered by the lawyer Martin, and claimed as a birthright by the dead man's grandson Eiji, who takes over the family and hunts the King himself. At the fund's briefing, a broke ex-soldier named Fu is thrown out by security — and, defending himself, displays fighting skills that catch the eye of Ngok Lo, a fast-talking agent for professional killers. Lo hires Fu as his muscle and his entry ticket: they will find the King together and split the hundred million. Inspector Chan Kwan warns them off, then watches them anyway. Lo tests his new asset on a small contract, outfits him, houses him — and introduces him, disastrously and then tenderly, to Kiki, Lo's daughter, a successful lawyer who is permanently ashamed of her father.

The case turns on the dead man's stomach. Martin discovers the promissory notes: the King kills with a wartime ledger in mind, and the notes point to a grievance — and to a suspect, an old man named Uncle Leung, whose family Tsukamoto destroyed in the war. Fu and Lo race Eiji to Leung's apartment block and fight through yakuza hitmen and a grenade-throwing assassin disguised as a priest, whom Fu kills with the man's own grenade — but Leung dies of a heart attack in the escape, and Eiji puts a bullet in the corpse's head for good measure. The trail seems dead. Then Fu and Lo walk into Eiji's penthouse to meet Martin, and Fu appears to betray everything: he shoots Lo and demands the bounty for himself. It is a performance — but Martin's answer is real, and it is the film's coldest fact: Eiji has already rewritten the fund's terms, so that whoever kills the King, Eiji collects. The hundred million was never on the table; it was the bait in the heir's own trap.

The penthouse erupts. And then the second secret arrives, in the shape of the film's mildest man: Inspector Chan, revealing himself as the King of Killers — the vigilante who executed Tsukamoto for his war crimes — joins Fu and Lo against Eiji's army. Eiji and all his men are killed. Martin, the fund's administrator, is left alive to do the film's last piece of paperwork: rewrite the terms back. The survivors divide the money as partners. And the King, his account with history settled and his fortune made, retires — appointing his successor on the spot. The new King of Killers is Fu: the penniless soldier who could not be taught to be cynical, crowned by the one man qualified to judge.

The full narrative summary is an editorial paraphrase of the documented accounts (Wikipedia's plot account), not dialogue or a scene-by-scene transcript.

Story & Chronology

The film unfolds as an execution, a manhunt and a coronation. The sequence below follows the film's narrative order.

Act I — The fund and the apprentice

Tsukamoto executed; the $100 million revenge fund activates; Fu hired by Lo; Inspector Chan's warning; the small contract as an entrance exam.

Act II — The notes and the old man

The promissory notes decode the King's motive; the race to Uncle Leung; the priest with the grenades; Leung's death; Eiji's bullet in the corpse.

07 / WORLD & SETTING

World & Setting

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Hong Kong, 1998 — the handover's second year

The film's world is Hong Kong just after the 1997 handover: a city of lawyers administering a Japanese dead man's fortune, mainland soldiers arriving broke with Golden Triangle pasts, yakuza heirs, foreign assassins, and a Security Bureau inspector with a night job in historical justice. It is a crossroads city in the act of changing sovereignties, and the film's underworld mirrors the moment — old empires (the yakuza boss, the wartime debts) being settled, new arrangements (the fund, the Bureau) being drafted, and a crown passing quietly from one holder to the next. The Setting's function in the record is the archive's portrait of the Hong Kong crime film taking its own city's temperature: everything in it is a succession negotiation. The archive files this Hong Kong as the decade's most completely realized succession city in film: a crown, transferred, at the handover.

The sleazy agent's flat, where Fu is housed, outfitted and fed, and where Kiki's lawful world collides with her father's at the dinner table. 10 / THEMATIC ANALYSIS

Themes & Analysis

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

The fine print is the plot

The film's governing theme is the discovery at its center: a hundred million dollars moves entirely according to its drafting. The theme is worked out through two rewritings of the fund until the audience holds its doctrine of the decisive document: in any contest over money, the fighters are contesting a text — its clauses decide who may win before the first blow is struck — and the most powerful person in the structure is the one who can amend it. Its proposition reaches every signatory in the audience. The reach is the story's question to every person who has signed, inherited or fought under a set of terms: who drafted yours, who can amend them, and have you read the clause that decides what happens when the founder dies?

The dead employ the living

The film's second theme is Tsukamoto's posthumous payroll: a corpse is the plot's largest employer. The theme is worked out through the fund's season of havoc until the audience holds its doctrine of the dead hand: wealth is the one agency that survives its owner intact, and a fortune arranged against the future will keep hiring, corrupting and killing on schedule long after its arranger is beyond appeal — which places the moral weight of every such arrangement on the drafting, done while the drafter can still be argued with. Its proposition reaches every maker of a will in the audience. The reach is the story's question to every person arranging money for after their death: what conduct, exactly, will your fortune be purchasing in your absence — and would you sign the purchase orders it is about to issue in your name?

Innocence passes the examination no one announced

The film's third theme is the succession: the crown goes to the hunter who never became cynical. The theme is worked out in Chan's long observation of Fu until the audience holds its doctrine of the unannounced audit: the tests that decide successions are rarely declared — the incumbent watches how the candidates behave under the temptation itself, and the qualities being examined (loyalty to a partner, indifference to the prize's corruptions) are precisely the ones that cannot be performed once known to be graded. Its proposition reaches every candidate for anything in the audience. The reach is the story's question to every person being watched by a quiet superior: which of your unobserved afternoons is, in fact, your examination — and would your conduct in it survive being read back to you at the coronation?

Justice without a plaintiff

The film's fourth theme is the King's ledger: executions for the war's victims, most of whom never learn their accounts were settled. The theme is worked out in the promissory notes until the audience holds its doctrine of the unenlightened settlement: a debt paid in secret to a creditor who dies unknowing poses the vigilante's hardest question — whether justice is a state of the ledger or a state of the victim — and the film, by killing Leung mid-rescue and finishing him with the heir's bullet, declines to let its King enjoy a clean answer. Its proposition reaches every settler of old scores in the audience. The reach is the story's question to every person balancing a historical account: if the wronged never learn of the payment, who was the payment for — and does your ledger require a witness, or only an entry?

11 / FULL SPOILER CONCLUSION

Ending Explained

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋
REVEAL THE ENDING

In Eiji's penthouse, Fu's apparent betrayal — shooting Lo — is exposed as a performance, but Martin reveals the real treachery: Eiji has rewritten the revenge fund so that he collects the $100 million whoever kills the King. Inspector Chan then arrives and reveals himself as the King of Killers; fighting together, Chan, Fu and Lo kill Eiji and all his men. Martin rewrites the fund's terms once more; the three survivors divide the money and discuss their futures. Chan retires, and appoints Fu as the new King of Killers.

What the ending establishes

  • Chan's reveal files the manhunt's authorship: the investigation was chaired by its quarry, and the warnings were the only honest policing in the film.
  • Eiji's amendment files the heir's true crime: not vengeance but architecture — a machine in which every outcome paid the house.
  • Martin's final redraft files the clerk's supremacy: the fortune's destination is settled, at the last, by the man with the pen.
  • The division files the partnership's graduation: the agent, the inspector and the soldier split a dead man's payroll as equals.
  • Fu's coronation files succession as judgment: the crown passes not to the strongest claimant but to the candidate the incumbent watched longest.

What remains uncertain

The documented accounts agree on the ending's shape; what kind of King Fu will be — whether the office's price list survives a holder who could never be taught cynicism — the film leaves to its audience, and the archive files the uncertainty as the story's own last word on its theme: an institution is only ever one appointment away from becoming its opposite, and the old King's retirement plan is, in the end, a bet on a young man's character.

12 / PURPOSES OF THE STORY

Moral of The Story

EXPAND SECTION DETAILS ＋

Hitman uses a dead man's bounty, a vigilante inspector and a penniless apprentice to examine the decisive document, posthumous money, and succession by audit: a fund whose fine print employs a city; an heir who redrafts his grandfather's vengeance into a house-always-wins machine; a King who kills only history's debtors and watches his replacement for a whole film before abdicating. Through the briefing room, the agent's kitchen and the penthouse, the film argues that contests over money are contests over a text, decided by whoever may amend it; that a fortune arranged against the future keeps purchasing conduct in its maker's name; and that the examinations that matter are the ones never announced.

Why tell this story?

Because Hong Kong in 1998 was itself a succession story — a handover completed, an industry exporting its champions — and its genre cinema processed the moment the way it knew how: with a crown, a fund, and an old hand picking his successor. The archive files the result as Li's Hong Kong farewell of the era and the mirror panel to Lethal Weapon 4: in America that year he is the silent villain; at home he is the innocent crowned. The Galaxy keeps the two 1998s adjacent on purpose.

1. Read the clause that decides the succession

The fund's terms are the film's first counsel, for every signatory and heir: the decisive text is the one governing what happens when the founder dies. The counsel is a drafting doctrine with Eiji's amendment as its standard: before joining any contest over money — a bounty, a partnership, an estate — locate the succession and amendment clauses, establish who may rewrite them and by what majority, and price your participation by those clauses rather than by the headline figure, because the hundred million was never the prize; the pen was. Audit your own arrangements with the same cold eye: if your Eiji can redraft your intentions unilaterally, he already has, in expectation if not yet in ink.

2. Draft your dead hand while you can be argued with

Tsukamoto's fund is the film's second counsel, for every maker of a will: posthumous money keeps purchasing conduct in your name. The counsel is a testamentary doctrine with the manhunt as its standard: arrange your estate as if its administrator were literal-minded, its beneficiaries ingenious, and its circumstances unforeseeable — because they are — and prefer arrangements that pay for construction over arrangements that pay for vengeance, since a fund for revenge hires exactly the season of chaos Tsukamoto's did. The dead cannot issue corrections. Draft like it.

3. Behave as if the examination is unannounced

Chan's observation is the film's third counsel, for every candidate: the audits that decide successions are conducted in secret, on ordinary days. The counsel is a conduct doctrine with the coronation as its standard: the qualities an incumbent is grading — loyalty when the prize is largest, mercy when no one is scoring, honesty when a performance would pay better — cannot be simulated once suspected, so the only strategy is the expensive one of actually having them. Fu did not know he was being examined, which is why he passed. Conduct your unobserved afternoons as if a quiet man in a good suit were taking notes for a crown. One day, the notes are read.

4. Grade the coercion, not the craftsman — and the drafter above the fighter

Martin's survival is the film's fourth counsel, for every analyst of a conflict: find the person who can amend the terms. The counsel is a power doctrine with the penthouse as its standard: armies, heirs and assassins contend inside a structure whose outcomes are pre-decided by its documents, so the diagnostic question in any struggle is not who is strongest but who holds the pen — and reform aimed at the fighters, while the drafting stays where it is, is choreography. When your own struggle feels unwinnable at the level of effort, look for its Martin. The fight you need may be a redraft.

5. Settle accounts so the creditor could know

Leung's death is the film's fifth counsel, for every settler of old scores: a payment the wronged never learn of is an entry, not a justice. The counsel is a restitution doctrine with the promissory notes as its standard: where a historical debt can still be acknowledged to its living creditors — an apology delivered, a name restored, a record corrected — prefer the settlement that enlightens over the one that merely balances, because ledgers are for the settler's peace, while acknowledgment is for the wronged, and only one of those is restitution. The King's notes were swallowed by the debtor and never read by the creditor. Deliver yours to the person they are owed.

6. Retire like Chan: divide the money, name the successor, leave

The division scene is the film's sixth counsel, its benediction, for every incumbent of anything: abdication is a skill, and it is performed in one sitting. The counsel is a succession doctrine with the three survivors as its standard: settle the accounts in full view of the partners, appoint the successor whose unobserved conduct you have graded yourself, transfer the title while your authority to transfer it is unquestioned — and then go, before the office reopens negotiations with your vanity. Chan leaves the film rich, alive and legendary. The archive files his exit as the genre's model retirement: most kings, in most stories, manage none of the three.

The moral of the story is…

Read the clause that decides the succession; draft your dead hand while you can be argued with; behave as if the examination is unannounced — and find the person who can amend the terms.

Hitman teaches that contests over money are contests over a text, decided by whoever may amend it; that a fortune arranged against the future keeps purchasing conduct in its maker's name; and that the examinations that matter are the ones never announced.

The film's final question is therefore not who killed Tsukamoto — the film files that under "ask the inspector." It is who may amend the terms you are currently fighting under; what your own posthumous money would be purchasing, season by season, in your name; and which of your unobserved afternoons is being graded, this week, for a crown you have not been told exists.
